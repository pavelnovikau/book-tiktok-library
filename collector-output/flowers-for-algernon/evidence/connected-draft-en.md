Charlie is thirty-two. He cleans at Donner’s bakery and attends Alice Kinnian’s classes. Reading and writing are difficult, but he keeps coming back: he wants to become smarter and have friends. Alice recommends him to researchers. Now Charlie must write down everything that happens to him. His first reports are full of mistakes.

Before the operation, Charlie takes tests. He is shown inkblots and asked what he sees. He tries to find the right answer and fears getting it wrong. The researchers are learning about his abilities; Charlie worries about whether they will let him into the experiment. He does not want to lose his chance to learn.

At the laboratory Charlie meets Algernon. The white mouse finishes his maze faster than Charlie can solve the task set for him. Algernon has already had an operation that increased his abilities. A similar procedure is now to be tried on a human for the first time. Losing to a mouse hurts Charlie, but Algernon’s success gives him hope.

Professor Nemur doubts whether Charlie is suitable. Dr. Strauss argues for him: his desire to learn is unusually strong. Charlie is warned that success is not guaranteed. Nemur talks about science and fame; Charlie cares more about being liked. He agrees, imagining how his life will change.

The operation is over, but Charlie still cannot understand things he could not understand before. He expected to become smart at once. Alice explains that it will take time. He returns to reports, lessons and tests. Soon changes begin to appear both in how he solves problems and in how he writes about himself.

At last Charlie beats Algernon in a race. But he notices something else: the mouse has to solve a problem to get food. Charlie dislikes that arrangement. Algernon is becoming more than a rival; Charlie wants to be his friend. Beside this small fellow subject, he increasingly has to think about his own position too.

At the bakery Charlie used to laugh along with Joe Carp and Frank Reilly. Now he begins to understand whom they were laughing at. He remembers his companions getting him drunk and leaving him alone. Once he took such pranks for friendship. Understanding other people now brings shame and anger: the old memories can no longer mean what they did.

Charlie discovers that Gimpy is stealing from Donner. Gimpy could once count on Charlie not noticing. Yet Gimpy has helped him at the bakery, and Donner gave him a job. Charlie seeks advice and eventually confronts Gimpy himself: if the stealing stops, he will not tell the owner. Gimpy agrees reluctantly.

Charlie masters more demanding work, but his rapid changes frighten his coworkers. They press for his removal. Donner dismisses him. The bakery was where people had known him for years, almost a family. Now that Charlie can do more, he has to leave it.

Miss Kinnian becomes Alice to Charlie. They meet outside class, and he falls in love with her. But trying to get closer brings panic. Alongside new knowledge come childhood memories that once left only a vague fear. He wants to be with Alice, yet acts as though someone is watching him.

He remembers his mother, Rose, trying to make him like other children and harshly punishing his interest in girls. His father, Matt, tried to protect him. After the birth of his younger sister Norma, the family’s conflicts grew worse. The adult Charlie begins to recognize voices and threats from that house within his own fear.

At first, conversations with students seem to open an enormous new world. Then their knowledge no longer satisfies him. He surpasses people he once regarded as unquestionable authorities. His condescension hurts Alice. Charlie wanted to understand the people around him, but increasingly becomes impatient and withdraws from them.

Nemur and Strauss take Charlie and Algernon to a scientific convention. The researchers have disagreed about whether it is too soon to announce success. Now Charlie listens to papers about himself. Nemur speaks as though Charlie had no human life before the operation. Charlie is furious—and can now understand why the scientists’ confidence in a lasting result is inadequately supported.

Seeing the limits of Nemur’s and Strauss’s knowledge, Charlie attacks their competence. Burt defends them as ordinary people attempting extraordinary work. He also challenges Charlie, whose education has grown faster than his ability to deal with others. Charlie refuses to be an exhibit, but his own treatment of people is also provoking quarrels.

Charlie releases Algernon from his cage, creates an uproar and takes him back to New York. He no longer wants to remain obedient proof of someone else’s success. A man and a mouse who have undergone similar operations settle into a new apartment. Charlie builds Algernon a maze he can explore without having to earn food.

A newspaper story about the experiment helps Charlie find his father. He visits Matt’s barbershop, but Matt does not recognize his son. Charlie cannot bring himself to reveal his identity. He fears his father would reject the changed man; that is Charlie’s fear, not an answer Matt has given. The visit ends without recognition.

His neighbor, the painter Fay Lillman, brings dancing, drinking and intimacy into his new life. With her, Charlie manages what he has not yet managed with Alice. But being with Fay does not end his loneliness or his fear of his former self. Algernon is beside him, now outside the laboratory walls.

In a diner, a young worker with an intellectual disability drops dishes. The customers laugh, and at first Charlie laughs along. Then he realizes he once stood where the young man stands. He stops the mockery. After this encounter Charlie decides to return to researching the experiment: he wants to do something for other people too.

Algernon becomes restless and unpredictable. Tasks he once mastered grow harder for him. Charlie sees the changes in the creature who underwent the experiment before him. He can no longer be sure the operation produces a lasting result. Now he must understand what is happening to Algernon and what it may mean for himself.

Charlie obtains foundation support for his own research and returns to the laboratory with Algernon. This time he wants to examine the data himself. People who once tested him must help him work. The questions remain open: why the mouse is changing, what the calculations missed, and how much time there is to find an answer.

Charlie learns that a place at the Warren Home has been arranged if he loses his new abilities. He asks to visit while he can still understand what he sees. The staff try to care for the residents, but imagining himself among them frightens him. He also asks that Algernon not be disposed of with other dead laboratory animals: he will take care of the mouse himself.

At a party Charlie quarrels with Nemur again. Even a failed experiment may give the scientists valuable knowledge; Charlie has to live inside that experiment. He rejects Nemur’s claim to have created him as a person. Nemur replies that Charlie has become arrogant and self-centered. After leaving, Charlie cannot simply dismiss the accusation.

Charlie immerses himself in work, often sleeping at the laboratory. Fay finds another man, while Charlie scarcely looks away from his research. Algernon may begin a maze confidently, then become lost. To explain these changes, Charlie works with the persistence he once brought to getting into the experiment itself.

He finds the flaw in the assumption that the result will last. In his paper on the Algernon-Gordon Effect, Charlie describes the relationship he has established within the experiment: the greater the artificial increase in intelligence, the faster the subsequent deterioration. The discovery applies to him too. He sends his findings to Nemur and Strauss. He has an answer, but no way to preserve his new abilities.

Algernon dies. Charlie examines his body, then buries him in the backyard and puts flowers on the grave. The laboratory keeps the observations; the yard holds the companion he raced, befriended and took away from Chicago. Charlie himself is beginning to have lapses of memory. Before it is too late, he wants to see his mother.

Rose no longer always understands who is before her. Charlie shows her his paper: now he can learn, now she can be proud of him. Norma is delighted to see her brother and apologizes for her childhood cruelty. But Rose suddenly seizes a knife and orders Charlie away from his sister. He leaves in tears. He has reached Norma; the reunion has not restored his old home.

Charlie struggles with the laboratory tests again and refuses to return. He opens Paradise Lost, a book he recently admired, but can no longer understand it. Furious, he tears it apart. Each attempt reminds him of something that was within his reach so recently. He keeps recording what happens while he can.

Alice comes to stay with him. This time the old panic recedes, and they can finally be intimate. Charlie wants the remaining time with her, but struggles to think of what she will have to witness. He asks her in advance to leave when he tells her to, and to promise she will not return afterward. For now, they are together.

Soon Charlie cannot understand his own paper on the Algernon-Gordon Effect. Alice’s attempts to help and encourage him to study make him angry. He remembers his mother’s pressure and orders Alice to leave. She keeps her promise. Charlie continues trying to learn, but the losses persist. Mistakes return to his reports. He does not want to live on money from Alice and Strauss and decides to seek work.

Donner takes him back. A new employee, Meyer Klaus, bullies and threatens Charlie. This time Joe, Frank and Gimpy defend him. Charlie is glad to have friends again. These are the same people whose mockery he once mistook for friendship and later learned to understand.

Forgetting that he no longer attends Miss Kinnian’s class, Charlie turns up for a lesson. Alice sees him and runs out in tears. He senses that the people he knows feel sorry for him and decides to go to Warren. His reports do not tell what life there will be like. Charlie says goodbye to the people he has known.

In the last entries Charlie struggles to express what he remembers. He is glad he was smart for a time and learned about his family. Finally he asks those who remain to put flowers on Algernon’s grave in the backyard. His reports end with that request.
