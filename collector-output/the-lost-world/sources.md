## Evidence boundary / Граница доказательности

The complete, unabridged public-domain text was retrieved from Project Gutenberg and researched across all sixteen chapters, using a chapter/paragraph survey and targeted close reading of the causal pivots, evidence disputes, colonial language, violence, escape, and ending. This is a selective analytical digest, not a line-by-line critical edition or a claim that every sentence received equal close reading. The fictional narrative establishes what happens **inside the novel**, never historical proof of surviving prehistoric animals, the plateau, its inhabitants, or an expedition. No private EPUB or private research files were used. No book text is reproduced at length.

## Claim map established before screen writing

- Desire: a young reporter wants a life worth admiring; a reader wants adventure and the possibility that the familiar world is incomplete.
- Tension: personal vanity, scientific vanity, unreliable appearances, and metropolitan disbelief obstruct both discovery and judgment.
- Narrative proposition: extraordinary experience changes Malone's capacities and loyalties, but does not mechanically deliver the reward he first imagines. This is a reading of a novel, not a nonfiction thesis.
- Causal spine: Gladys's heroic ideal → dangerous assignment → Challenger's contested evidence → independent expedition → isolation and entrapment → direct observation → rash solitude and collective rescue → intervention in an existing conflict → local knowledge enables departure → public evidence and private disappointment → a freely chosen next journey.
- Carriers: Malone; Challenger; Summerlee; Roxton; Maple White's traces; Zambo's sustained logistical work; Gomez's revenge; the young chief Maretas's assistance.
- Mechanisms: cumulative but vulnerable evidence; complementary skills; competing goals of discovery and survival; fear versus shame; observation becoming intervention; local knowledge versus grand invention.
- Qualifications: Malone is a participant with partial knowledge. Scientific correctness does not excuse bullying. Rescue does not automatically justify conquest. The book's racial/evolutionary ladder and gender assumptions are dated and rejected as modern factual guidance.
- Durable model: observe → test → cooperate → examine consequences. Competence can grow while certainty and entitlement shrink.

Arthur Conan Doyle, *The Lost World*, Project Gutenberg #139, produced by Judith Boss; HTML by Al Haines.

- [Full UTF-8 text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/139/pg139.txt)
- Role: `book`. Supports the complete sixteen-chapter plot and attributed views.
- Scope: I romance and motive; II assignment and deceptive letter; III assault and refusal to prosecute; IV Maple White, sketch, photographs, wing fragment, isolation theory; V public challenge and committee; VI Roxton's test and equipment; VII Amazon logistics and Challenger's arrival; VIII route and ambiguous sighting; IX failed route, direct pterosaur sighting, tree bridge, revenge, Zambo; X camp, iguanodons, rookery; XI predator, map and competing aims; XII solitary journey, lake, trap, empty camp; XIII capture and rescue; XIV alliance, battle and rhetoric; XV predator attack, limits of guns, balloon and cave map; XVI Queen's Hall, uncertain pterosaur fate, Gladys, diamonds and return plans.

- Rights: Gutenberg marks #139 public domain in the USA; the author died in 1930. This package is an original digest rather than a redistribution of the edition. Follow applicable local rules for the source edition itself.

### 4. Portsmouth Conan Doyle Collection — literary context

[Lost World exhibition](https://www.conandoylecollection.com/conan-doyle/exhibitions/lost-world), Portsmouth Conan Doyle Collection.

- Role: `reference`. Supports 1912 publication, Challenger's introduction, the South American setting, the connection to Wells's *The Country of the Blind*, and the 1925 screen adaptation.
- Disagreement: this page dates the first Strand appearance to June 1912; other bibliographies give an April–November run. No precise serialization month is asserted in the digest. The stable, independently checked year is 1912.
- Limit: museum context does not authenticate the imaginary expedition. Museum images were not reused.

### 5. Natural History Museum, London — palaeontological correction

[Pterosaurs: The truth about these ‘flying dinosaurs’](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/the-truth-about-pterosaurs.html), Natural History Museum.

- Role: `reference`. Pterosaurs are related to, but distinct from, dinosaurs; birds evolved from dinosaurs, not pterosaurs; pterosaurs disappeared at the end of the Cretaceous, about 66 million years ago.
- Limit: this source corrects classification and chronology. It supplies no evidence of a surviving South American population. No numerical certainty is added to the novel's fleeting identifications.

### 6. Smithsonian Human Origins — evolutionary correction

[Introduction to Human Evolution](https://humanorigins.si.edu/education/introduction-human-evolution), Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History.

- Role: `reference`. Humans and living African great apes share ancestors; human evolution is a branching history, not a ladder ranking living peoples.
- Limit: not a real-world identification of Doyle's fictional ape-men. Their exact species remains unspecified.

### 7. AABA — race and racism

[AABA Statement on Race & Racism](https://bioanth.org/about/aaba-statement-on-race-racism/), American Association of Biological Anthropologists, statement accepted 27 March 2019.

- Role: `primary`. Rejects discrete biological racial types and biological hierarchies of living peoples; acknowledges anthropology's role in sustaining colonial racism.
- Limit: used for bounded modern correction, not as a claim about what every person believed in 1912 or about Doyle's private intention.

## Editorial boundaries

No precise real-world location or identification with Roraima is asserted. No dinosaur is confidently renamed from film adaptations; the night predator remains uncertain in the text. No inflation conversion is supplied for the fictional diamond valuation. Colonial and racial assumptions are discussed explicitly in both languages. Violence is described without gore, but the battle's extermination of males and enslavement of females and young are not concealed. The digest does not advise a fourteen-year-old to imitate armed exploration, solitary night travel, animal collection, or coercion.
