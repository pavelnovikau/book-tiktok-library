## Claim / plot / theme map — fixed before screen writing

**Reader's desire:** understand whether a frightening animal has an unchangeable nature, and learn to read an adventure without confusing survival skill with moral worth.

**Central question:** which pressures make this particular wolf-dog hostile, and what would have to change for another response to become possible?

**Shortest analytical thesis:** London makes character both inherited potential and accumulated adjustment to surroundings. Human treatment can intensify aggression; the novel tests whether changed relationships can alter it. This is an interpretation of fiction, not a validated treatment theory.

**Causal chain:** scarcity narrows choices → exploratory experience builds distinctions → human power adds learned rules → exclusion removes play and reinforces suspicion → service purchases security without affection → spectacle turns aggression into saleable identity → new conditions test the apparent permanence of that identity. The Essential sequence stops at the question; Deep examines the later test.

**Consequences and counter-readings:** security can cost autonomy; obedience need not be affection; an excluded creature can reproduce oppression; benefiting from a violent system is different from merely observing it; explaining a cause does not justify abuse. The novel's opposition of human settlements and wilderness is unstable. Racial hierarchy, physiognomy, sex-based instinct and claims about inherited memory belong to the period's narration and are challenged, never turned into reader advice. No human morality is inferred from canine biology.

**Durable model:** ask what a behaviour was adapted to, what present conditions reward it, and what costs are invisible from a winner's perspective. For real people this is a question to investigate, not a diagnosis or an obligation to endure harm.
