# The Children of Captain Grant — core

An intentionally unfinished route. The ending is not revealed here.

## The shark astern

The new yacht Duncan was returning from a trial voyage off Scotland when a shark appeared astern. Her young captain, John Mangles, suggested catching it. Lord Glenarvan and his wife Helena watched from the deck as the sailors lowered a rope with a hook and bait. The fish swallowed the piece of bacon and jerked the line, but soon it was struggling beside the yacht: a noose slipped around its tail prevented further resistance. Curiosity continued on deck. While the shark was being cut open, the boatswain noticed a large hard object inside it. The sailors first took it for a stone, then recognized a bottle. Glenarvan ordered them to remove it carefully and bring it to the cabin. It might still contain nothing but a joke, some seaman's idle message thrown overboard; already, however, the lord was thinking of people who had entrusted their last appeal for help to the sea.

## Glass on the table

It was a thick champagne bottle, with a remnant of wire around its neck and a crust of marine growth. While Glenarvan cleaned it, Major McNabbs confidently identified the vessel's origin; Helena was far more interested in its contents. The cork was damaged. Clotted papers could be seen inside, but there was no way of drawing them through the narrow neck. The bottle had to be broken. Fragments of glass and wet sheets lay on the table. The sea had eaten away entire lines, leaving isolated words and scraps. There were three sheets: English, German and French. Each had suffered differently. John suggested comparing the surviving places: water could hardly have destroyed precisely the same words in all three copies. The people around the table began passing scraps from hand to hand. What had seemed a meaningless jumble of letters gradually acquired a ship's name and human faces.

## One latitude

Pieces of the port's name made Glasgow; other fragments yielded the three-masted Britannia, Captain Grant and two sailors. The French sheet preserved a latitude: 37 degrees 11 minutes south. The longitude could not be read. This described a line around the globe, rather than a point on a map, but the excited Glenarvan was already completing the words. “Gonie” seemed the end of Patagonia; “contin” suggested a continent; “indi” suggested Indians. On that reading, three sailors had reached South America after a wreck and become prisoners. Even the June date reconstructed from scraps appeared certain. A shipping paper supplied news of Britannia's departure from Callao. Every discovery strengthened the first conjecture, although the missing portions of the message remained unknown. Glenarvan went to London to ask the Admiralty for a rescue vessel. Newspapers carried an advertisement inviting anyone with information about Britannia to contact him.

## To see his handwriting

Two children arrived at Malcolm Castle. Mary Grant was about sixteen, her brother Robert twelve. They had come by train and walked the rest of the way. Robert immediately demanded to see the lord; his sister, embarrassed by his determination, explained who they were. Helena sat them beside her and described the papers. The hope was still too fragile for her to promise their father's return, but the children listened as though each word already showed him rescued. Mary asked to see the message. Helena explained that her husband had taken it to London and assured her that she had repeated everything it contained. The girl wanted something else: to see her father's handwriting. For everyone else, the scattered letters were a puzzle. To her, they might become the first sign of his living hand after a long silence.

## Sister and brother

While they waited for Glenarvan, Helena learned the family's history. Their mother had died soon after Robert's birth; an elderly relative cared for the children during their father's voyages. Grant sailed in pursuit of a plan he had cherished for years: he was looking for a place in the Pacific for a Scottish colony. He wanted poor fellow countrymen to have land and an independent life overseas. Then his letters stopped, the relative died, and Mary was left alone with her little brother. She worked, denied herself things and tried to keep his education going. In telling the story, she attached no special importance to her sacrifices. Robert, hearing it all together for the first time, flung his arms around her: now he understood how much care she had hidden from him. Helena kept them at the castle. That night the children slept, exhausted by travel and emotion, while their hostess lay almost sleepless waiting for her husband.

## Helena's proposal

Glenarvan returned angry and dispirited. The Admiralty had refused: the papers were too obscure, too much time had passed since the wreck, a search inland would cost more lives. He also believed that hostility to Grant's independent Scottish ambitions lay behind the refusal. Not yet knowing that the children were at the castle, he announced in their hearing that hope was lost. Mary fell to her knees before him; Robert prepared to go and argue with the men who had refused to save his father. His sister proposed appealing to the Queen. Glenarvan knew how difficult it would be for two poor children to reach her, but before he could object Helena approached with a proposal of her own. Duncan had been built for their honeymoon: why not use her to find the missing men instead? He embraced his wife. The search now lay in their own hands.

## A prayer before sailing

Coal and provisions for a long voyage went aboard Duncan. Mangles enlarged the stores, arranged cabins and inspected everything they might need far from familiar ports. Mary and Robert would sail too. The boy refused to remain merely a passenger: he asked for real sailor's work. The captain undertook to teach him. Major McNabbs, Glenarvan's cousin, joined the expedition as calmly as he usually took his place at dinner. On the eve of departure they all gathered in St Mungo's Cathedral. When the prayer for a safe voyage was over, Mary's own prayer rose in the silence, thanking God for those setting out to find her father. The travelers returned to the yacht that night. By morning the Scottish shore was falling behind them.

## The collapsing telescope

Mary soon had to grow accustomed to the sight of her brother high in the rigging. Mangles reassured her: Robert would become a sailor they could proudly present to Captain Grant. She herself had grown up aboard her father's ships, knew nautical terms and maintained that she could manage a sail if necessary. John listened with particular pleasure. During one of the first days, while the others toured the yacht, the major stayed on deck with a cigar. A stranger suddenly appeared before him: tall and thin, wearing large round spectacles, his pockets bulging with notebooks. He looked about for some time, then extended a long telescope. His observations complete, he leaned on it as though it were a stick. The sections collapsed and he sprawled on deck. Even that failed to disturb the major's composure.

## The passenger in cabin six

The stranger called for the steward, announced himself as the passenger in cabin six and demanded breakfast: after his journey from Paris he had slept for thirty-six hours. He greeted Mangles as Captain Burton, called the yacht Scotia, confused married and unmarried people, and spoke with such courteous assurance that his listeners could hardly get a word in. At last he introduced himself as Jacques Paganel, secretary of the Paris Geographical Society, listed his scientific connections and declared that he was going to India. Glenarvan knew his name and his work. The scholar, after twenty years of studying the world indoors, had finally decided to see it. In the dark harbor he had boarded the wrong vessel and gone to bed while Duncan's entire crew was attending the cathedral service.

## Not Scotia

Explaining that this was neither Burton nor Scotia took some time. The final proof was the name Duncan, which Paganel read beside the wheel. He rushed below, checked his luggage and returned in complete dismay. How could he appear before the Geographical Society after reaching Chile instead of India? He even suggested that the whole party change its route: for a fine yacht, turning toward Calcutta seemed a simple matter. Helena then explained why they were sailing. Once he heard about the children, the bottle and the search, Paganel stopped pressing his own wishes. He examined the document carefully, agreed with the reading that pointed to Patagonia and promised not to delay anyone. They would land him at the first convenient stop. Until then, he would gladly enjoy their hospitality.

## Landing postponed

The first convenient stop, however, kept receding. Off Madeira, Paganel remembered that the island was thoroughly described and offered little interest to a geographer. The Canaries would be better: he had always wanted to climb the peak of Tenerife. When it appeared above the sea, it emerged that Humboldt had already done everything worth doing there. The yacht then reached the Cape Verde Islands for coal. Rain fell so heavily that the town almost disappeared behind a curtain of water; he might wait months for passage back to Europe. His friends urged him to stay. Ahead lay the Straits of Magellan, the Cordilleras, little-known rivers and people to be helped. His resistance weakened until he admitted that he wanted to sail with them and was merely afraid of imposing. Robert greeted the decision so enthusiastically that he almost knocked his new teacher over.

## The Southern Cross

Now Paganel had plenty to do. He taught Robert geography, spread maps over the dining table to the despair of Olbinett the steward, and began studying the Spanish he would need in Chile. He found books in the chief officer's chest. The others could hear him endlessly repeating unfamiliar syllables. Duncan passed through the Straits of Magellan between snowy peaks and wooded shores. Fire from volcanic mountains gleamed on the water; at night the Southern Cross shone over the yacht. Paganel would not miss a rock or a bay, while Mangles increasingly found himself beside Mary. The others were beginning to understand his attentions. The young people themselves had not yet named what was growing between them.

## A shore without a trace

They found no trace of Britannia on the Chilean shore. The consul at Concepcion had heard of no such wreck; careful inquiries and examination of the coastline produced nothing either. To Paganel's surprise, people also understood his Spanish badly, but he blamed his pronunciation. The failure of the search was much more troubling. They laid out the fragments again. The geographer proposed changing one part of their reading: perhaps the sailors were not merely about to become prisoners but were already captives when they wrote. Glenarvan objected that a bottle could not be thrown into the sea from the interior. It could be thrown into a river, Paganel answered. The latitude might then describe the place from which they tried to send news, rather than the shore of the wreck.

## Seven ashore

The new possibility restored hope. They decided to cross South America from west to east along the given parallel, looking for the prisoners. Meanwhile Duncan would round the continent and wait on the Atlantic side. The land party consisted of Glenarvan, McNabbs, Paganel, Robert, chief officer Tom Austin and the sailors Wilson and Mulrady. Mangles wanted to accompany them, but the yacht, Helena and Mary were entrusted to him. Mary found it hard to let Robert go, yet there was no holding him back. Paganel described the crossing almost as an outing: some mountains, a descent, then an easy plain. Those staying behind clung to his confident words. When the seven mounted their mules, the yacht passed close to the shore once more. Helena could send her husband a last farewell before their paths separated.

## Through Chile in an armchair

An English guide who had lived in Chile for years led the caravan behind a little mare with bells. The travelers wore ponchos and high boots. Paganel struggled before settling on his mule; Robert quickly grew accustomed to his and constantly tried to get ahead. At first Duncan's smoke was still visible on the horizon and they rode almost silently. Then the ground began to rise, streams became more numerous, and the Cordilleras grew before them. The geographer identified crossroads and rivers from the map so confidently that the guide asked whether he had traveled here before. Paganel explained that he had done so in an armchair. He immediately gave nameless streams sonorous names and entered them on his map. His language practice continued, but practical conversation with local people was still proving difficult.

## A wall across the pass

To stay reasonably close to the parallel, they chose the Antuco pass. The guide knew it but warned them that it was chiefly used for herds, not an easy road. The higher they climbed, the narrower the ledges became. The caravan disappeared around bends or emerged on a neighboring slope across a deep cleft. At last the way ended at a sheer wall. A recent earthquake had altered the pass. The mules could go no farther; a detour would take several days. The major observed that an obstacle to animals need not stop men. All seven decided to continue on foot. They paid the guide and distributed food and weapons among themselves. Now there were no pack animals ahead of them, and no man responsible for leading them to the other side.

## A hut in the snow

At first it seemed that one more climb would reveal the descent. Instead, each slope led to another. The track vanished among stones; snow and ice followed. Wilson tested doubtful ground with his feet; Mulrady helped wherever strength was needed. The men supported each other across cracks, offered shoulders to climb on and clung to projections. They spoke little. At this height every step demanded more effort; breathing grew difficult and heads swam. Glenarvan already regretted leading his companions so far, but turning back was no easier than continuing. Robert refused to admit fatigue although his strength was ebbing. When it seemed that they must spend the night in the snow, McNabbs noticed an almost buried hut. The sailors dug out its entrance and the seven crowded under its roof.

## An unsuccessful supper

The shelter was cramped but had a hearth. Outside, across a dark valley, Antuco blazed: smoke and ejected stones glowed against the dusk. They might have watched for a long time had Wilson not reminded them to find fuel. Dry plants and lichens were gathered; the fire was hard to kindle. Coffee warmed them, but the dried meat did not much please Paganel. He began talking about fresh game, and soon the sound of an entire herd came from outside. Frightened guanacos swept across the plateau. Almost everyone managed to lie down; the standing geographer was knocked over. The major shot an animal. Paganel eagerly undertook the cooking and promised excellent cutlets, but the first mouthful produced the same grimace on every face. He explained that the flesh of a hard-driven guanaco lost its flavor. The cook himself became the subject of their amusement.

## The slope moves

Gradually the hut filled with snores. Glenarvan alone could not sleep. The animals' flight troubled him: what had sent them racing across such slopes at night? A dull subterranean rumbling sounded outside. He went out but saw a clear sky, stars and the distant volcanic fire; there was no storm or visible threat. Toward morning he finally slept. A terrible crash woke him. The walls rocked and opened, the floor gave way beneath his feet. An enormous section of the mountain had broken loose and was carrying the hut and its occupants downhill. They clung to rocks and vegetation. Snow blinded them and cliffs collapsed around them; calling to one another was impossible. The mass sometimes slid smoothly, sometimes lurched beside abysses, until a final impact flung the men down at the mountain's foot.

## One is missing

The major was first to rise. He counted the figures beside him: one was missing. Robert had vanished. The others had escaped with a terrible shaking, but Glenarvan could not rejoice. He demanded that every crevice be searched and was ready to be lowered by a rope into any chasm. Wilson remembered that the boy had still been on his left shortly before the final shock. They used that recollection to define an area and divide the search. The men climbed over debris and emerged from fissures with torn clothes and bleeding hands. All in vain. Below, summer sunlight already shone on green trees and a running river. In that peaceful valley Glenarvan kept looking upward, toward the place where they had just lost the child.

## A speck in the sky

He refused to leave. That night he wandered on the slopes again, calling Robert and listening; Paganel or the major followed to keep him from dying there himself. By morning provisions were running out. McNabbs spoke of the need to move, but his cousin begged another hour, then another. How could he face Mary? How could he restore her father at the price of his son? When delay could no longer continue, Glenarvan noticed a dark speck in the sky. A condor circled an inaccessible ledge. He watched almost motionlessly until the bird disappeared behind a rock and emerged with a heavy burden in its claws. Robert's body hung beneath it, caught by his clothes. The lost boy was now before all their eyes, yet more unreachable than ever.

## A shot from the valley

Glenarvan seized a gun, but his hands shook. The major took aim himself. Before he could fire, another shot rang out from the rocks below. The condor had been hit in the head. Its enormous outspread wings checked its fall; without releasing the boy, it sank beside the river. Glenarvan rushed to it, pulled Robert from beneath a wing and put his ear to the boy's chest. His heart was beating. They bathed his face and loosened his clothes until at last his eyes opened. For a moment he did not understand who was bending over him. The lord, moments earlier ready to shoot at a bird with a child in its claws, now knelt and wept with relief. His companions nearly smothered the rescued boy with embraces before remembering the man whose shot had restored him.

## The book by Camoens

The stranger stood nearby, tall and broad-shouldered, carrying a long gun. This was Thalcave, a guide across the Pampas. Glenarvan needed no shared language to express gratitude, but Paganel was called for further conversation. The scholar pronounced a carefully prepared greeting. Thalcave did not understand. Paganel repeated it louder, then changed his phrase, without success. The guide's Spanish reply seemed an unintelligible dialect to him as well. The major suggested that the fault might lie with the pupil rather than the other speaker. The geographer then produced his textbook: Camoens's Lusiads. Glenarvan immediately recognized the Portuguese poet. For six weeks Paganel had been learning Portuguese, believing it Spanish. Everyone laughed, and then he laughed too. Thalcave patiently waited for the end of this inexplicable merriment.

## A horse at a whistle

The languages' resemblance nevertheless helped: little by little they could talk. Thalcave examined Robert, gently helped him recover, then took Glenarvan and Paganel to the nearest encampment. They bought horses and provisions there. The guide needed no new mount himself: at his whistle Thaouka bounded from a grove, a beautiful swift horse. Horse and rider moved so perfectly together that even the unexcitable major admired them. When the party set off eastward again, snowy summits lay behind and dry plains opened ahead. Paganel now tried to learn Spanish directly from the guide, without a book. Robert sat securely in the saddle, and Glenarvan gradually stopped scrutinizing his every movement with the fear left by the mountains.

## A map in the sand

For a long time Thalcave did not question their purpose. Eventually he wondered why they never turned toward a town but always rode east. Paganel began explaining the bottle. Words failed him; he drew the continent, oceans, latitude and longitude on the sand and used his hands to help. The guide watched, then briefly repeated the essential point: they were seeking a prisoner and following this line. Moreover, he had heard of a brave European held by a local chief. Robert asked how to say that this was his father and seized Thalcave's hands. The guide lifted him onto his horse. But he did not know the prisoner's name, had not seen him and had heard no recent news. His companions' joy was already going beyond what he could actually confirm.

## The dry lake

The plain grew drier. Water was issued in small portions, some reserved for the horses. Mosquitoes tormented them at night. Paganel grew angry, the major teased him, and an encounter with horsemen who hastened away started an argument about harmless herdsmen and robbers. Thalcave blamed the friends' irritation on the north wind. Almost everyone laughed except the geographer; soon he too recovered his temper. Their real anxiety could not be laughed away. They counted on Lake Salinas, where the streams flowing into it should provide fresh water. In the evening its shores appeared. There was no water in the lake or its tributaries. The weary horses lay down beside the dried mud. Thirst, still bearable until then, now threatened their ability to go on.

## A remembered voice

They decided to separate. Thalcave, Glenarvan and Robert would ride the strongest horses to the Guamini; the rest would follow more slowly. If the river too had dried up, the scouts would return and they would make a long detour south. Robert was almost as delighted to be taken as though this were another outing. Along the way he told the lord about his father, who brought presents from different countries, spoke softly and lulled his son to sleep with a Scottish song. The tune was growing hazy in his memory. Glenarvan listened while the horses plodded more heavily. Thaouka alone could have gone far ahead, but Thalcave would not leave his companions. Suddenly the horse began straining forward, tossing his head and testing the air. This time he sensed something other than danger. A bright line gleamed in a hollow.

## Water found

The horses plunged chest-deep into the river, carrying their riders with them. The men drank, untroubled by their involuntary bath. Nearby stood an enclosure open on one side. They decided to wait there and prepare food for the others. Game abounded near the water. Robert killed an armadillo; Thalcave skillfully brought down a nandou with his bolas; they also collected smaller birds. The meat was intended partly for their hungry companions, still laboring across the dry plain. After supper the three lay down in soft dry grass. Water had been found; the horses had drunk and were feeding. For the first time in days they could fall asleep without thinking of empty bottles. Thaouka remained standing. His quiet neigh during the night made Thalcave rise and peer into the darkness beyond the fence.

## Eyes in the darkness

Shadows moved across the plain; eyes flashed between the bushes. Thalcave loaded his gun and fired. Glenarvan and Robert sprang up at the report and the howling. Aguaras, red wolves, were approaching in an enormous hungry pack. They could still defend the opening, but little powder remained after the hunt. The guide stopped his companions as they prepared to shoot at the nearest animals and showed his almost empty flask. They had to let attackers come closer and save each charge. Thalcave gathered dry grass and lit a fire at the entrance. For a time the flames cut the pack off, but more animals pressed behind the first. Glenarvan spoke encouragingly to Robert. He himself understood that neither fire nor gunshots would last until morning.

## The last dry grass

The dry grass vanished quickly in the fire. Thalcave was already thrusting his knife through the flames at the animals; Robert stood beside him, pale but holding his weapon. Glenarvan embraced the boy, trying to conceal despair. Then the howling suddenly stopped. Robert thought the pack had gone, but Thalcave shook his head: the wolves were going around the enclosure. Scratching and cracking sounded behind the rotten boards. The horses plunged about. The guide calmly began saddling Thaouka, checking every strap. For an instant Glenarvan thought he meant to flee, but he was wrong. Thalcave intended to dash out on the swift horse, draw the pack after him and let those remaining survive until dawn. They could not all escape on the other horses, which were too exhausted and terrified.

## A leap through fire

Glenarvan refused to let the guide go and demanded the horse for himself. Thalcave would not yield. Each intended to leave the other beside the child; they argued in broken words and gestures while the wolves attacked the fence. Suddenly Robert pushed the lord aside, leaped onto Thaouka and, clinging to his mane, cleared the dying fire. A clear voice had time to bid farewell before horse and rider vanished. The pack rushed after them. Glenarvan ran outside but could see only movement receding in the dark. The guide assured him that the horse would save the boy. The lord could imagine only a fall, a single mistaken leap. He wanted to pursue them immediately, but Thalcave held him back: their remaining mounts could never catch Thaouka. They had to wait for daylight. Their own danger was over; somewhere on the plain Robert's was still continuing.

## Toward the signal shots

At dawn the two rode toward the rest of the party. Signal shots sounded in the distance. Robert was there with their companions, safe and well, still mounted on Thaouka. Glenarvan jumped down and embraced him; Thalcave hugged the boy too, then stroked and praised his horse. Robert gave the chief credit to Thaouka. When the lord asked why he had not let an adult take the risk, the boy answered simply: Thalcave had already saved his life, while Glenarvan was going to save his father. Dead wolves still lay beside the Guamini enclosure, and the food prepared the day before awaited them. Once men and horses had drunk, the party finally enjoyed the abundant breakfast for which they had hunted. The journey east continued.

## The talkative commander

The captain's trail seemed close, but the places where Thalcave expected nomads were empty. Encampments had long been abandoned; lone riders vanished before they could approach. They hoped for an explanation at Fort Independence near Tandil. There Paganel was unexpectedly welcomed as a dear fellow countryman. The commander, a former French sergeant named Manuel, had lived in Argentina for years, started a family and delighted in describing his sons, all future soldiers. He talked without pausing. Glenarvan had to wait for this other family's history to end before returning to his own painful concern. At last they learned that the tribes had gone north because of war. If the captain had been taken with them, the search would have to begin in a different direction altogether.

## Another man's story

The major asked about European captives. The commander remembered two, and hope flared again. The details did not fit, however: it had been much earlier, and one prisoner was French, the other Italian. The Italian had been killed; the Frenchman escaped. Paganel recognized the story: it concerned Guinnard, whom he had already mentioned to his friends. Another man's misfortunes had been mistaken for Grant's trail. Thalcave had honestly repeated what he heard. No one blamed him, but that brought no comfort. Robert walked silently beside Glenarvan, his eyes full of tears. At supper each faced the same map that had so recently promised to lead them to the captain. The sea was not far away now. Returning to Duncan meant admitting that they had crossed the continent without coming closer to the children's father.

## Horns above the mud

Glenarvan delayed departure; the major made the preparations himself. Paganel kept rereading the document, refusing to believe it had nothing more to tell him. Meanwhile the country ahead was changing. Wet meadows replaced dry plains, then gave way to pools and yielding ground. Robert noticed a strange field of horns: the tops of a dead herd's heads protruded from the mud. Thalcave explained that the animals had been engulfed entirely. Rain soon began. They spent the night in a wretched hut where water dripped through the roof and threatened the smoky fire. By morning the plain was even wetter. The guide rode aside, stood in his stirrups looking for a way out and urged his companions onward more insistently. Even he found the amount of water unusual.

## A tree ahead

Danger announced itself in noise and fleeing herds. To the south a broad wave appeared, sweeping away shrubs and tall grass. Thalcave turned north. At first the horses ran, then waded chest-deep, and finally swam. Looking for high ground was almost hopeless: level plain surrounded them. The major first noticed a solitary enormous tree. The current was carrying them toward it. Another wave engulfed everyone. When the men surfaced, almost all the horses had disappeared. Robert clung to Thaouka's mane. At the tree Thalcave climbed onto the branches and helped pull up the exhausted swimmers. The current swept his horse onward. The guide looked after him, jumped back into the flood and was soon swimming beside him with an arm around his neck. The friends remained in the tree and watched the two heads recede through the water.

## A kitchen in the branches

Their refuge was an ombu, with a massive trunk and a whole forest of interwoven branches. Robert and Wilson climbed higher and saw an endless watery plain. Roofs, uprooted shrubs and dead animals floated past; Thalcave and his horse were still visible far away. Glenarvan cut marks into the trunk to watch the water. For the moment it was not rising. McNabbs displayed a bag of surviving provisions: having avoided drowning, he did not intend to starve. Powder had survived too; Thalcave had handed his flask to the major before jumping. Paganel found dry moss, used his telescope lens and made fire. Wet ponchos were hung over branches. The geographer immediately turned their enforced stop into a household, with a kitchen below and bedrooms overhead.

## A name from the treetop

They could not forget the captain. Glenarvan no longer knew what to say to Robert. The major proposed following the one reliable figure, going around the world along the parallel if necessary. From his observation post above, Paganel listed the lands on that line: Atlantic islands, the Indian Ocean, Australia. The last name broke off in a cry. He came tumbling from branch to branch and almost fell into the flood; the major caught him in time. Paganel had suffered no fresh injury. A thought had struck him: from the start they might have been searching somewhere Grant had never been. The fragment “austral” need not refer only to the southern hemisphere. It could begin the name Australia.

## A new reading

Glenarvan objected: what about Patagonia and the Indians? Paganel answered that “indi” could be expanded into the French word for indigenous people, while “gonie” could belong to a different word. There were several possibilities, although this still did not supply the final solution. He offered a new reading with Australia and spoke almost as confidently as before. Leaving America no longer seemed an abandonment of the captain. The friends decided to continue by sea. To celebrate renewed hope, Paganel and Robert hunted through the branches while Wilson caught fish with a makeshift line. They had a festive supper. The usually undisputing major constantly found himself arguing with Paganel, this time over whether Noah should have preserved predatory beasts. Robert listened to them from a tree in the middle of a drowned plain.

## Fire above water

Heavy clouds rose over the water toward evening. No wind yet stirred the leaves, but the air grew close and still. Everyone tried to secure a place among the branches. Paganel unhelpfully recalled that a solitary tall tree especially attracted lightning; the major appreciated the timing of this information. Then thunder made conversation impossible. Flashes illuminated faces and the endless water. Rain fell almost vertically. A fiery light appeared on a great branch, an explosion sounded, and the ombu caught fire. The men moved onto the side still untouched by flames. Dry boughs cracked and fell into the flood. Wilson tried swimming to safety, but his cry made the others haul him back: caimans had gathered at the tree's foot. The way down was as firmly closed as the way through the fire.

## The drifting ombu

The storm itself provided the escape. A waterspout struck the ombu, uprooted it and overturned it in the current. Much of the fire went out. One caiman still climbed over the roots, but Mulrady hit it with a heavy branch and knocked it away. Their refuge now raced across the flooded plain instead of standing still, lighting the darkness with its remaining burning boughs. Only toward morning did the roots begin scraping the ground; then the tree stopped abruptly. The men scrambled ashore and heard a familiar whistle. Thalcave was waiting: the current had carried him and Thaouka to the same place. A fire and meat were already prepared in an abandoned farm building. Paganel tried to explain Australia to him. What the guide saw first was that his friends were alive and hoping again.

## Lights offshore

They walked the last miles; Thaouka carried tired men in turn. By the time they reached the sand hills above the Atlantic, darkness was gathering. They could hear the sea nearby but could see no yacht. Glenarvan could not believe Mangles had failed to arrive in time. He paced the beach looking for lights, then woke Paganel, remembering his good night vision. The geographer honestly peered into the black horizon, nodded more and more heavily and finally fell asleep on his feet. The lord led him back to his bed in the sand. At dawn Duncan appeared, standing far offshore because of the waves and shoals. Thalcave fired several times. A white puff rose from the yacht and a cannon's report reached them. A boat was coming for them.

## Thalcave stays

While it worked its way ashore, Glenarvan invited Thalcave to sail with them. The guide pointed to Thaouka and the Pampas: he belonged here. He refused payment too, explaining that he had helped for friendship. After the flood the travelers had almost nothing left to give him. Glenarvan took out Helena's portrait and offered it; Paganel gave his map of South America, which Thalcave had often studied with interest. Robert embraced both guide and horse. As he was entering the boat, Thalcave lifted him once more and bade him farewell as a man. Asked whether they would ever meet again, he answered only: who knew? For a long time his silhouette remained visible above the surf. Then the shore receded, and Robert at last threw his arms around Mary on Duncan's deck.

## A place at a real table

From the yacht Mary had tried to count the people in the approaching boat, imagining she saw her father, then losing hope again. Glenarvan hastened to explain that Grant was not with them, but a new way of searching had opened. Over breakfast they described the mountains, the condor, Thalcave and the flood. Robert was embarrassed by the praise and hid his face beside his sister. Paganel delighted in a real tablecloth after branches and dried meat, took second helpings of every dish and blamed absentmindedness. Mangles described his voyage around Cape Horn and how calmly Helena and Mary had weathered the storm, chiefly anxious for the land party. Robert innocently embarrassed his sister by announcing how fond she was of the captain. John too grew uncomfortable and tried to change the subject to the journey. Watching them, Glenarvan and his wife already understood each other without explanation.

## Every land on the parallel

Before sailing again, McNabbs asked for another look at the map. Australia now seemed as certain as Patagonia once had: why should a third equally convincing country not follow? They examined every land along the parallel. Tristan da Cunha and Amsterdam did not fit the surviving words; New Zealand consisted of islands, whereas they read “continent” in the message. In the Pacific the English chart named a little Maria Theresa, which explained nothing either. Australia remained the most plausible conjecture. Nevertheless, the major insisted that they visit the island groups along the way: better check them now than return later. Mangles was ordered to steer for Tristan da Cunha. Uncertainty had not disappeared, but for the first time since the Chilean shore they again had a direction in which they could act.

## A cabin for her father

At sea their familiar routine returned. Paganel wrote his impressions of the Pampas and polished sentences aloud. Mary arranged a cabin for her father, vacated by Olbinett. They had not found him, but his place aboard already waited. At Tristan da Cunha they questioned inhabitants and sent boats around the shore, finding no trace of Britannia. After replenishing coal at the Cape of Good Hope they headed for the Amsterdam group. Along the way Paganel enthusiastically imagined life alone on uninhabited land. Helena reminded him that his dreams conveniently supplied a cave, food and agreeable company, whereas a genuinely solitary man might wait years to hear another voice. On the island old Monsieur Viot, a Frenchman living with two workers, welcomed them. He knew nothing of Britannia and wished them success. They returned to the yacht, leaving another examined possibility behind.

## Rifle against telescope

The damaged message's date again prompted discussion. If the ship had sailed from Callao late in May, how could it have reached Australia early in June? Paganel allowed that the sea might have erased a digit before the surviving seven. With a later June date the crossing became possible. This was another addition to the conjecture, but the travelers readily accepted it. Soon the debate became more playful. The major doubted Paganel could name as many Australian explorers as he claimed. They wagered rifle against telescope. Paganel began with the earliest navigators and listed names until his listeners begged for mercy. McNabbs then offered to win back his gun with a historical fact unknown to the scholar: an English joke about a French captain supposedly frightened away by croaking frogs. Paganel resented the joke, but lost the wager. The major kept his weapon.

## The propeller stops

Before Australia the wind dropped. The silence worried Mangles: the barometer was falling although the sky remained clear. He stayed on deck, reduced sail early and prepared his men. The storm came at night. When the passengers ventured up, waves were already sweeping over the side. The captain sent the women below, then heard an alarming whistle from the engine room. The engine had stopped. One propeller blade had bent and jammed it. The yacht now had to fight under sail. Glenarvan wanted to remain and help, but this time John insisted: he needed a clear deck and only his sailors. The lord obeyed. In the cabin Paganel recalled Shakespeare's boatswain in The Tempest, defending his right to manage the ship among the waves even before a king.

## Beyond the breakers

Sails tore; the yacht plunged bows-first and rose again. A small surviving piece of canvas finally let her run before the wind, but that carried her straight toward land. Mangles warned Glenarvan that he might have to beach the ship to save those aboard. The lord entrusted Mary to him, resolving to remain with his wife. Through the fog appeared terrible breakers; beyond them the captain distinguished quieter water. To reach it they still had to cross the bar. He ordered oil casks brought up and, at the right moment, emptied over the sides. The film briefly subdued the crests, and Duncan slipped into a sheltered inlet. Behind her the waves rose again. The yacht anchored. Glenarvan's brief thanks were enough for John to understand the value of what he had achieved.

## A distant windmill

Inspection confirmed the damaged propeller. They decided to sail to Melbourne for repairs, continuing to examine the coastline on the way. There was still no trace of the captain. At Cape Bernouilli they landed on an empty shore, climbed through a break in the cliffs and noticed a windmill in the distance. They walked toward it through scrub; gradually fences, fields, livestock and a house appeared. The owner came to meet them with his wife and sons. Paddy O'Moore, an Irishman, welcomed them without questions and invited them to a table already laid. Poverty at home had driven his family here; now the farm supported many people. Servants ate with the family. He readily told his own story but demanded no answering confession from his guests. Glenarvan himself asked about Britannia. O'Moore remembered neither the name nor a corresponding wreck.

## A voice at the table's end

They had to explain the search. One of the workers listened at the common table alongside hosts and guests. When Glenarvan finished with almost no hope, he spoke: if Captain Grant was alive, Australia was indeed the place to look. He called himself Tom Ayrton, quartermaster of Britannia. He did not claim to have seen the captain after the wreck: a wave, he said, had swept him off before the ship struck. But he confidently named the eastern coast and the right latitude. Here at last was someone who appeared to have stood on that deck, rather than merely read a piece of paper. Mary held his hand and begged him to speak about her father. Each detail seemed more precious than the abstract conjectures they had carried so far around the world.

## The captain's signature

Ayrton remembered Mary and Robert before the departure from Glasgow, the farewell breakfast, the boy already trying to climb the rigging. Robert confirmed the memory. The account continued through a voyage, storm, captivity and escape across inland Australia. According to Ayrton, after surviving he had spent a long time among indigenous people before escaping and reaching the farm. O'Moore vouched for his work and honesty. Still the major asked about his papers. Ayrton produced his engagement aboard Britannia, and Mary immediately recognized her father's signature. There could be little doubt that he had actually served with Grant. The other parts of his account were accepted as true as well. To the children he became a living link with their missing father; to the expedition, a guide toward a definite place instead of another interpretation of scraps.

## Another way to the coast

They could wait for the repairs or cross southern Australia to the eastern coast, calling the yacht later. Paganel warmly supported the crossing. This was not the waterless interior, he assured them, but settled Victoria: the women could ride in a wagon and the men on horseback. Asked about robbers and convicts, he cited local prohibitions. Glenarvan agreed. Ayrton promised to lead them at least to the supposed scene of the wreck. He was especially concerned about where Duncan would wait for further instructions. The captain also wished to go, listing every reason except the one already understood. Glenarvan asked whether Tom Austin could be trusted. At John's confident reply he allowed him to join: it would be well for him to be present when Mary's father was found.

## A room on wheels

A long heavy wagon with solid wooden wheels was altered for Helena and Mary. Mangles and the carpenter divided it: stores and Olbinett's kitchen behind, a little room with a carpet, couches and leather curtains in front. Oxen would draw it and Ayrton would drive. The rest obtained horses. During a return visit aboard the yacht, the quartermaster carefully examined the engine, bunkers, weapons, cannon and rigging. Her speed particularly impressed him: such a vessel could escape a pursuing warship. At dinner he advised taking more sailors into the land party, but Glenarvan chose to leave enough hands for work and repairs. Before parting, Mangles again ordered Austin to wait at Melbourne and carry out the lord's instructions exactly. Loud cheers followed the expedition ashore.

## Supper at Apsley

For the first days the wagon rolled across open plains. Helena invited riders in turn into her traveling drawing room; Paganel found time to hunt, converse and observe. His confidence in this extraordinary country knew no limits. Over supper at Apsley he listed astonishing plants and animals and exact livestock totals; the major immediately asked whether they included the sheep they were eating. Even the climate's properties came into the discussion. Paganel declared that the local air could reform criminals themselves. Helena wondered what it would do to a man already as good as the geographer. He promised to become excellent. While these accounts remained part of a cheerful evening, no one yet saw a reason to test this buoyant explanation of the world seriously.

## Dust above the herd

One day a huge dust cloud appeared over the plain. It came from a herd driven by Sam Machell with numerous men and dogs. His journey had lasted months and would continue for many more. He described leading oxen, obstinate sheep, weeks of waiting before a river the animals might suddenly decide to cross for no new reason. While he talked, the living mass that raised the dust kept passing. Soon he rode back to its head and the wagon went in the opposite direction. That evening Olbinett reminded everyone of Christmas through the festive food he had kept for the occasion. Scottish dishes, tea and wine appeared in the Australian camp. For a little while they might imagine themselves at Malcolm again, although an unfamiliar country lay beyond the tent and the road awaited them in the morning.

## A trefoil horseshoe

At the Wimerra crossing Ayrton selected shallow ground and assured them that the women need not leave the wagon. All went well until midstream; then the water rose, the wagon lurched and something cracked. The quartermaster jumped beside the oxen and helped keep their course. They all reached the opposite bank, but the wagon's forepart was broken and Glenarvan's horse had lost a shoe. Ayrton offered to ride to Black Point for a smith. Next morning he returned with a silent workman who repaired the damage quickly and skillfully. McNabbs noticed deep recent marks around his wrists and asked about them. He received no answer. The new horseshoe bore a cut trefoil. Ayrton explained that the local brand helped identify strayed horses. Once paid, the smith departed with hardly a word, and the travelers moved on.

## The swing bridge

Towns appeared unexpectedly among the pastures. Ayrton preferred going around them to save time, but Paganel and Robert still inspected Carisbrook: a bank, courthouse, market, regular streets and busy people concerned with gold. Later they heard a whistle ahead and saw inhabitants hurrying toward the railway. Beside the bridge over the Loddon lay a wrecked train. Five carriages and the engine had fallen into the river; the sixth had been saved by a broken coupling. Smoke rose from twisted metal while rescuers fought the fire. At first people blamed the swing-bridge keeper's carelessness. But luggage had been robbed and the surviving passengers attacked. The inspector considered it a crime. When the keeper's murdered body was brought in, the theory of his complicity collapsed. Suspicion fell on escaped convicts. Glenarvan led his friends away, trying to spare the women the sight.

## The boy with a label

Near the road a little boy in European clothes slept under a tree. They found a label on him: Toline, to be taken to Echuca, carriage paid. Paganel resented a child being sent almost like luggage. On waking, Toline spoke English. He had indeed been on the wrecked train; the man entrusted with him had died. He was returning to his family on the Lachlan after years at a Melbourne school. He called Robert his brother and offered his hand. Glenarvan decided to camp there for the night. The newcomer sat beside Robert at supper, and Robert chose the best morsels for him. Toline meant to become a missionary. His seriousness and religious fervor touched the company; news of his first prize in geography finally won Paganel over.

## Where Lord Napoleon lives

The prize was a small Bible bearing the school's inscription. The geographer delightedly began examining the boy, and the further his questions went the more the major enjoyed himself. Toline's school map, from Oceania's islands to Europe, proved entirely English. France was a province, its chief city Calais, where Lord Napoleon lived. Paganel tried to object, then could no longer restrain his laughter. Toline answered steadily and confidently: he was repeating the knowledge for which he had won his prize and could not understand the storm of amusement. Even the moon, he believed, would become English in time. The geographer had to walk away from camp to finish laughing. McNabbs reminded him that he had foreseen how the pupil might enlighten such a learned examiner.

## The gift of a geography

Glenarvan found Richardson's geographical compendium among their traveling books and gave it to Toline so he could correct his mistaken ideas. The boy accepted it, looked through it and shook his head, reluctant to pocket the gift. Robert shared his bed with him that night. In the morning Toline was gone. No one could say whether he was eager to reach his family or hurt by Paganel's laughter. Helena found a fresh mimosa branch beside her. In his pocket the geographer discovered the very new compendium Glenarvan had given away. Conversation about the strange boy still accompanied the caravan as the trees grew taller and the road led into long bright forest avenues.

## The newspaper at Seymour

After the railway crime the caravan kept closer together and posted watches at night. In Seymour the major read a newspaper report about Ben Joyce's gang. A reward was offered for its leader. He informed his companions after the women had retired. They could no longer dismiss convicts so easily, as Paganel had thought them out of place here. Glenarvan proposed discussing a return to the yacht in Melbourne. Ayrton objected: the southern road was no safer, while continuing east might still reveal the captain's trail. They decided to proceed. Mangles, however, refused the request to summon Duncan to the coast immediately. She might still be under repair and their plans might change. Better issue the order once they reached the sea themselves.

## Mozart among the trees

The eucalyptus forest gave less shade than its height promised. Trunks rose far overhead without branches; sunlight penetrated the foliage. Paganel explained everything with his usual enthusiasm; the major observed that the trees had considered themselves but not travelers. Olbinett used a burnt-out hollow trunk as a hearth. Later, on watch, the geographer heard a piano. He was ready to attribute it to some incredible Australian bird until he recognized an aria by Mozart. Next morning two young neighboring landowners, Michael and Sandy Patterson, rode up and invited them to visit. The music had indeed come from their house. Among remote pastures stood a great established property with European trees and a comfortable home. After asking about the search, the hosts invited them to rest there for a day.

## Mangles's promise

During the hunt Robert again got ahead. Dogs pursued kangaroos; a large male stopped at a tree and defended itself so fiercely that one dog died. The boy went too close and the animal sprang upon him. Mary saw her brother fall from the carriage. No one dared shoot for fear of hitting him. Mangles opened his knife, rushed forward and killed the animal at close quarters. Robert rose unhurt. His sister embraced him and offered John her trembling hand in thanks. He merely recalled his promise to watch the boy. The hunt ended there. Next morning the visitors parted from the Pattersons, inviting them to Malcolm one day. The comfortable house vanished behind the trees. Mountains lay ahead, which Paganel initially described as no very serious obstacle either.

## The road through hail

The Australian Alps demanded hard work. Rocks, ravines and thickets blocked the way; the wagon had to be supported on slopes, and women and men walked when the animals found the load too hard. They asked about the pass at the little Bush Inn. A reward notice for Ben Joyce hung on the wall. Then Mulrady's horse suddenly fell dead. It had shown no obvious illness; fatigue was offered as the explanation. Glenarvan gave the sailor his own horse and joined the wagon. On the descent a terrible hailstorm struck: great pieces of ice pierced the covering and battered people and animals. They sheltered among rocks until it passed. Beyond the pass Ayrton again urged them to summon the yacht, but the major and Mangles held to their plan: reach Twofold Bay, then send the order.

## The wheels sink

The Gippsland plain was oppressive. Among tree ferns Paganel's horse suddenly collapsed. He disentangled himself and announced that it was dead, like Mulrady's before it. Another horse and an ox soon died. Each loss now threatened the whole journey: without draught animals they would have to abandon the wagon, with the coast still far away. Yet the survivors looked healthy. Next day they made better progress, and visitors, cool drinks and conversation returned to the moving drawing room. Hope of a safe conclusion revived whenever several miles passed without a fresh misfortune. Toward evening mist appeared above the Snowy. Ayrton turned the oxen toward woods beside the river, and the heavy wheels sank abruptly into soft ground. The wagon was embedded up to its hubs.

## Night beside the Snowy

Attempts to move it merely exhausted the team. They decided to wait for daylight, pitch the tent and examine the situation in the morning. Ayrton himself got the oxen out of the mud and took the remaining animals to pasture, trusting no one else with their care. Glenarvan even thanked him for his diligence. After a brief supper they lay down in heavy, stifling air; distant lightning glimmered on the horizon. Only the last stretch remained to the place the quartermaster described as Britannia's wreck site. Yet they could not advance even a few steps. The river sounded beyond the trees, the wagon stood fast in clay, and their horses were dwindling. During the night the major woke and noticed a strange faint light between the trunks.
