# Essentialism — essential

## promise — A promise people can trust

Graphic designer Trisha Morse used to regard fulfilling every client request as good work. Agreement brought an immediate reward: the client thanked her, and she felt useful. But her promises grew beyond the time and energy available. Working almost continuously, Trisha began making mistakes; neither her clients nor she liked the results. In McKeown's account, the change begins with a fairly modest restriction: before accepting an assignment, she checks whether she can complete it on time with the resources she has. If she cannot, she declines. Clients are disappointed, but they understand the honesty of her answer.

## promise-2 — A promise people can trust · 2

Gradually, the question changes. Trisha now asks not only whether another assignment will fit into her schedule, but whether there is a better use of her resources. Declining some entirely feasible assignments frees time for creativity, preparation and anticipating obstacles. According to the author, quality recovers, she becomes calmer, and clients' trust increases: accepting a commitment really means that she will finish the work. The entire transition matters—from automatic agreement through a test of feasibility to a deliberate choice of contribution. Merely reducing the number of assignments would not explain why she keeps the others.

## promise-3 — A promise people can trust · 3

The book grows from this question: where should people direct their limited time and energy to make their most significant possible contribution? McKeown calls his answer Essentialism. It is a philosophy of contribution and a way to make decisions, then organize their execution. Fewer activities serve the better accomplishment of what has been chosen. A full day does not show that effort went toward something important; an empty day proves little by itself either. The central task is to distinguish what deserves a person's full commitment from what prevents that commitment.

## cycle — Explore, eliminate, execute

McKeown compares accumulating commitments to an overfilled wardrobe. Ask whether each item might someday be useful, and almost everything earns the right to remain. Stricter questions—whether it fits, gets used and deserves its space—help separate what is needed. But arranging clothes into two piles does not yet remove anything. Money spent, memories and doubts remain; the bag must actually leave the house. Sustaining order then requires a regular process rather than waiting for the next collapse from the shelves.

## cycle-2 — Explore, eliminate, execute · 2

This produces three connected actions: explore possible directions, eliminate nonessential commitments, and organize execution of the essential ones. The first requires time to consider alternatives; the second takes courage to decline and stop sustaining the unnecessary; the third needs a system that reduces obstacles. Exploration alone produces only a long list of possibilities. Refusal alone can free time without identifying its purpose. Trying to execute everything more easily can improve the very overload a person wanted to escape.

## cycle-3 — Explore, eliminate, execute · 3

These actions form a recurring cycle. Life differs from a wardrobe because new demands arrive continuously rather than staying where they were put yesterday. The chosen tasks change too. The subject is therefore not one final plan after which thinking becomes unnecessary, but a discipline of returning to the question of greatest contribution. The author develops its foundation through three propositions: the capacity to choose, the unequal value of opportunities, and the inevitability of trade-offs.

## choice-3 — Default is a choice too · 3

The author distinguishes available alternatives from the act of choosing. He insists that, although we do not always control the alternatives available to us, choosing among them remains ours. In his philosophy, this inner capacity cannot be taken away; it can be forgotten. McKeown therefore places renewed recognition of one's power to choose at the beginning of Essentialism. If every incoming demand is treated as compulsory in advance, selection cannot even be discussed: the allocation of one's energy has already been handed over to other people's decisions.

## returns-2 — Effort has unequal returns · 2

Unequal returns do not make diligence useless. He disputes the assumption that each additional unit of effort produces approximately equal value. He invokes the Pareto principle and Joseph Juran's work on the vital few: in quality improvement, a substantial part of the result may depend on a small share of causes. The 80/20 relationship illustrates unevenness here; it is not a promise that every personal calendar follows exact arithmetic. What matters for selection is establishing where differences in returns really are large.

## returns-3 — Effort has unequal returns · 3

If alternatives are unequal, comparing them ceases to be an interruption of work. Further exploration can repay itself when a person subsequently spends a long time on a more significant activity. McKeown's Essentialist therefore considers more alternatives than someone who immediately agrees to the first good opportunity. They accept fewer things, but do not necessarily notice fewer. Differences must be seen first; otherwise, reduction will be arbitrary and important work may disappear alongside the unnecessary.

## values — When values collide

A list of attractive words such as innovation, leadership and quality offers little help when these values pull an employee in different directions. The same applies to promising equal service to customers, employees and shareholders. McKeown contrasts this enumeration with ordered obligations. In his account of the 1982 crisis involving poisoned Tylenol packages, Johnson & Johnson relies on a credo that places consumers before shareholders. Recalling the product means enormous losses, but the ordering of values supplies a basis for choosing customer safety.

## values-2 — When values collide · 2

Strategy should help when everything cannot be preserved at once. For the same reason, McKeown objects to seventeen top-priority projects when resources cover five. Spreading inadequate effort across all the items does not resolve the conflict; it makes the conflict less visible until delivery. Clarity requires saying what will take precedence and what must be postponed or removed.

## values-3 — When values collide · 3

The author also expressly qualifies the joke about four burners—family, friends, health and work—some of which supposedly must be switched off for success. He does not demand sacrificing family or health. His requirement is to address actual conflicts among priorities consciously. Family, care and work do not become nonessential simply because they take time. The task and the consequences of choosing must be identified, rather than making severity of refusal a virtue in itself.

## space — A place to understand

Before choosing less, we need conditions in which differences can become visible. McKeown describes Frank O'Brien of the marketing firm Conversation: once a month, employees meet without phones or email to think and talk. The meeting occupies the first Monday of the month rather than an accidentally available hour. Clients know the arrangement. Protected time no longer depends on whether urgent requests happen to stop arriving that day. Someone being too busy to attend gives O'Brien a reason to examine workload and how work is organized.

## asset-3 — Protecting the ability to choose · 3

For McKeown, sleep belongs in the exploration section. Distinguishing among opportunities requires attention, memory and an ability to notice connections. An additional waking hour is not necessarily an additional hour of effective work. McKeown admits that he himself tried drastic sleep reduction before abandoning it. For him, needing recovery does not indicate insufficient ambition: ambitious people may find it especially difficult to agree to do less today in order to retain their ability to act well tomorrow.

## selection — A strict criterion cannot predict the future

For exploration to end in a choice, McKeown proposes explicit, strict criteria. His 90 percent rule begins with identifying the most important criterion for the decision. An alternative is rated from zero to one hundred; anything below ninety is rejected. This is a way to stop agonizing among numerous fairly suitable possibilities. The number expresses a high selection threshold, but does not turn a subjective rating into a measured probability of success. What is being assessed and why it matters still have to be decided.

## selection-2 — A strict criterion cannot predict the future · 2

The author develops the rule while selecting program participants: obviously strong and obviously unsuitable applicants stand out quickly, while intermediate ones consume discussion. He proposes not filling a place simply because someone is good enough. Yet he acknowledges the price of this strictness: after declining a good offer and waiting for an excellent one, it may never arrive. The method makes the choice conscious; it does not guarantee a favorable continuation. Without this qualification, selection would become a promise the book does not make.

## selection-3 — A strict criterion cannot predict the future · 3

For an unexpectedly arriving opportunity, he offers a fuller procedure. First write it down, then identify three minimum and three ideal criteria, ordering each list by importance. The option must meet all three minimum conditions and at least two of the three ideal ones. Otherwise, the answer is negative. Writing matters: the appeal of easy gains or the fear of missing out should not quietly rewrite the conditions. An explicit criterion also permits discussing the decision with others, including a manager, instead of silently submitting to the next request.

## intent — An intent that excludes the unnecessary

After selecting a promising direction, the goal still has to become clear enough for daily decisions. McKeown calls this essential intent. It should be both inspiring and concrete, meaningful and recognizable when achieved. A broad desire to change the world does not say which work to choose today. A quarterly measure may be specific, yet fail on its own to explain why people should unite their efforts. Intent connects direction with an identifiable result and therefore prevents many recurring arguments.

## intent-2 — An intent that excludes the unnecessary · 2

The author suggests beginning with a decision rather than elegant wording: if only one thing could be done really well, what would it be? The next question asks how success would be recognized. The answer must withstand actual trade-offs between competing projects. If the statement accommodates everything, it fails its purpose. Clarity also extends to roles: people need to know the contribution expected of each person, or even a comprehensible shared task can leave daily work undefined.

## refusal — Declining a request while preserving a relationship

A clear intent now has to withstand someone else's wish. McKeown explains the difficulty of refusal through fear of disappointing people, seeming ungrateful, missing opportunities or harming relationships. He does not regard these feelings as a character defect. But if they entirely determine the answer, brief awkwardness becomes a lasting commitment that displaces something important. Deciding about a request must therefore be separated from one's relationship with the person: declining a particular activity does not in itself reject friendship, respect or cooperation.

## refusal-2 — Declining a request while preserving a relationship · 2

A graceful refusal has to be sufficiently clear. An evasive promise to try, when someone already knows they cannot deliver, leaves the other party uncertain and creates greater inconvenience later. Courtesy appears in the form of the answer and attention to circumstances, but does not require concealing its substance. A blunt single word is not compulsory; current workload can be explained, limited help offered or another time suggested, if that help and time really fit. The point is an honest boundary around the new commitment.

## refusal-3 — Declining a request while preserving a relationship · 3

McKeown also acknowledges the immediate price: refusal can cause irritation, disappointment and anger. He hopes for respect over time, but this does not remove the risk at the moment of deciding. The question of the trade-off remains central. Before choosing a convenient answer, we need to understand which existing important activity agreement will displace. In the book, refusal is a means of truly agreeing to the essential, not grounds for treating every other person's needs as insignificant.

## manager — Talking with a manager

Workplace hierarchy calls for a particular response. McKeown suggests making the actual trade-off visible to a manager rather than silently dropping assignments. When a new urgent task arrives, an employee can agree to make it first and ask which previous priority should be removed. Another option is explaining that existing commitments prevent completing the additional work to the required standard. The discussion moves from a presumed willingness to try hard to allocating finite capacity. The decision about changing priorities returns to the person entitled to make it.

## sunk-costs — Past spending cannot decide the future

The hardest work begins after the unnecessary has already received agreement. Discussing Concorde, McKeown explains sunk-cost logic: the more invested in an unprofitable undertaking, the stronger the desire to continue and finally justify the expenditure. He interprets the project's prolonged support as an example of this trap. What matters to his argument is not the aircraft's engineering merit, but the confusion between two questions—what cannot be recovered and what deserves further resources. Continuing does not itself bring back the past.

## sunk-costs-2 — Past spending cannot decide the future · 2

In a research scenario he reports, someone has bought nonrefundable tickets for two skiing trips that accidentally fall on the same weekend. The more expensive trip costs one hundred dollars; the more desirable one costs fifty. According to the book, more than half the participants prefer the expensive trip despite expecting less enjoyment. Their decision preserves an appearance of thrift, but both sums have already been spent regardless of the choice. Trying not to acknowledge a loss can deprive a person of a further, better available result.

## sunk-costs-3 — Past spending cannot decide the future · 3

McKeown proposes shifting the assessment toward the future: how much time, money or effort would someone invest in this project if starting today from zero? How else could the remaining resources be used? This requires admitting a mistake rather than defending it through further investment. In the book, acknowledgment releases a person from continuing an unnecessary decision. It does not remove the difficulty of withdrawing from a commitment to others, so reassessment also requires communicating the change rather than simply disappearing.

## zero-base-2 — Commitments need a present justification · 2

Casual promises also require an interruption. The author proposes a short pause before agreeing, allowing time to understand a request's importance. If a promise already causes regret, he advises finding a way to withdraw, apologizing and explaining that the whole situation was not understood when agreement was given. This acknowledges the uncomfortable side of changing a decision: others were relying on it. Avoiding unnecessary promises initially is preferable, but the age of a mistaken agreement should not prohibit reconsideration forever.

## boundaries — A boundary can be expensive

Jin-Young completes a presentation for a board meeting early, working long days to free time before her own wedding. But her manager, Hyori, sees the available time and requests another project. Previously, Jin-Young always accepted such requests; now she declines, protecting the time she deliberately created. Other employees also decline, and Hyori must do the assignment herself. In McKeown's account, she is initially angry, then notices flaws in her management and starts clarifying expectations, responsibilities and the team's work results.

## boundaries-2 — A boundary can be expensive · 2

In another case, Clayton Christensen declines Saturday work because he has promised the time to his family, then declines its Sunday replacement on religious grounds. The author reports that his position eventually earned respect. But he explicitly warns that neither story guarantees favorable outcomes for others. Jin-Young might have faced worse consequences, and Christensen's refusal could have damaged his career. The cost of boundaries is real and cannot be removed from the argument to make a neat promise of respect.

## boundaries-3 — A boundary can be expensive · 3

A boundary identifies what is protected in advance, rather than forcing an improvised refusal under pressure each time. McKeown regards it as a source of freedom within chosen space. Its content, however, is tied to what a person actually considers essential and the consequences they will accept. Having a boundary does not automatically make someone else's task worthless. It helps clarify which part of that task one should accept and which cannot quietly be added to existing responsibility.

## buffer — Execution begins with a margin

After selection and elimination, a question remains: why is important work still so difficult? McKeown proposes directing released resources toward organizing execution. The first part is a buffer. The world is unpredictable: journeys take longer, suppliers are delayed, clients change requirements. A schedule without gaps works only when all its assumptions happen to hold. When any deviation makes tasks collide, people must rescue them through overnight work, lower quality or passing problems to colleagues.

## buffer-2 — Execution begins with a margin · 2

The author compares a margin to safe distance between cars. It is needed not because the driver knows exactly when the car ahead will brake, but because they do not. In one example, a four-hour seminar reserves an entire hour for questions and discussion. Organizers consider this excessive; with the reserve reduced, the conversation does indeed run out of time. Restoring the buffer preserves both substantive discussion and the overall schedule. Unallocated time serves the result rather than indicating insufficient preparation.

## constraint — Removing obstacles in sequence

McKeown begins the search for a limiting obstacle with an outcome and a completion criterion. In the book's example, a fifteen-page report draft must reach a client by two on Thursday. Next, list obstacles: missing information, fatigue, perfectionism and others. Find the one whose removal would most substantially unblock the other actions. The author does not treat all impediments as equally important. At a particular moment, one may limit all progress, while work on secondary obstacles leaves the result unchanged.

## constraint-2 — Removing obstacles in sequence · 2

If trying to write a perfect text immediately is the constraint, an additional researcher will not solve it. The draft must be allowed to remain a draft because that is the result currently required. If delay involves another person, the author advises asking what prevents them from completing their part and offering help with that obstacle instead of another reprimand. Conditions for action become the object of joint work. This differs from demanding that someone simply try harder under the same difficulty.

## progress — Small progress must matter

The scale of an intent does not require beginning with the largest gesture. McKeown contrasts grand projects with a small victory that really advances the desired result. He cites Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer's work on diaries of workplace experience: progress in meaningful work is associated with motivation and positive feelings. Both terms matter—progress and meaning. Completing numerous small items can create a sense of activity without advancing the principal goal, so the subject is not accumulating arbitrary checkmarks.

## progress-2 — Small progress must matter · 2

His question about minimum viable progress seeks the smallest result that already has value. While preparing the book, he first tested ideas briefly in public posts, then developed themes that interested readers into articles. At Pixar, in his description, work begins with drawn story alternatives and early screenings rather than a finished, polished film. A small result supplies information before large investments. This does not remove later work on quality, but allows earlier understanding of what is worth improving.

## progress-3 — Small progress must matter · 3

Another technique is minimum preparation in advance. Opening a future presentation file and adding initial thoughts can happen long before the deadline, without trying to produce the complete text immediately. A small beginning makes subsequent work more accessible and reduces the need for a heroic last-night push. The author also advises making movement visible: notice and recognize intermediate achievements. Effort then receives a concrete response and provides support for the next step, instead of constant comparison with an enormous, still-unattained goal.

## routine — Routine saves energy for what matters

A well-designed routine should make doing the chosen work a natural continuation of the day. Without it, a person repeatedly has to decide where to begin, when to prepare and whether to become distracted. McKeown connects repetition with the gradual easing of an acquired action. This does not promise the total disappearance of effort: practice remains necessary, and difficult work remains difficult. But less energy goes toward identical decisions, leaving more for the task's substance, exploration or creativity.

## routine-2 — Routine saves energy for what matters · 2

In the book's account, Michael Phelps follows the same sequence of preparation for competitions over many years: arriving early, warming up, putting on his suit and headphones, and completing familiar stages before the start. With coach Bob Bowman, he regularly rehearses the swim mentally in detail. The race therefore enters a familiar sequence already underway, rather than appearing as an isolated moment when he must assemble himself for the first time. The author describes organized ease resting on extensive prior work, not an absence of training.

## routine-3 — Routine saves energy for what matters · 3

Order need not oppose novelty. If stable elements of the day cease to require constant choice, more attention remains for the unfamiliar. Yet habits can also serve the nonessential—automatically opened email, a distracting purchase, an unhelpful activity. The discipline of routine therefore follows the question of content. Making something automatic does not make it important; first, we must understand which action deserves that place in life.

## presence — What matters in this game

In his account of rugby coach Larry Gelwix, McKeown emphasizes the question of what is important now. Players are asked to return to the current game rather than endlessly relive a previous mistake or worry about the eventual result. Another distinction is concentrating on their own coordinated play instead of losing it while mentally trying to become the other team. The author connects the approach with the coach's success.

## presence-2 — What matters in this game · 2

Presence completes the logic of selection at the level of an individual moment. Someone can select important work, clear time for it and still mentally occupy themselves with five other activities. Protected attention has effectively been dispersed again. McKeown does not require banning all simultaneous actions: people can eat and talk or wash dishes and listen to the radio. He distinguishes combining actions from attempting full concentration on two subjects at once. His concern is the direction of attention, not a count of physical movements.

## presence-3 — What matters in this game · 3

Planning the future and learning from the past remain useful. But people can act in the present, and reflection should serve that action rather than endlessly displace it. The question of what is essential therefore gains temporal precision. Something important in general does not necessarily need execution this minute. Being present sometimes requires postponing even a genuinely good task until its proper time.

## identity — A way of being, not another activity

In the concluding chapter, McKeown distinguishes using a few techniques from adopting Essentialism as one's normal way of living. In the first case, the new method can become another item on an already long list. In the second, the rule by which the list is formed changes. Repeated choices of the essential gradually make the next such choice more familiar. The author describes a change of identity through accumulating actions, not a single solemn decision after which difficulty never returns.

## identity-2 — A way of being, not another activity · 2

He acknowledges continuing to want to please, accept too many attractive offers and check his phone. Becoming therefore remains a process. Gandhi, religious traditions and other examples of simplicity appear in the book as images of life organized around a higher meaning; McKeown does not require copying their deprivations or activities. His own choice might mean an evening with his wife Anna instead of a speaking engagement, or time with children instead of meeting a celebrity. The essential is determined not by an achievement's prominence, but by what someone chooses to give a finite life to.

## team-2 — The same discipline for a team · 2

McKeown identifies five areas of team leadership: talent, strategy, empowerment, communication and accountability. A leader selects people carefully; establishes a concrete essential intent; clarifies each person's contribution and boundaries of responsibility; communicates what is needed clearly and on time; and follows the work in order to help overcome difficulty and recognize progress. The absence of constant interference does not mean the absence of oversight. McKeown contrasts useful regular support with swinging between total supervision and a manager's prolonged disappearance.

## team-3 — The same discipline for a team · 3

This completes the workplace side of the philosophy of contribution. Fewer coordinated directions should let people reinforce one another instead of competing only for the boss's attention. A clear result and clear roles provide grounds for discussing new requests because the important work they displace becomes visible. Personal selection and organizational design are connected in the book: where everyone must serve everything simultaneously, individual calendar techniques cannot resolve the entire conflict of responsibility.

## final — The close-up

At the book's end, McKeown turns to a parable about a father collecting video recordings after his young daughter's death. There are many tapes: trips, places, food and events are recorded in detail. Yet there are almost no close-ups of the child herself. He preserved the surroundings and missed what he now wanted to see the recordings for. Through this parable, the author explains his own view of family and life's brevity. Its question concerns the object of attention within time already being lived.

McKeown arrives at something other than another productivity exercise. What matters is that life should not become a collection of external achievements from which the people and work recognized as essential have disappeared. Choosing, exploring, declining, preparing execution and being present need to remain connected. Doing less then acquires positive content: sufficient energy to give oneself fully to what has been chosen. The author hopes this will bring greater clarity, control and joy. His philosophy's ultimate criterion is a life whose allocation a person can answer for as their own choice.
