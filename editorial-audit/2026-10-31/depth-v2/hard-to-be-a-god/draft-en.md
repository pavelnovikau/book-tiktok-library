# Hard to Be a God

A connected original-word condensation of Arkady and Boris Strugatsky's novel. Working complete Deep route; reveals the ending. Headings mark source divisions, not reader screens.

## Prologue

Anton, Pashka and Anka had slipped out of boarding school to the lake and hauled their boat onto the sandy shore. A whole day without adults lay ahead. Anka had a modern crossbow, Anton a heavy one made after an ancient design; Pashka preferred an air rifle. They shot at a pine until Anka reminded them that damaging a tree was wrong. Then they decided to find an abandoned highway missing from the map. The forest smelled of resin and wild strawberries. Walking beside Anka, Anton enjoyed being close to her and tried not to look like a boy desperately eager to impress.

Pashka staged an ambush, his friends dived into the ferns, and before long he himself was a prisoner with his hands up. Their game featured bandits, noble dons and the rebel Arata; Anton tried to make even a cruel hero good. The arrows were real. When the boys boasted that they played William Tell, Anka asked for a demonstration. There was no apple, so she rolled Pashka's red pirate headband into a tall cap. He put it on and kept smiling. Anton counted thirty paces. Now he would be shooting at something other than a tree above his friend's head.

The crossbow suddenly felt heavy. Anton remembered how long they had once spent failing to hit a small, stationary target with snowballs. He felt sure the arrow would strike Pashka in the face however he aimed. Anka stood beside Pashka; her expression was no longer cheerful. Anton raised the weapon higher, then higher still, and fired. He never saw where the arrow went. Loudly announcing that he had missed, he walked towards his friends on stiff legs. Pashka unfolded the headband, tied it on again and silently indicated what he thought of such marksmanship. Anka did not even look at Anton.

A rusty no-entry sign hung over the old highway. Pashka studied a vehicle's tracks and wanted to follow them; Anton stubbornly chose the opposite direction, beyond the sign. He wanted an obstacle he would have to force his way through. Anka stayed by the road. They returned at dusk, scarcely speaking. Pashka asked what he had found beyond the prohibition. Anton described a destroyed bridge and a fascist's skeleton chained to a machine gun. Meanwhile Pashka had helped someone repair a vehicle. A day that had begun so well ended in a quarrel none of the three could find the words to mend.

## Chapter 1

Much later, Anton was riding along a night road on another planet under the name Don Rumata of Estor. The much-praised stallion received in payment of a gambling debt could barely move. Mosquitoes tormented him, the marshes gave off a stale, metallic smell, and a distant fire glowed. Arkanar was a country of ruined villages, royal decrees and gallows; the villages' ugly names had recently been replaced with pleasant ones, without changing life there. A local nobleman could take considerable liberties. The man from Earth behind this distinguished name possessed still greater power—and had to manage without killing the people among whom he lived.

A short townsman emerged from the bushes and asked permission to walk beside his stirrup. His name was Kiun; he was fleeing Arkanar. Rumata first decided simply to trust someone seeking protection, but habitual suspicion prevailed. He spoke in the language of the man's persecutors, threatened him with loyalty to Minister Reba, then with the roadside gallows. Kiun trembled and said nothing. Something splashed into the ditch. Driven to despair, he finally called Rumata a traitor and told him to hang him. Rumata released his shoulder and said he had been joking. For Kiun, it was a familiar joke: everybody here joked in the same way.

Kiun retrieved a bundle from the ditch. It contained one book—his own. He had recognised Rumata by his gold circlet and had initially been delighted to meet him. Their conversation now resumed awkwardly. Kiun described a boy from the Patriotic School who dreamed of becoming an informer: his drinks at the tavern would be paid for, strong men would fear him, and women would be defenceless against his insolence. Kiun had tried to explain how informers sometimes ended up, but the boy did not believe him: they had not covered that at school. When Kiun wrote down their conversation for his book, the boy panicked, thinking he was being informed on himself.

The gray patrol's horses stood outside an inn. Rumata led his companion past, loudly playing the devoted defender of order for whom a commoner's place was to keep quiet and lick his master's boots. Storm troopers pursued them, demanded travel papers and immediately faltered on recognising the don. Rumata wanted at least a fight, some release for his accumulated hatred, but his opponents were already apologising. One galloped away; the other retreated. When Rumata called Kiun, there was no answer: the fugitive was making his way across a field towards the Irukan border. The rescued man left without knowing whether he had met a friend or another lord with dangerous whims.

Rumata rode on, remembering Arkanar's shopkeepers over their beer. They approved of exposed plots and executions, praising the king, Reba and the gray companies. It suited them to consider literacy the source of unrest. Meanwhile people who could heal, teach, investigate nature and create things that had never existed before were leaving along the roads. Rumata rescued some, but arrived too late far too often. That evening he felt especially clearly how far apart these two lives were: comfortable conversations about peace on one side, frightened fugitives driven out of the country by that peace on the other.

Father Kabani's hut stood deep in the Hiccupping Forest. Superstitious neighbours considered it a terrifying place; in fact it smelled of stale alcohol, contained boiled turnip and housed a drunken inventor. Kabani had devised barbed wire to keep out wolves; it had been used to fence the mines. His meat grinder had reached the torturers. Now he was trying to drink all his own burning water before it too could be put to an evil use. He raged against his gift, insisting that an inventor pulled things from an unknown box without understanding their real purpose. Rumata knew how Kabani had begged Reba to return his invention. It had already been too late.

When Kabani fell asleep, Rumata smashed his distilling apparatus and reached a far corner of the storeroom. Beneath the rubbish stood Midas, a field synthesizer from Earth. Sawdust went into its intake; gold coins bearing the king's profile fell into a bucket. Rumata then put his host to bed, removed his shoes, covered him with a hide, swept the floor and emptied the alcohol he found. It was his sixth year among such juxtapositions: Earth technology, rubbish, other people's suffering and aristocratic lace. Sometimes it felt like a play in which all he had to do was perform his role correctly. But no audience waited beyond the walls, ready to release him home after a successful scene.

A familiar hum announced a helicopter. Don Condor entered the hut—a senior dignitary from neighbouring Soan, but to Anton, Alexander Vasilyevich, his older colleague at the Institute of Experimental History. Rumata wanted to embrace him; instead his body first performed a courtly bow. Condor was in a hurry and asked about Doctor Budakh. The physician was supposed to travel from Irukan under the protection of a man Don Gug trusted. Only a bag of books and the password had reached Rumata. Budakh himself had vanished between the border and their meeting place. Rumata promised to find him if he was alive; what he wanted to discuss was the situation throughout Arkanar.

Rumata argued that the persecution had gone beyond ordinary medieval cruelty. Anything rising even slightly above the general level was threatened: knowledge, doubt, unfamiliar speech. He called the gray terror fascist practice and complained that their task of bloodless influence was becoming justified inaction. Condor believed his facts but heard no constructive way out. Two hundred and fifty Earth visitors were working on the planet; all found restraint difficult. The first observers had been forbidden to interfere at all, even to save a person. Assistance was now possible, but it did not confer the right to satisfy one's anger in the name of helping humanity.

Alexander Vasilyevich spoke without his earlier official severity. He too had experienced helplessness and shame. What he found most frightening was gradually becoming a local dignitary, losing the memory of Earth and a humane attitude towards those around him. Rumata also remembered colleagues who had broken down: one intervened for women being tortured and died; another led peasants but was mortally wounded while trying to stop looting; a third staged a coup and imposed happiness until an emergency team took him away. These stories brought no comfort. They made it harder to imagine a simple solution after which there would be nothing left but joy for those saved.

At last Don Gug appeared—a rosy courtier in a wig, concealing Pashka. Patrols and fights had delayed him; Condor had simply flown, having no time for adventures on the road. The friends returned to the missing physician. Pashka had escorted him to the border; beyond it the trail disappeared. Rumata asked them to leave the search to him and to try to understand what he was saying about the gray threat. Neither childhood friendship nor their shared Earth made that understanding quick. In his sleep, inventor Kabani spoke Reba's name, while the three Earth visitors sat nearby, each inside his own difficult role.

## Chapter 2

Rumata's morning began with shouting beneath his windows: Don Tameo was beating a man who had splashed him with mud. Rumata called Uno. The thin boy brought letters, coffee and chewing bark for cleaning teeth, but answered questions about washing resentfully: prayers seemed more reliable than his master's explanations of microbes. Uno grumbled again while pouring water for his morning wash. Other respectable people kept to familiar arrangements, while this gentleman needed two tubs, a clean towel and strange underwear. Rumata could not simply dress without a servant either: that too would have looked suspicious for someone of his standing.

One letter came from Dona Okana, Reba's new favourite. She invited him that evening and explained quite unambiguously what she expected. Rumata blushed even in front of the boy. Going repelled him; declining seemed foolish, since she might know a great deal. Another letter, already opened by someone else, came from the gray lieutenant Ripat, requesting an extension on a debt. A third challenged him over a lady: a copyist had prepared the standard text and left spaces for names. Rumata's reputation for love affairs was maintained partly by his own agents. He had to appear a fashionable rake, although his habits fitted that reputation badly.

Trifles sometimes proved easier. Rumata had once produced a lace handkerchief, and soon the whole guard carried handkerchiefs; some dandies went as far as entire sheets trailing elegantly along the floor. Underwear had enjoyed no such success. Tameo and Sera came to breakfast. Eating and drinking, they discussed restricting commoners' appearances in the streets. Rumata kept up the conversation while despising his guests, then reminded them of their former jokes about Reba. Both immediately became frightened and began making excuses. Once again, friendly table talk revealed how quickly it could become material for somebody else's accusation.

After delivering his drunken companions to the guardroom, Rumata went about his own business. At the Patriotic School he heard classes chanting answers about authority, laws and interrogation. Its director, Father Kin, explained that not every literate person was dangerous, only one who doubted. What they needed was loyalty. Rumata found two scholars, Tarra and Nanin, positions there, paid and threatened to punish any mistreatment. It was modest shelter under the protection of an aristocrat's whim. He had to present the rescue as fulfilling an eccentric paternal bequest, something his listener understood far better than disinterested concern for learning.

Father Gauk's weapons shop sold blades, but Rumata also visited for poetry. Its owner had sad eyes and ink-stained fingers. They discussed the poet Tsuren and listened to new verses; before leaving, Rumata recited his own translation of Hamlet's soliloquy. Gauk lit up and asked who had written it; Rumata claimed the poetry as his own. Then he found Nanin in a tavern and told him about the school position. It emerged that Tarra had suffered a broken rib the night before: he was red-haired and had drunk too much. Rumata gave more money for his care. The protection he had arranged already needed repairs almost as soon as it existed.

The port was crowded with angry people and detained ships. For three days Reba's orders had prevented anyone from going to sea. Grays stood on the quays, mercenaries with blowpipes sat aboard ships, and war galleys intimidated everyone with burning oil. Rumata made his way into a den and entered a small room where a shrivelled old man worked at a tall desk. Gang leaders waited motionless along the walls. Here etiquette was different: an arriving nobleman could be ignored. Old Vaga the Wheel commanded a night army and connections no royal decree could replace.

Vaga counted his income, then explained the new opportunities to his men. The king was exterminating scholars: they could receive money from the authorities for some heads, and payment from fugitives for saving others. Later those heads could be sold too, if required. He spoke affectionately, called his listeners children, then suddenly wept over his old age. A bow to Rumata followed: the noble guest should state his business and leave. Rumata asked about the missing Irukan don who had accompanied Budakh. Vaga replied that he knew nothing. With everyone assembled, Rumata could obtain no more.

Rumata left gold for future information. At the door he added, as if remembering a vow, that he planned to found a university and would like first choice of captured bookmen before Reba. Vaga warned that rare merchandise was expensive. Rumata answered with aristocratic hauteur, the proper way to discuss a pious obligation. Again he was buying living people a chance in language that turned them into booty. Vaga remained useful precisely because his interests were different from those of the people being saved. Once outside the den, Rumata still had to discover that he had been robbed there.

## Chapter 3

Without a purse, a dagger or the gold ornaments from one trouser leg, Rumata stopped outside a tavern. Two grays watched and grinned. His hand clenched of its own accord; his opponents hurried away. Only afterwards did his own outburst frighten him: a little more, and he might have cut them down for laughing. There had been no order, plan or need to protect anyone. It felt as though the nobleman inside him was acting before the man from Earth. He blamed nerves and exhaustion, but remembered how easily he had recently sliced through an armoured dummy for a wager. The same strength would work on a living body.

An excited crowd was dispersing in the Street of Abundant Gratitude. Some praised the attack, others had felt sick; anyone who voiced doubts was hushed. Rumata wanted to turn aside but adjusted his hair to expose the stone in his circlet. It was the transmitter lens: Earth needed to see what its observer saw. Gauk's shop had been wrecked. The grays at its entrance said they had caught a concealed bookman, and for a moment Rumata felt relief: a prisoner could still be bought out or rescued. Then he noticed the owner hanging from the gateway opposite. The familiar man's thin, ink-stained fingers seemed all that remained of him.

Leaving the shop, Rumata imagined destroying Reba and his fortress, followed by a country where the power released would fall to bandits, invaders and saviours fighting one another. His imagination offered both revenge and its possible cost. He could not see how to save everyone and hated his own ability to list reasons why it was impossible. At home, gloomy Uno greeted him: a girl was waiting in the study, and the boy had not decided whether she was a maid or a dona. Rumata knew at once who had come. For the first time that day he felt relief. He ordered that no one be admitted, not even Reba himself.

Kira sat in an armchair, leafing through a book. She was eighteen; her father was an assistant clerk, her brother a gray sergeant. A quiet, red-haired girl was not greatly valued in Arkanar. For Rumata, she was someone beside whom loneliness disappeared. Each noticed the other's exhaustion, and each first wanted to know what had happened to the other. Kira asked him to take her away. Rumata promised a distant country and people among whom she would be happy. She did not believe in a place where nobody cried, but believing him was easy. Before their intimacy, he removed his circlet and covered it with a book.

During their cold meal Uno lit the lamps although daylight remained: they had a guest. Yet he refused to buy new sheets, considering the old ones perfectly adequate. Kira heard love for his master in the grumbling and suggested taking the boy with them. Uno contemptuously rejected travelling to some incomprehensible paradise for nobles. They discussed Pampa, whom Rumata often mentioned. He called the baron a good man and a comrade; to Kira even using that word about a baron seemed strange. She wanted to meet him, and Rumata had to admit that his friend was wonderful, but still a baron. Class habits survived even alongside tenderness at their little table.

At last Kira explained why she would not go home. Her father copied bloodstained torture records and drank to live with them. Her brother returned drunk with blood on his hands, questioned his father about being literate, and had now dragged a man home and beaten him with his friends. Kira asked to stay even as an unpaid servant. Rumata stroked her hair, then began describing Earth: gardens, cities, friends and astonishing things. She listened, pressing closer when a patrol passed outside. To her this country was a fairy tale; to him it was home, something he was trying to help another person feel.

Once Kira was asleep, Rumata announced the new housekeeper to his servants and ordered them to obey her as they did him. Then he stood alone at a window in the dark house. He thought of Reba, whose unremarkable appearance explained neither his extraordinary power nor the succession of failures he had survived. Rumata had watched him for years and still did not understand whom he was dealing with. He remembered Okana's invitation. Changing clothes, he left the transmitter in a drawer, tucked a white feather—the sign of passionate love—behind his ear and went out. Even at the door he thought of what the visit might mean for her if Reba learned of it.

## Chapter 4

Guardsmen, ladies, old dandies and courtiers had gathered at Okana's. Some ate, others played cards or repeated familiar witticisms. Ripat stood beside Tameo; the nurse Reba had assigned was drunk. Everyone immediately noticed Rumata's white feather. They took him for an audacious lover who dared compete with the minister. Okana appeared, accepted his greetings and announced that she would show her guest the new carpets. The company willingly upheld this transparent convention. Rumata followed her through palace rooms, feeling shame and revulsion but persuading himself that the information was worth such humiliation. He knew Reba talked in his sleep and took her to interrogations.

Close to Okana, his plan no longer seemed possible. She hurried him, kissed him, mistook awkwardness for inexperience and tried to undress him. He could not make himself continue. Everything that had seemed a lesser evil than bloodshed had become unbearable. Rumata held her hands, loudly praised her carpets and announced his departure. At first she failed to understand, then grew furious and insulted him. He escaped through a window into the garden, tore off the feather and gulped the air. He had obtained no information. Again he had to ask whether the work could demand such a transformation, and what it meant to be a bad historian if this was the only way to become a good one.

Back home Rumata fell asleep, but noise woke him: Uno was trying to stop a visitor and hanging from his trouser leg. The visitor was the enormous Baron Pampa, a dearly loved friend. He embraced Rumata and announced that he no longer drank wine. The unbelievable had happened: Pampa had quarrelled with the baroness. Neighbours came to the castle supposedly to hunt, got drunk, wrecked furniture and vanished, leaving their host to answer to his wife. Now the baron meant to punish her through his own sobriety and would under no circumstances go home. He ordered beer to replace lost fluid. After a while he acknowledged that Rumata's cellars were too good and suggested a tavern instead.

On the way Pampa talked of hunting and his son, howled and banged on other people's shutters, then grew sad outside the Gray Joy. His wife was alone; every woman here seemed insignificant beside her. Rumata suggested leaving, but the baron decided to stay and badly wanted his friend not to abandon him. Inside, he drank his first large dipper of wine and declared everything lost: all that remained was to enjoy himself. Penniless dons gravitated to the table. Food and drink multiplied, while Pampa protested against the grays whom a nobleman had to tolerate in the capital. He spoke loudly enough to ensure the officers at the neighbouring table heard.

Pampa boasted of recently driving storm troopers out of his own Golden Horseshoe inn. Someone answered by throwing a knife into his breastplate. Delighted, the baron drew his two-handed sword, immediately severing a ceiling beam. The grays surrounded him but feared approaching the whirling blade. Rumata disabled one and tried to send the others away. Pampa wanted a proper fight and would not yield. Rumata had to embrace him around the waist and remind him that his ancestral sword was intended for noble combat and should not be drawn in taverns. The baron pondered this worthy argument; meanwhile the grays had an opportunity to leave.

Once they could sit down again, Rumata returned to the story of the inn. The grays had apparently held three prisoners: a noble don, an elderly bookman and a servant. Could these be the men he was seeking? Rumata asked what had become of those freed. Pampa was genuinely astonished: he had freed nobody. They were under arrest, a matter of state; his quarrel with the grays did not change that. Rumata had to acknowledge his mistake. His friend could risk his life and rout a detachment, yet never think to interfere with someone else's arrest. The baron's cheerful courage did not mean they understood the surrounding events in the same way.

Ripat appeared at the door and called Rumata outside. He reported that he had arrested Okana on Reba's orders; she had subsequently died under torture by fire. The charge was espionage. Ripat began explaining his helplessness, but Rumata cut him short. Even before the visit he had understood that he might destroy her. His fear had now become news, and he had obtained no information with which to justify himself. Returning to Pampa, he demanded strong wine and ordered more. The baron went on eating. Rumata decided to join his merrymaking, already knowing that something irreversible had happened beyond their table.

After speaking to Ripat, Rumata demanded strong wine. The night became a succession of taverns and fights. At first Rumata took Earth's remedy against drunkenness and still noted patrol concentrations, mercenaries on the road and a black monastic detachment outside the Patriotic School. He separated fighters, trying to prevent killing. Then he became drunk himself. He remembered people cheering his drawn sword and a table cut apart, while his own fury felt like liberation. At dawn he came to on waste ground, his knuckles grazed, an empty ampoule in his hand. His blades were notched but clean. He could not fully reconstruct what he had done during the gaps in his memory.

Gradually the most shameful thing returned. He had dragged the baron home, where Pampa bedded down on the floor in a tapestry torn from the wall, then burst into Kira's room himself. In her terrified eyes he saw a drunken master against whom she needed protection. That look had driven him out of the house. Now Rumata returned, demanded water, vinegar and clean clothes, and spent a long time washing away the dirt. Uno explained that Kira had come downstairs to ask whether her master had been home; he had assured her it was a dream. The boy had sat with a crossbow all night, guarding the staircase against any drunken behaviour from the baron. Rumata thanked him, but the protective fiction brought no relief.

In the morning Kira helped perfume his collar and cautiously mentioned her frightening dream. Rumata sustained the lie: a new place, a noisy guest. An obstacle seemed to be rising between them, one absent even amid the city's horrors. She sat on the arm of his chair and continued; listening, he understood that he was losing closeness with the only person in Arkanar who truly felt like family. At last he admitted that it had been no dream. Kira neither withdrew nor interrogated him. She felt sorry for him and offered to bring brine. After everything he had imagined, her simple response was nothing like the catastrophe he had expected.

## Chapter 5

Arkanar's court had once maintained scholars and poets because learning was considered a proper ornament. Among flatterers and charlatans lived real researchers and writers. Few remained from that former life. Rumata had taken the astronomer Bagir away; his observatory had burned. He had failed to save the physician Tata despite spending much gold and attempting to rescue the condemned. Afterwards he had staged an ambush at the alchemist Sinda's house, disarmed the arriving grays and sent the scholar to Soan. The poet Tsuren had agreed to leave only after being beaten. His departure, his voice from the departing ship, remained in Rumata's memory alongside those he had failed to reach.

Gur the Writer remained at court. He had written a book about a prince's love for a girl from a hostile people; after talking to Reba, he had burned it himself in the square. Now he supplied patriotic verses on demand. The dignitaries complained chiefly of dull balls, although many had noticed no change at all: previously, they had enjoyed the intervals more than the art. This was the palace Rumata entered that morning. The king was being dressed, surrounded by courtiers. Rumata hoped to obtain Budakh's release through a sudden public intervention before the minister could stop him. He knew hidden archers guarded the royal bedroom and a mistake could cost his life.

Exercising his family's privilege, Rumata fitted a shoe onto the king's right foot and came close to him. The sovereign complained of pain and berated Reba for hanging all the physicians. Even if Tata had been a poisoner, he argued, that had not prevented him from healing. Rumata announced loudly that the Empire's best physician could be there very soon. When asked, he named Budakh: the doctor had been travelling from Irukan but the grays had seized him, and the minister must know where he was. Rumata had said the essential thing and remained alive. It seemed surprise had worked, placing Reba under the inconvenient necessity of answering before witnesses.

But the minister showed no fear. He explained affectionately that the soldiers had been sent to protect the old man from the road's dangers, and that he was detained to investigate a possible Irukan plot. If the sovereign insisted, the physician would appear after dinner. The king approved this caution and looked forward to treatment. Rumata, however, saw an entirely unexpected satisfaction in Reba's face. Before they parted, the minister asked about Rumata's forthcoming night duty with the prince. Once again he had to walk through guards and damp palace corridors without understanding why his apparently successful attack felt so little like a victory. The search had finally produced an open answer—whose meaning remained hidden.

Ripat reported that Reba was talking to Vaga the Wheel in the palace. According to him, the gray officers had interpreted Rumata's morning intervention as support for their side against the minister. Rumata disclaimed such an alliance and hurried to the purple chambers. Behind a curtain he managed to settle close enough to listen. But Vaga and Reba spoke in incomprehensible thieves' jargon. Rumata saw only tense politeness, a knife within the minister's reach and Vaga's hidden guard with a blowpipe. When the conversation ended, the old man headed for the door, Reba reached towards the knife and the guard prepared to fire. The minister stopped. An agreement had been reached; its contents remained inaccessible to the observer.

At the royal dinner Rumata found himself beside Gur. He reminded the writer that he had created something genuinely new in literature, whereas his present verses were bad. Gur feared darkness and the way it made everyone alike; he no longer owned his own work. Rumata told him copies survived and offered his. For an instant the writer wanted to reread the book, then immediately feared the consequences. He had been taught that truth meant whatever benefited the king. Suddenly rising, he shouted another panegyric; the sovereign allowed him to resume eating. Gur rejected Rumata's attempt to persuade him to write a new book: let Kiun write, it was too late for him.

At last the king demanded the promised physician. An elderly man entered in a decorated robe, carrying a bag under his arm and wearing a timid, ingratiating smile. This was not how Rumata had imagined Budakh. But the broken Gur sat beside him, and prison might have changed another scholar just as horribly. The king made the newcomer squat, inspected his teeth, then allowed him to examine the painful leg. The physician mixed several remedies with wine; a pungent smell spread through the hall. Rumata was puzzled: Budakh's treatise recommended quite different treatment for gout. Meanwhile the man beside the king insisted that the mixture was to be swallowed, not rubbed in.

The sovereign demanded that the physician taste it first. He drank, then the king did too. The king turned purple, demanded wine, began cursing and checked his knee: the pain remained. The prospect of taking the mixture for a whole week infuriated him. Courtiers rushed out, overturning their chairs. Rumata hid behind a curtain and laughed; somebody nearby was laughing just as helplessly. Behind the absurd performance remained the physician's difference from the man expected, Reba's pleasure and the approaching night. Rumata had secured the appearance of a man called Budakh. He had yet to discover what he had actually achieved.

## Chapter 6

Before midnight duty Rumata went home. The city was unusually quiet, taverns had closed and grays waited silently with torches at the crossroads. Suspicious people followed him; a crossbow creaked in the dark. At home the servants were already armed, and somebody had tried the door several times. Pampa had gone out carousing again. Rumata looked at Kira, asleep fully dressed, and wondered whether to stay. But an observer was supposed to be where events were being decided. He put on his protective Earth shirt and entrusted Uno with the essential task: whatever happened, keep Kira safe. If he did not return within three days, the boy was to take her to Kabani in the Hiccupping Forest.

The prince was ten. Rumata brought him books and described stars and flying ships; the boy liked Tsuren's songs and disliked Reba. Beside the sleeping child the guards had to perform a ridiculous changing ceremony, while a different night already waited outside. Alone, Rumata watched the dark city and thought of the people for whom he remained there: sick Tarra, Nanin, Gur, Budakh. He tried to see them as brothers rather than dependants of a powerful visitor. His inner argument kept returning to killing Reba. The wish to stop evil immediately collided with the thought of how many more people would then have to be killed.

Lights began moving along the streets, fires appeared and noise rose over the city. Someone hammered at the door. The minister of the court burst in shouting that Budakh had poisoned the king, then rushed to save the prince. Grays followed. Rumata drew his swords, retreated behind a table and repelled the first assault: axes lay on the floor, and several attackers withdrew with dislocated arms. He reached the window, but a spear from the courtyard struck his back. His protective shirt held; the impact knocked him to the floor. Rumata rose, fought with fists and elbows and dragged along men clinging to him, until new opponents threw a net over him and bound his legs.

He stopped resisting to conserve his strength. They beat him, then dragged him through the palace. Through the open bedroom he saw the minister of the court pinned to the wall by a spear, and a bloodstained bed. To him, that answered the question of what was happening: a coup. There was nothing left to feel for the prince but pity. Then Rumata lost consciousness. The observer who had intended to stand at the centre of events and help others understand them had become a prisoner himself, without managing either to grasp what was happening or to protect the child behind the neighbouring door.

## Chapter 7

Consciousness returned through pain and icy water. Rumata lay in a puddle while people around him discussed how difficult the enchanted nobleman had been to subdue. They untied his legs; his arms remained bound. He was taken towards the chancellery rather than the torture tower: apparently they meant to talk before executing him. In a room of bloodied prisoners, grays wanted to take his precious circlet. The man in black escorting him forbade it. A storm trooper threatened him and received a bolt from a concealed loophole. The others hurriedly retreated. The night's victors no longer knew who could command them or where the next order would come from.

At the desk sat Reba, the gray leader Father Tsupik and a friendly fat man, Brother Aba. They affectionately discussed hanging or burning Rumata: someone of noble birth deserved a more distinguished execution. Then they discussed property. Rumata demanded that his hands be freed, sat down and began massaging his swollen hands. Tsupik kept looking towards the curtain as though expecting his own men. Black monks appeared instead. They seized him and carried him away. Reba continued the interrogation as though interrupted by a trifle. Later Aba was removed in the same fashion; a terrible cry behind the curtain broke off. The minister was alone with his prisoner.

Reba announced that Rumata was an impostor: the real nobleman had died five years earlier. He listed rescued scholars, the vanished Kabani and the rebel Arata, snatched from custody. Rumata remembered his helicopter, which had astonished guards and rescued man alike. Then the minister produced coins. The gold was too pure for local hands, yet Rumata received no money from his ancestral estates. Another detail: one hundred and twenty-six duels in five years, without a single killing. An incredible swordsman persistently spared his enemies. Capturing such an opponent required heavy, strong men above all, rather than the finest fencers. Reba had understood and used this.

The exposure stopped, however, where Earth began. The minister did not know who sat before him or where the technology, gold and strange restraint on strength came from. He tried threats of evidence extracted by torture; Rumata answered insolently, watched his fear and sought a way to regain the advantage. There was a disturbance behind the curtain: the man called Budakh at court had broken loose. He shouted that he had been deceived, that it had been poison. They immediately dragged him away. Rumata now demanded the real physician's whereabouts. Reba observed that Rumata himself had never seen him. The earlier scene at dinner acquired a new, still partly obscure meaning.

They threatened each other without courtly pretence now. Rumata prepared to leap; the minister understood that even a mortally wounded opponent might reach him. At last Reba admitted that Budakh was alive in the Merry Tower. Then he pulled aside a tapestry and revealed the square. The Holy Order's black cavalry stood in precise formation. According to the minister, it had landed during the night and suppressed the rebellion of the grays and the night army. Arkanar was now a province of the Order. Reba proclaimed himself bishop and governor. His former allies had helped open the way for a force already disposing of them.

Twenty thousand horsemen of the Order did not make Rumata abandon his demand: the physician's release order must be waiting in the morning. Reba still tried to exult, then admitted his fear. The man before him might be a devil, a god's son or a visitor from unknown overseas lands. He wanted such a person close, even offering cooperation. Rumata retained his noble name and right to whims, revealing nothing more about himself. He left beaten and free. The streets held smoking ruins, bodies and black patrols. An iron bracelet issued at the palace had become the necessary pass beyond someone else's spear.

At home Rumata first asked about Kira. She was well, upstairs. Then he noticed Uno's absence. Old Muga calmly said the boy was dead. He lay on a table in the servants' room with his eyes open. Rumata lifted the sheet's edge and immediately lowered it: help from Earth could do nothing now. In fury he seized a praying monk, but Muga explained that the grays had attacked. Threatening fire, they had forced the door open, beaten the servants and climbed upstairs. Uno had fired two crossbows to defend the rooms. They brought him down with knives and were finishing him off when the arriving blacks stopped them. For the boy himself, they had come too late.

Rumata sat beside the body and cried. Then he kissed Uno's forehead and went upstairs to Kira. She removed his torn clothes and protective shirt, bathed his bruises with vinegar and wept over his battered body. He muttered in Russian without noticing the language. When Earth's medicine restored his strength, he prepared to leave again. Kira begged him to stay. When he assured her that powerful people were helping them, she asked where those people had been when Uno was killed. All he could say was that they had been late but were protecting them again now. She saw how quickly he had recovered and still could not stop being afraid.

Rumata explained that he still had to discover who was alive and save those who could be saved. Kira listened, then unexpectedly admitted that sometimes she could not understand why he never beat her. He froze. The very possibility seemed absurd to him; to her his kindness remained something astonishing, almost unearthly. She asked him to tell her the truth about himself someday. He promised. Before leaving he gave her his own bracelet, telling her to wear it on her left wrist and show it if anyone came again. Leaving was harder now: he knew she was waiting for the return of someone she did not fully know.

## Chapter 8

Rumata approached the chancellery through backyards, fences and gardens, catching his lace on nails. Without his bracelet he was stopped by monks, but an inconspicuous man intervened and the patrol released him. Apparently Reba had supplied both spy and bodyguard. The blacksmith Kikus asked what had happened and whether he could go to work. Learning about the Order frightened him, then he hoped to adapt: masters still needed food and craftsmen. He had eight children and owed his employer money. Rumata reminded him of the thousands of equally solitary men, but achieved no sudden transformation of fear into shared resolve.

Tameo emerged from the bushes, also heading towards the new authorities. Drunkenly, he welcomed a renewed Arkanar without insolent grays. Walking through mud and rubbish, he spoke of how freely one could breathe now; he also intended to submit another memorandum against commoners. Even here his own class interests eclipsed everything else. A queue had formed outside the chancellery. One nobleman was sentenced to flogging and kissing a boot for an old joke about Reba; another was declared clean and issued an iron token. Privileges celebrated at court only recently meant nothing before the Order. Sera was already explaining to everyone that a noble don could perfectly well accept a few strokes.

The list even granted Rumata thanks for special services and the right to dispose of Budakh. But the sheet he received was not a release order. It authorised a departmental pass through which he could obtain another instruction. The clerk repeated directions without answering his question about the order itself. Rumata saw another petitioner whose bracelet would not fit; a separate instruction book was already being opened for that contingency. He walked around the desk, took bracelets from the chest, pronounced the required pious greeting and left. Five rings fitted his left arm; the rest went on the right. He held the sheet with its seals in plain sight.

With that very paper he went straight to prison. Below, monks bustled and moved prisoners; outside the torture chambers Patriotic School pupils discussed their work and examinations. Rumata forced himself to look: Earth's historians needed to see what was happening. The students noticed the nobleman turning pale, but the many bracelets and impressive document commanded respect. He found some monks, demanded the warder and declared that the bishop had given him Budakh. One monk appointed himself warder, examined the seals and sent for the physician. The paper went into the file. Rumata never followed the prescribed chain of offices.

They brought the old man on a rope. Rumata removed the noose from his neck, established that he was Budakh of Irukan and led him away. The physician could barely stand. Given an Earth tablet, he habitually examined and smelled it, then, feeling its effect, began guessing what extraordinary substance it was made from. Even after prison he retained a researcher's curiosity. They were climbing towards the exit when a familiar roar came from below. Pampa was there. With remorse, Rumata realised he had completely forgotten his friend. He put two bracelets on Budakh and sent him upstairs to wait, then ran back.

The baron hung upside down, chained to the wall, filling the dungeon with curses. Rumata struck the executioner with his sword hilt, cut apart the clerk's table and tore the chains holding Pampa's legs out of the wall. Freeing his hands, the baron rejoiced: at last he had found Rumata. All night, it emerged, he had been searching for his arrested friend, fighting his way through the city and expecting to find him in prison. Rumata watched him affectionately. Pampa's first demand was beer; he found a barrel beneath the executioner's basin and emptied it. Then he asked whether going naked was appropriate. The executioner's apron would do for returning to the baroness.

The corridor cleared before them. In the square Pampa demanded a horse, received a sword from Rumata and prepared to go home: the Order had already occupied his castle. Even in haste he asked whether his friend would be safe and what to tell his wife. Then he galloped through the approaching monks. Reba appeared beside Rumata and reproached him for taking liberties. Rumata replied that helping a noble don in trouble was quite natural. Horsemen rode after Pampa. Rumata hinted that the minister would now need a replacement for the commander appointed to the baron's castle. Budakh moved towards Reba in such a way that Rumata hurried between them and took the physician home.

By dinner Budakh was washed, shaved and dressed afresh. He explained his outburst in the square: Reba had demanded a delayed-action poison. Neither threats nor promised torture could have made the physician agree. Then children were brought in, and the minister announced that he would test every remedy in the medical bag on them, one after another. Budakh complied. Rumata understood why his intervention had pleased Reba so much. The king would never have accepted a suspicious substance from his minister; instead it was administered by a charlatan passed off as the famous physician whom Rumata had publicly demanded. Trying to rescue someone, the Earth visitor had unwittingly assisted the substitution and prepared a charge against himself.

Budakh was hungry but declined meat, eating salads and jam pastries. After the prison corridors Rumata could scarcely eat. Outside, the city was adapting: old images were being struck down, beer was sold again and carts of bodies went past. The physician reflected on people's astonishing capacity to endure torment. Without it, good people might have died out long ago; with it, they grew accustomed to tolerating more and more. Rumata asked why scholars capable of acquiring knowledge were so passive before evil. Budakh objected that different people called different things evil, and he could not imagine improving the general lot without worsening someone else's.

Rumata said the world changed. Budakh, by contrast, found near-perfect order in society's pyramid: workers below, then nobles, clergy and king. Even prison had not destroyed his belief in a heavenly arrangement. Rumata proposed an imaginary conversation: what advice should one give the almighty if he wished to make people happy? Kira listened especially closely. Budakh began with the simplest thing—enough food, clothing and shelter for everyone. Rumata answered for their imagined god: the strong would take the gifts from the weak. The physician asked for cruel rulers to be enlightened; his companion objected that others, still cruel, would replace them.

Budakh suggested punishing the cruel. Rumata continued the argument: new strong people would fill the vacancies, until everyone had to be punished. Then let everyone receive what they need and prevent anyone from taking it, the physician decided. But Rumata feared that people given everything without work would become their benefactor's dependent pets. So things should be given little by little. Gradually, he answered, people would obtain what they needed themselves. Budakh lost his confidence. Two people at the table wanted to reduce suffering, but none of their simple wishes survived the other's objections. Kira followed the conversation after a rescue that had itself barely succeeded.

The last proposal was to change desires: make work and knowledge the central human aspirations. Rumata thought of Earth's means of mass influence, which might allow them to attempt it. Aloud, he asked whether such remaking would replace one humanity with another, depriving it of its own history. Budakh thought. At last he advised either creating them afresh or leaving them to follow their own road. Rumata replied that pity would not let him leave. The carts creaked outside again. Looking at Kira, he saw fear and hope in her eyes. They had reached no settled solution at the table.

## Chapter 9

Budakh was put to bed before the journey. The medicine was wearing off in Rumata himself; his bruises hurt again. He wanted sleep, to contact Condor, report to the Base and understand what the Earth visitors could still do. But a black monk was already waiting in his study. Under the hood was Arata the Hunchback. He had arrived with the Order's men: among their maimed officers he did not stand out. His own army was in the usual trouble, he said. The men had scattered to divide land and refused to fight on, while the duke gathered strength. Arata intended to raise them again, then lead Arkanar's people too once life under the Order drove them to revolt.

Once he had been called the Handsome. The brand, lost eye and hump were traces of crushed rebellions and betrayals. His wrist ring remained from a galley chain: young Arata had seized a ship and tried to establish freedom at sea, but freedom alone had not turned the liberated people into what he hoped. Rumata did not feel his usual pity towards him. He respected him and, in his own troubled dreams, imagined himself like this fighter, who had survived torture and retained the right to act. Arata knew defeats by former friends' names and by the marks on his own body. He discussed helplessness without bookish composure.

Rumata had once rescued Arata by helicopter and tried to explain where he came from, even pointing out the distant Sun in the sky. The rebel had understood something else: the priests were right after all, powerful gods lived above the heavens. Now he wanted their lightning. Gold helped, and ordinary weapons too, but heavenly power could destroy fortresses. Rumata evaded the conversation, asking how his guest had entered the house. Arata would not let him retreat: he had not summoned the visitor or prayed to him; since he had come unasked, he should explain his refusal to help. To him, the explanation about another planet had never changed the central fact—Rumata possessed inaccessible power.

Asked what he would do with the lightning, Arata answered directly. He intended to destroy the nobles with their descendants, burn their armies and everyone supporting them, and obliterate their fortresses. Once only freed slaves remained and peace arrived, he would return the weapons. Rumata refused. Aloud, he offered only one argument, a lesser one in his own estimation: Arata was mortal, and the power might pass to people with different hands and intentions. They fell silent. Rumata set bread and wine before his guest. He considered his refusal right, yet felt humiliated rather than satisfied before the man to whom he could not give what he demanded.

Arata told him to leave. Previously he had relied only on himself, fighting every battle as his last. Now he preserved himself for a future decisive battle in which Rumata would finally intervene. Hope of another's strength had weakened his own will. So the visitor should either help properly—with weapons, a flying machine or his own swords at the rebels' head—or return to heaven. Rumata wanted to believe he harmed nobody. Arata described harm already done inside himself. Dismissing that testimony was harder than explaining yet again that he was no god.

Privately Rumata foresaw another disaster. Even victorious, Arata would start giving land to his followers; they would need serfs, and new barons would arise from yesterday's liberators. This was his inner historical forecast, not an answer his guest heard. Meanwhile Arata spoke of Vaga, with whom he had once started out. Vaga chose his own power, helped for profit and then his men betrayed Arata to Reba. Now, the rebel reported, he had caught up with his old companion at the port. In his cause he recognised no half-friendship. Pulling up his hood, Arata merely asked whether the gold was in its usual place and left. Rumata was again left wondering about the secret entrance into his own house.

## Chapter 10

The Drunken Den had been cleaned. Sober Kabani sat with washed hands while the three Earth visitors waited for Budakh to fall asleep. They had secretly added a sedative to his drink; the old man resisted drowsiness until he collapsed into Rumata's arms. Kabani was envious: the physician could get drunk, while he could not. Then they switched to Russian. Furiously, Rumata stated the outcome: they had failed to recognise the Order's intrigue, Arkanar had become a stronghold of reaction and their previous work might be destroyed. His forecast for Reba was bleak: in his view the minister would not last long either, but the Order would remain. This was the judgment of a shaken participant still to grasp the full extent of defeat.

Unexpectedly Condor said Reba should have been physically removed: killed, abducted, deposed, imprisoned—Rumata should have acted rather than consulting colleagues who understood nothing. Rumata was astonished, reminding him that he had not understood either. The older man replied that at least he had sensed it. Now Pashka could not believe the conversation. Were they seriously discussing murder, he asked, and did they realise where it might lead? Condor spoke of extraordinary measures. Rumata considered removing Reba pointless now: the new master was stronger than the minister, while at least the minister feared him. The argument left the contradiction between their former restraint and present desperation unresolved.

Condor proposed blockading Arkanar. Rumata would not answer immediately: they needed to look around, consult the Base and meet again. They carried Budakh to the helicopter. Pashka remained at the table, warning Anton as he gathered his belongings. Rumata blamed everyone's exhaustion and irritation. He asked Kabani to take the horses to Pampa, whom he expected to visit soon. Then Condor flew him home. Budakh slept behind them; the cabin smelled of familiar Earth materials, and Condor's confident handling even aroused envy in the exhausted Rumata. The rescued physician was being taken further away, while Rumata himself still had to return to the city.

During the flight Condor cautiously raised Kira's name. Everything dear to an observer should be far away or hidden inside him, beyond seizure and use as a hostage. Rumata understood the warning and promised to think of something. Later he and Kira lay in the dark holding hands. He dozed and unwittingly squeezed her fingers, yet asked her to continue: he badly needed sleep but wanted her company still more. Kira passed on family news. Her father had been dismissed and beaten. Her brother had already sworn loyalty to the Order, acquired a new position and threatened his sister for being involved with a nobleman. She could not go home.

Tidying the rooms, Kira had found Gur's novel about the prince and a girl from beyond the mountains. It seemed to be about them, and she had cried. Rumata promised they would not be separated while imagining their journey to Earth: he would pilot the ship and explain everything so she would not be frightened. Drowsiness immediately offered an absurd future scene in which Anka urged Kira off an immense height with an Earth device at her waist. These were still thoughts and a dream, no established route to safety. Waking at her frightened voice, Rumata began describing a nearer refuge—Pampa's strong, distant castle and his affectionate wife.

Horsemen stopped outside. Someone struck the door and demanded entry in God's name. Kira thought they had come for her. Rumata broke the window and shouted down. They were taken aback: the master was not expected before morning. They argued about how to tie him up and recalled that he did not kill. Rumata threatened them, hurried into his protective shirt and told Kira to dress: he would drive the attackers away and they would go to Pampa. She remained at the window. Below, they were already breaking the door. He bent for his second sword, then straightened to see Kira clutching the curtain and sinking to the floor.

One crossbow bolt had pierced her throat; another stood in her chest. Rumata carried her to bed and called her name. She gave a sob, stretched out and never answered again. He stood beside her. Then he picked up his swords, slowly descended and waited for the door to fall.

## Epilogue

On Earth Anka listened to Pashka and waited for more. He looked away, reached for wild strawberries and spoke haltingly. Anton had left the transmitter at home. When the house caught fire, those aboard the patrol airship realised there was trouble and headed into the city, releasing sleeping gas as a precaution. At first they did not know where to search, then they saw the traces of his passage. They found Anton in the palace. People lay around him: some asleep, some not. Reba was there too. Anton was taken to the Base. Pashka could not reconstruct his actions in detail; his friend said nothing and had become generally quiet. Asked about the girl, Pashka said he did not know.

Anka feared she had come for nothing and Anton was somewhere waiting for her to leave. Pashka assured her that was unlike him: he was simply walking and did not yet know about his visitor. They remembered the childhood highway with its no-entry sign, which Anton had called anisotropic. Pashka sensed a connection between that road, where going back was forbidden, and his friend's story. But he could not explain it and honestly admitted it was only an impression. Anka advised talking to Anton more, even arguing over nonsense. Pashka had tried; Anton smiled and went walking again. All he could do was sit and wait for his return.

Anka stood up. Anton was crossing the clearing—large, broad-shouldered, his face untanned. Outwardly he had changed little. He greeted her affectionately and held out his hands. She went towards him, reached for him and suddenly recoiled from the red on his fingers. It was not blood. Anton had been gathering wild strawberries; his hands were stained with berry juice.
