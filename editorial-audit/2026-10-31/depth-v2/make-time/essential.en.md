## Make Time

Jake Knapp and John Zeratsky

A daily Highlight, attention for it, energy, and observation of what happened.

## A day between two streams

By evening you can have answered dozens of emails, attended every meeting, relaxed in front of a screen, and still wonder where the day went. Jake Knapp and John Zeratsky (JZ) begin with that bewilderment. There are people and activities you want to wake up for, yet everyday life keeps postponing them. The authors want to give them an actual place: writing part of a novel, playing with a child, making dinner, learning something interesting. A more productive day does not automatically provide that place.

They name two forces competing for attention. The Busy Bandwagon demands filled calendars, quick replies, and constant progress through task lists. Infinity Pools offer streams with another video, message, or news item always waiting. After overload, the stream looks like a deserved reward. Together these forces can occupy the entire day. Defaults sustain their power: notifications are already enabled, meetings already have customary lengths, and the screen already faces the sofa. The choice was made long ago, often by someone else. The authors therefore propose reconsidering the design of a day before demanding still more self-control.

## Four steps within one day

The system connects Highlight, Laser, Energize, and Reflect. First decide what you need time for today. Then protect attention so the reserved minutes actually reach that activity. Energy supports the ability to concentrate rather than slide toward the easiest entertainment. Evening notes feed observations into the next day: which conditions helped, and which need changing. This is a recurring loop, rather than four unrelated collections of advice.

A Highlight can be professional or personal, obligatory or chosen for pleasure. Laser means becoming absorbed in it, rather than sustaining maximum tension all day. Bodily care serves work, conversation, play, and rest. Reflection distinguishes an author's recommendation from your own useful experience. The book contains 87 tactics, but nobody is asked to perform them simultaneously. The authors suggest beginning with one tactic each for Highlight, Laser, and energy, then considering what happened in the evening. A useful tactic can be repeated. An unsuccessful one does not force you to restart the entire system: tomorrow offers another opportunity to change a setting.

## The scale of a Highlight

Between intending to write a book someday and being asked to answer an email immediately lies work of an intermediate size: drafting part of a chapter, for instance. This is the scale that interests the authors. A distant goal can guide a life but be too remote to supply daily satisfaction. Small tasks are necessary yet can consume all attention without leaving room for meaningful progress. A Highlight makes a priority visible within today.

It does not have to be your only activity. Making dinner for guests qualifies, although it includes shopping and several cooking stages. Completing a presentation might include conclusions, slide design, and rehearsal. The point is to understand the whole you want to accomplish, rather than choose the easiest item on a general list. The authors suggest looking ahead to the evening: which activity would you want to call the day's main event? That question changes the selection criterion. Responding to whatever arrives gives way to deciding what will receive your attention. A decision alone is insufficient, however: it must subsequently find hours in the calendar and room among distracting objects.

## Urgency, satisfaction, and joy

Three different questions help select a Highlight. For urgency, ask which significant task really cannot wait today. It might be a proposal for an expectant client or helping a daughter with a project due tomorrow. Urgency here does not mean the loudness of the latest notification: the authors seek a substantial but manageable task that meetings and email can easily crowd out.

The second question asks what will bring satisfaction by evening. It points toward long-postponed work without an immediate deadline: a chapter draft, a new project plan, or a family trip. The third asks what will bring joy today. Reading, music, meeting a friend, or playing with a child need no justification through future productivity. The system leaves room for desires that do not resemble career achievements. The authors do not propose calculating the correct answer in a table. They trust intuition, circumstances, and a personal sense of importance. When an activity meets all three criteria, the choice is especially easy; otherwise, no criterion must always defeat the others.

## Time and permission to change your mind

The book's suggested size for a Highlight is roughly 60–90 minutes. The authors consider this long enough for meaningful immersion and limited enough not to require redesigning the entire day. It is their practical guideline, rather than a compulsory duration for every worthwhile activity. What matters is comparing the intention with available time. Otherwise, a short task name can conceal work that cannot fit its assigned window.

The choice is not an oath. Jake once planned to edit a hundred manuscript pages, but plumbing trouble, a headache, and unexpected dinner guests disrupted the day. He changed his Highlight: instead of unsuccessful editing, he would enjoy dinner with friends. His experience of the day changed too. Such revision does not abandon deliberate choice; it makes the choice again under changed conditions. Time not found today need not become a lifelong debt. Tomorrow you can return to the same Highlight, continue its next stage, or choose another activity that now makes more sense.

## Put the Highlight in an actual calendar

The chosen activity needs a start time and duration. The authors suggest deciding how long it will take and when you intend to begin, then entering it in the calendar. This also reveals supporting conditions. If dinner must be ready at seven and cooking takes an hour, you need to begin at six. If groceries are needed on the way, another decision appears: when to leave work. The schedule holds a chain that makes dinner possible rather than an abstract wish to cook.

A new invitation must then be compared with an already occupied interval. You can arrange a meeting before or after it, postpone it, or consciously revise your plan. You cannot simultaneously regard the time as assigned to the Highlight and available for any incoming request. For JZ this discovery grew from Google's arrangements: colleagues could see his calendar and invite him to meetings. While he relied only on a task list, important work often yielded to easy work. A calendar made the day's limits visible and let him reserve space before other people divided the leftovers.

## Stop before complete exhaustion

The Busy Bandwagon always offers one more useful task. Waiting for an empty inbox or fully crossed-off list supplies no stopping signal: the stream can continue indefinitely. The authors propose choosing an ending in advance. In their five-day design sprints, where a team worked on one problem, they began finishing at five after noticing that stopping before exhaustion preserved energy for the rest of the week. This is an observation about their process, rather than a universal finishing time for every shift.

Today's Highlight offers another reference point. If it received time, there is reason to finish with satisfaction even when smaller tasks remain. If a genuinely serious unexpected project displaced it, that also deserves recognition. At his first office job JZ noticed that an uncomplicated task he struggled with in the evening often became easy the next morning. Stopping then preserves future ability to work. The same logic limits morning and night experiments: a found hour is no gain if it ruins the following day.

## Put a small obstacle back

Designers work to remove unnecessary steps between wanting and using a product. In Laser mode, the authors propose restoring some of those steps. Entering a password, finding a put-away phone, or reconnecting to the internet creates a brief pause before an automatic action. During that pause it becomes easier to notice that you intended to work on the Highlight. Protecting attention moves from repeated internal struggle to arranging the environment.

The benefit concerns more than a sum of saved minutes. After switching to messages, you must remember where complex work stopped and become immersed again. An uninterrupted period can therefore offer a different quality of attention from the same minutes cut apart by checks. The authors call this Laser but do not require equally strenuous concentration for every activity. Playing with a child also benefits from full presence. The barrier depends on what interferes with you: logging out may suffice for one person, while another needs to delete an app or close access beforehand. An unsuitable measure can change without abandoning the principle of protected time.

## Logging out and urgent contact

A less radical measure is ending the session after using email or a social service and declining to have the login remembered. JZ strengthens the obstacle with complicated passwords in a password manager: access remains but no longer opens through an unnoticed gesture. The password is not magical; the added need to pause and take a separate action does the work.

The authors also propose sorting notifications rather than blindly disabling everything. Go through the settings, switching off almost all alerts while retaining genuinely useful ones—calendar reminders, for example—and a way to communicate urgency. Their example of that channel is SMS. Ordinary email and other chat alerts are disabled so every message does not gain equal permission to interrupt the day. Decline new apps' notification requests by default. After a few days, assess the difference. This combination matters: fewer random signals alongside a preserved emergency route. It connects with colleagues' expectations later; without it, the advice could easily become a demand to be unavailable regardless of obligations.

## Do not give the morning to the first stream

Sleep interrupts both the work rush and endless consumption for several hours. The authors suggest preserving that pause rather than immediately checking news and messages. Once a stream is opened, other people's questions occupy the mind even when no immediate reply is required. JZ prepares his morning the night before: he closes tabs and logs out so the computer does not greet him after coffee with a ready collection of distractions.

Next identify your particular distraction “Kryptonite.” It need not be the best-known social network. For one person it is startup news; for another, a sailing forum. The sign is habitual return followed by regret over the time spent. The authors match barriers to the strength of the habit: logging out, blocking a site, temporarily disconnecting, or deleting an app. This addresses a specific recurring route rather than a general promise to spend less time online. Removing one especially powerful source can be more useful than declaring war on every application and quickly abandoning an impossible plan.

## Make an appointment for replies

The authors suggest moving email toward the end of the working day and reserving, for example, half an hour or an hour for it. An unavoidable next transition—a meeting or the end of work—creates a stopping boundary. Knowing that messages have a future window makes not checking now easier. The authors even see an advantage in late-day tiredness: less temptation to compose an unnecessarily long reply or accept another commitment.

JZ aims to clear his inbox by the week's end while handling matters needing earlier resolution meanwhile. The image of paper mail helps separate a message's arrival from a conversation beginning in the same room. The general principle is to reply as slowly as consequences permit rather than as quickly as the device allows. Obligations determine that limit. You can explain that important projects occupy you and provide an urgent route—the book uses SMS. Slowing down must not remove access for urgent matters. Less frequent processing and preserved emergency contact belong to one procedure; without them, the advice would become arbitrary disregard for correspondents.

## Expectations, exceptions, and time zones

Explicitly announcing new rules is one option, rather than a compulsory ritual. At Google Ventures colleagues gradually inferred from the authors' behaviour that email replies took time, while urgent contact was possible personally or by SMS. No special announcement was issued. The book explicitly distinguishes sales and customer support: some roles really require fast responses. A writer's comfortable frequency cannot become everyone's professional norm.

Even Jake, who used an access blocker, did not choose his schedule arbitrarily. He identified the latest acceptable first check: ten-thirty still allowed replies to European colleagues before their working day ended. He allocated half an hour, then blocked email until three, preserving concentrated work and time for US correspondents. Software maintained the boundary outside those windows. The example shows the sequence: understand actual consequences of delay, choose windows, then protect them. Another person's schedule is useful as an example of reasoning rather than a set of times everyone should adopt.

## Make the beginning easier

Even with distractions removed, uncertainty can make starting intimidating. The authors offer three different supports. A closed door marks a boundary for others and yourself; without a separate room, headphones can provide a similar signal, even without music. An external deadline adds reality: design sprints schedule user interviews for Friday in advance, so a prototype is needed by Thursday. JZ returned to running by agreeing to an upcoming race rather than waiting until completely ready.

The third support divides the Highlight into small actions containing verbs. Planning a holiday becomes checking dates, considering destinations in a guidebook, talking with the family, and searching for flights. The authors borrow this from David Allen. Small steps here serve the chosen whole rather than compete with it. A clear next action reduces the difficulty of entering; the following step appears, and work gains momentum. All three supports are not required together. Choose according to what actually obstructs the beginning: interruptions, lack of commitment, or uncertainty about the first move.

## When attention drifts again

A protected beginning does not eliminate the wish to stray. Rather than checking immediately, write a sudden question on a separate sheet: it is preserved and can wait. One attentive breath reconnects attention with bodily sensation and the task. If the absence of a stream feels boring, the authors suggest remaining there briefly instead of automatically filling it. Boredom differs from being stuck: when stuck, there is a task but no next move yet.

You can shift to paper, walk around, or simply remain with the unfinished work while keeping it in view. Such an interval is not necessarily useless. Yet the book does not demand pushing through every kind of fatigue indefinitely. If different approaches fail, perhaps a day off is needed; where that is impossible, try a gentler pace, real breaks, or an enjoyable Highlight. This is a meaningful fork. Enduring the discomfort of beginning or searching for a solution differs from treating exhaustion as a character flaw and applying more pressure. The system's next part explains why concentration depends on a person's condition as well as a closed browser.

## Why a calendar needs a body

A free hour and removed apps do not supply the energy for a chosen activity. When exhausted, familiar entertainment is easier than complex work or attentive conversation. The authors describe energy as a battery charge: an everyday image of a condition, rather than a quantity measured by a medical instrument. Replenishment helps keep decisions about time from disappearing at the first drop in energy.

This part includes movement, food, caffeine, quiet, company, and sleep. Its connection with earlier steps is specific: enough rest makes Laser easier, while protected attention helps prevent rest being spent on another stimulating message stream. The authors use personal observations and studies cited in the book, but their advice remains a set of proposals for personal trials. The book's practical question is which everyday conditions help these two people and the reader feel able to give attention to the Highlight. That is what evening notes subsequently observe, rather than measuring success through perfect nutrition or athletic performance.

## A walk built into the journey

The authors try to fit walking into existing travel rather than add another item to an overloaded schedule. Walk part of the commute, leave public transport earlier, or park farther from the entrance. The principle is to find movement where transport is otherwise automatic. JZ began walking when his office moved closer to home. Although the journey took longer, he felt he had gained time to think and prepare his Highlight rather than merely lost minutes in transit.

The book extends this idea through deliberate small inconveniences: cook dinner, use stairs, carry things. The authors even disagree about luggage: JZ sees carrying as an opportunity for movement, while Jake refuses to surrender wheels. Their disagreement shows the list's conditional nature. Not every comfort must disappear; choose changes compatible with everyday life. A dual purpose is especially useful in this system: a journey becomes a walk, while cooking becomes movement and a chance to talk. Suitability remains attached to circumstances rather than inconvenience's supposed moral superiority.

## A break without another stream

Headphones can support concentration, but continual sound also fills intervals when thoughts might wander freely. The authors suggest occasionally leaving them home or going without for an hour. This does not cancel their use as a Highlight cue or a barrier to conversation. One object can have different functions at different times.

A real break is also judged by what it does. After screen work, a fresh feed may preserve the same posture, add anxiety, and fail to restore. The book offers looking out the window, a short walk, food when hungry, or a suitable conversation instead. Logging out beforehand helps here too: encountering a password request reminded JZ that he meant to rest rather than check Twitter. When mentally depleted, Jake sometimes watched an entire film. He chose a finished work without an automatic invitation to another episode. This explains the apparent contradiction with limiting television: a specifically chosen rest is different from a ban on screens under all circumstances.

## A bedroom and a gradual evening

To prevent sleep beginning immediately after the last strong stimulus, the authors suggest changing the bedroom: remove the phone and charger, television, and other distracting devices. For reading, they prefer paper or a reader without distracting apps and bright white illumination. A separate simple alarm clock avoids bringing the smartphone back for waking. This changes the environment once, reducing the need to argue with an available screen every evening.

JZ adds a “fake sunset.” A few hours before intended sleep, light gradually dims; calm lamps replace bright overhead lighting. Screens shift to night mode and are removed before bed. The book suggests an eye mask if external light interferes. For difficult waking it also mentions the reverse tactic: gradual morning illumination. This conveys the authors' transition strategy without promising to resolve every sleep disorder. The important connection is that sustaining stimulation all evening does not let simply closing your eyes automatically finish the day. Preparing the night continues work on tomorrow's energy.

## When caring for another person is central

The authors separately address those caring for a child, partner, parent, or friend. Under these conditions, suggestions to walk, be alone, or sleep peacefully can sound selfish or simply impossible. This is more than another busy week into which habits need fitting. Another person's needs can govern time and consume emotional resources independently of one's calendar.

At classes before their first child's birth, Jake and his wife Holly heard the oxygen-mask comparison: preserve your own ability to help before supporting someone else. In the book it means at least some care for food, available sleep, and brief pauses. It does not turn care into an obstacle to a personal project or promise complete control. Caring for someone close may itself be what matters most. Energy then supports continuing that care rather than escaping it toward a more prestigious Highlight. This boundary returns the system to a person with real constraints: some tactics are unavailable now, and an impossible schedule is no measure of personal failure.

## What to record in the evening

Reflection completes the other steps. At the day's end, the authors suggest noting whether time was found for the Highlight, whether you concentrated on it, how energy felt, which tactics were used, and what happened. Then decide what to keep, change, or try tomorrow. Beside this is a question about what brought pleasure and gratitude today. This is brief feedback rather than a report on conformity to an ideal day.

The authors frame their ideas as hypotheses: one chosen focus may bring satisfaction; barriers may support attention; changed bodily habits may support energy. Tactics become ways of testing those ideas in personal life. Observing one person does not establish a universal effect or separate every possible cause of change. For the book's purpose, however, it helps choose the next move. Without notes, an appealing tip can be repeated while its actual result is forgotten, or a useful one rejected after one awkward day. Notes preserve particulars: what you did, what you noticed, and what you plan to change, rather than a general impression of your own organisation.

## Where small windows led

The conclusion shows two actual changes of direction. Jake began finding writing time in 2010 and made writing his principal work by 2017. Between those dates came evenings with manuscripts, a first completed book, and a growing understanding that he wanted to write more. Leaving Google was not a sudden instruction from the system. The decision developed over seven years as actual activity clarified his priority.

For JZ and his wife Michelle, sailing provided that direction. Preparing boats, learning about engines, electricity, and navigation gradually led toward longer journeys. In 2017 they left their jobs, sold their apartment, moved aboard, and sailed from California toward Mexico and Central America. JZ emphasises enjoyment in the activity itself rather than a title or external reward. The authors do not propose that readers repeat either the writing departure or the voyage. The opposite discovery is possible: your current profession fits your wishes, while favourite activities need separate time alongside it. Priorities can change again. The system serves as a way to notice direction through lived days rather than guarantees one final decision about life.

## Three days to begin

The book ends with a short practical recipe. First put a Highlight in the calendar—tactic 8. Then block one especially powerful distraction—tactic 24. For energy, add a little walking—tactic 62. Make notes in the evening and repeat for three consecutive days. Then consider what you learned and what is worth keeping. This is more definite than a promise to master all 87 tactics someday and considerably smaller than a demand to transform life completely tomorrow.

Each step addresses another's weakness. An intention alone creates no time; a calendar without protected attention can remain a formality; concentration without recovery soon runs down; a good or bad day without reflection says little about what to try next. An empty calendar, identical mornings for both authors, or perfect adherence is not required. They themselves restored phone email while travelling and watched favourite series. The book's final proposal is to test a small change within a real day where duties, desires, other people, and a definite limited amount of time coexist.

## After the third evening

Three days provide initial observations. They inform what comes next: what received time, what helped attention, what changed energy, and which experiment now makes sense.
