# Selfie — Will Storr

## Book Zero. The dying self

### The woman who could not become perfect

Debbie Hampton regains consciousness in hospital and gradually recognises the objects around her. She cannot yet put her own life together. When she understands that she has survived a suicide attempt, her first response is anger at herself. For Will Storr, this begins an investigation: how does someone so determined to live correctly come to hate the fact of being alive?

Debbie describes her parents’ divorce and her promise never to cause her own children the same pain. She marries her school sweetheart, becomes a mother and tries to resemble her mother-in-law, whom she sees as a faultless wife and homemaker. But domestic life does not bring the fulfilment she expected. She grows resentful, the marriage ends and new relationships fail to work. Each setback becomes an accusation against her whole person: she has failed her children, will never earn enough and does not deserve love. There seems to be no route between the actual Debbie and the woman she feels obliged to become.

Storr has encountered similar reasoning in accounts from people with very different lives. Professional failure, a partner’s betrayal or an athlete’s injury destroyed more than plans: it undermined their sense of deserving respect. He recognises that reasoning in himself. His vulnerability is not concealed behind an expert’s detached voice; the book’s question concerns him personally. Yet similar stories do not establish a common cause. The distance between a recognisable experience and an explanation is precisely what he must investigate.

### More than setting a high standard

In Glasgow, Storr meets suicide researcher Rory O’Connor, who immediately complicates the picture. Different combinations of vulnerability and circumstances can lead towards suicide. Mental illness is frequently present, but most people with depression do not die by suicide. Naming a disorder therefore cannot, by itself, explain a particular transition into danger.

O’Connor studies socially prescribed perfectionism: the belief that other people expect flawless performance of one’s roles. Someone need not pursue records to feel like an unworthy father, daughter or provider when they fail to meet an assumed requirement. There is a crucial difference between what others actually demand and what a person imagines they demand. O’Connor has found associations between this thinking and suicidal experiences across social groups, but the reason for the association remains a question. Greater sensitivity to signs of social defeat is his hypothesis.

In the model he discusses, defeat becomes especially dangerous alongside entrapment: failure seems impossible either to repair or to escape. Here he also draws on Roy Baumeister’s account of suicide as escape from the self. Not every disappointment produces this outcome. Storr is interested in the combination of humiliation, the disappearance of an apparent way out and painful self-blame. His interviewee admits his own sensitivity to criticism and fear of letting people down. Scientific knowledge does not place him outside the mechanism he describes.

### An environment where everyone can see failure

Gordon Flett distinguishes several forms of perfectionist thinking. In one, people impose perfection on themselves. In another, the gap between an apparently worthless present self and a desired future self is particularly painful. An error in one activity becomes evidence of total inadequacy. Achievement is expected to repair the person, or hide their unacceptability from others.

Flett does not claim that an increase in perfectionism itself has already been demonstrated. In the book, he explicitly acknowledges the absence of such research. The environment may have changed instead: there are more opportunities to feel defeated, and a public mistake can provoke a faster, larger response. This leads to his interest in perfectionistic self-presentation, the attempt to conceal mistakes and display only a successful version of life.

Meredith, one of Storr’s younger interviewees, remembers friend counts in a messaging service becoming a measure of popularity, followed by a happy Facebook feed that constantly reminded her of her own distress. She saw other people’s joy, could not feel it herself and still posted successful images to keep up. A person making comparisons also becomes a producer of them. Storr retains other explanations: increases in people seeking help may reflect better recognition of problems and a greater willingness to discuss them. His cultural account neither removes these possibilities nor becomes the only theory of suffering.

### Two investigations instead of one piece of advice

The book will follow two paths together. The first concerns the workings of the human self: why we need recognition, control and a story in which we appear worthy. The second concerns culture: where we acquire our image of what a worthy person should be. Wanting approval does not, on its own, explain why slimness, enterprise, openness or fearlessness become particular sources of pride.

For Storr, the contemporary Western ideal combines an almost impossible number of demands: independence and care, success and social usefulness, beauty and naturalness, confidence and likeability. Apparently, all that is required is to uncover oneself, follow a dream and make an effort. But this image has a history. It emerged within economic arrangements, religions, books and arguments involving people who lived long before us.

Storr deliberately follows a selected line through that history rather than promising a complete history of civilisation. He first examines ancient social mechanisms, then traces several transformations of the Western ideal. The purpose is to understand how a public demand can be experienced as one’s own deeply personal truth. Failure to achieve perfection then ceases to be merely a question of insufficient willpower.

## Book One. The tribal self

### John and his new family

John Pridmore tells his story in his mother’s flat in East London. The enormous former criminal sits beside an elderly woman who remembers a very different boy. Storr does not immediately force him into an abstract scheme. First comes a childhood of seaside holidays and a father’s stories about famous gangsters. His policeman father described criminals with such respect that they seemed heroic.

Then his parents asked him to choose which of them he would live with after their divorce. His mother entered a psychiatric hospital; home became anxious and unpredictable. John began stealing, passed through correctional institutions and learned to fight. Later, in prison, another inmate tried to establish authority over him in front of an audience. Giving way would mark him as someone who could be exploited repeatedly. Fighting brought punishment, but in this world it appeared to protect his reputation.

After his release, John becomes a bouncer and grows close to a criminal businessman called Buller. Buller provides money, assignments and a new community. John must wear a black suit to a meeting with a rival; the pub fills with identically dressed men demonstrating their employer’s power. Belonging literally has a uniform. John gains a place among his own people and a chance to rise. His new life promises to restore what he previously lacked: recognition, protection and control over how others treat him.

### Reputation matters more than peace

Former London criminal John Pridmore describes a gang that controlled drug dealing in clubs: he became one of its most dangerous enforcers. Money, cars, women and other people’s respect confirm that he has chosen the right path. Ordinary working people seem foolish to gang members: they spend all day earning what these men acquire much faster. Belonging changes both behaviour and the scale by which a worthwhile life is judged.

The mechanism becomes especially clear outside a club when two visitors refuse to recognise John’s authority in front of a woman he wants to impress. He is not physically afraid of them. Nor does he need to defend his life. But public disrespect seems to threaten his recently established name, and he assaults them severely. Buller, his mentor in the gang, has taught him to build, above all, a reputation as someone who cannot be treated with contempt.

Storr hears an extreme form of an ordinary dependence on others’ judgments. At home, John could be moved by a family television drama, then leave to inflict serious harm. Neither behaviour cancelled the other: different relationships supported different ways of being himself. Explaining the social rewards of violence does not excuse it. The account shows how an act that looks monstrous from outside can acquire an intelligible meaning, and even approval, within a group.

### Alliances, gossip and the ladder of status

To explain this dependence, Storr turns to research on primates and human groups. In Frans de Waal’s accounts, a leading chimpanzee’s position depends on more than strength. It requires allies, protection of weaker animals and the ability to divide opponents. Status is never permanently secure, so coalitions require constant attention. Storr uses these observations as an evolutionary framework for human concern with hierarchy, not as an experimental explanation of John’s biography.

Stories carry reputations. Gossip conveys whom to trust, who has broken a rule and what to fear. It both transmits information and maintains order: a story about wrongdoing provokes indignation that can lead to punishment. Exclusion from a small group once threatened the loss of indispensable support. Modern audiences are larger, but the fear of becoming an object of condemnation remains recognisable.

Storr derives a double task for social humans: getting along and getting ahead. To rise, people must demonstrate qualities useful to their group; to remain in the game at all, they must be accepted. Recognition is therefore often pursued in the language of selflessness. A person wants not simply a reward, but a reputation for deserving it. An internal judgment of one’s behaviour allows this process to operate even without an external spectator.

### Good for whom?

Experiments with young children described in the book find a preference for a helping character over a hindering one, and an early sensitivity to unfair distribution. For Storr, these support an account of ancient foundations for moral approval: groups need assistance and exchange, so they value people who do not abuse them. The findings do not prove that every adult always cares about everyone equally.

John’s story places an obstacle before the explanation. If people want to appear selfless, why did cruelty bring him prestige? Storr’s answer concerns the boundaries of the group. By risking his freedom and health for the gang, John could appear useful and loyal to his own people. Self-sacrifice for some coexisted with violence towards others.

Storr also recalls reporting from Guatemala, where rivalry between school shifts developed into conflict between armed groups. This is a witness’s account, not laboratory proof that the outcome was inevitable. Its purpose is to show the cost of dividing insiders from outsiders in a particular dangerous setting. The general mechanism does not imply that every group is equally cruel. It helps explain why a participant’s moral certainty cannot guarantee the harmlessness of their actions.

### A conversion that changes the story

John’s life increasingly revolves around revenge and public demonstrations of strength. After another serious assault, he sits alone at home, afraid of the consequences. He says he hears a voice listing his wrongdoings, experiences terror of condemnation and turns to God. The religious experience becomes a break with his previous existence.

He visits his mother, goes to confession and recognises himself in the parable of the returning son. Instead of being someone who takes, he wants to become someone who gives. Storr cannot confirm a supernatural origin for this experience. He asks why an available Christian story could organise a frightening event and offer an intelligible next step. That language had surrounded John since childhood, even when he lived against its rules.

Yet conversion does not conclude with a complete change of character. After becoming religious, John still punches a man who spits at his mother. He sees a significant difference: formerly, he would have been unable to stop. For Storr, this is an early indication of the limits of promises of self-reinvention. Belonging, daily actions and the meaning of life can change without the tendency to anger magically disappearing. A new story can redirect someone without making them an entirely different creature.

### The storyteller who does not know everything

Michael Gazzaniga’s experiments give Storr another way of discussing personal stories. In some patients whose brain hemispheres had been surgically disconnected, researchers could supply information to one side without the other side’s language system receiving it. An action or feeling arose while the explaining voice lacked access to its real source. Nevertheless, the person offered a convincing reason.

In one account, a patient felt frightened after a disturbing film had been presented only to her right hemisphere. She explained the feeling through the unpleasant room or the researchers’ behaviour. In another, a command to walk, received without the speech system’s knowledge, was accompanied by an explanation about wanting a drink. The point is not that patients deliberately lied: they believed the reason they supplied.

Storr connects this to research with people who had undergone no such operation. Participants chose the more attractive face from two photographs; sometimes the experimenter secretly substituted the unchosen picture. Many failed to notice and explained the merits of a face they had not selected. The mind can protect the coherence of a story before checking its foundation. This does not make every explanation false. But a feeling of obviousness does not prove access to the causes of one’s own decision. The familiar story, “I knew what I wanted, so I acted,” becomes open to question.

## Book Two. The perfectible self

### Someone else’s story in one’s own body

The next stop begins with Storr himself examining his stomach in shop windows. His weight feels like a visible moral defect: not merely a bodily feature, but evidence of laziness and lack of self-control. Reasonable explanations involving age cannot remove the shame. Classicist Michael Squire points him towards the ancient Greek association of physical beauty with inner worth, kalokagathia.

Storr’s point is not that every contemporary dissatisfaction with appearance descends directly from one ancient idea. He notices that a supposedly private feeling uses a public language of judgment. In another environment, being larger may signify status, while weight loss causes concern. Even an apparently self-evident bodily ideal requires cultural context.

Environmental influence extends beyond beliefs that people can articulate. Sophie Scott describes how her team had to redesign a simple task comparing emotional sounds when working with Himba participants. The laboratory procedure assumed ways of using short-term memory familiar to people with formal schooling. Participants in another culture possessed many skills the researchers lacked, but the task was not transparent to them. Even an attempt to discover universal human characteristics can quietly contain local assumptions.

### What the brain inherits

Discussing childhood development, Storr describes the formation and selection of connections in the brain. Early possibilities gradually adapt to the environment in which a person lives. Losing unused distinctions and strengthening frequently used connections helps explain why culture becomes more than a set of rules one has read. It participates in forming habitual perception.

Nature and nurture are not two independent portions that can be measured out for an individual. Storr’s interviewees discuss their interaction. Genetic research is better at identifying some patterns than at explaining precisely how environments operate, a limitation he preserves. A percentage of heritable variation between people is not a percentage of predetermination in one person’s destiny.

Family, peers, social categories, religion, film and literature offer overlapping instructions about worthy behaviour. They do not turn everyone into the same person. Yet shared expectations become material for constructing an image of one’s future. This returns Storr to control: who decided what counts as successful action before someone resolved to take charge of their life?

### The shores where the individual emerged

In Storr’s historical account, ancient Greece gives the improving individual a distinctive importance. According to Richard Nisbett and historians consulted by the author, poor soil, mountains, islands and maritime trade encouraged varied independent occupations and numerous city-states. Encounters with unfamiliar goods and ideas supported debate. Personal skills and the ability to persuade could matter in winning recognition.

This freedom was far from universal. Storr explicitly limits it to certain men and does not depict the polis as a modern society of equal rights. He is interested in an emerging image of people capable of acting and taking responsibility for the direction of their lives. Argument, athletic competition, political participation and public honours become different forms of that existence.

Aristotle supplies a philosophical language of potential perfection. Humans are political animals with qualities they can develop. Pursuing excellence promises both satisfaction and benefits to the community. Storr sees an important source of Western optimism about self-improvement here. Nisbett’s ecological chain is a historical interpretation, not an experiment isolating one cause across millennia. Its value to the book is to make familiar individualism something that arose historically, rather than the only conceivable arrangement of consciousness.

### How someone acquires a plot

Culture works through more than explicit instructions. Psychologist Dan McAdams describes the development of narrative identity: people connect memories and expectations into a story of who they are. During adolescence, life begins to be experienced as a larger sequence with direction and meaning. The past is selected and rearranged from the perspective of today’s protagonist.

Storr observes that culture already supplies the material. Family stories, religious parables, films and news suggest which trials deserve attention, who the opponent is and what counts as a good resolution. John Pridmore found one such plot in Christianity. Another person may find an available model in an entrepreneur’s or athlete’s story.

The inner account is usually favourable to its narrator. In research discussed by Nicholas Epley, people attributed a stronger interest in meaningful, valuable work to themselves, and greater concern with money and external rewards to others. The protagonist therefore not only acts, but considers their motives more elevated. This connection matters to Storr: the pursuit of perfection draws on stories that help us respect ourselves and explain our place among others, rather than on an objective inventory of qualities.

### Another way of being worthy

To make the Western path appear less universally natural, Storr turns to Confucius and Chinese history. In Nisbett’s account, fertile plains, agriculture, large cooperative undertakings and centralised power created a different pattern of interdependence. A good life primarily required coordinating actions and preserving relationships.

The exemplary Confucian person understands the duties of their position, avoids parading their virtues and cares for harmony. Storr also presents the thinker as a particular, sometimes sharp and exacting man. His teaching did not acquire political power as soon as he expressed it. Followers carried it forward, and it later proved useful to political unification. A cultural idea has carriers, circumstances and a history of adoption.

The distinction is not that some people possess personalities while others do not. Both models address relationships and recognition. But independent distinction and appropriate participation in a whole can carry different weights. Before applying such descriptions to anybody, the book’s initial qualification is essential: these are group tendencies with enormous individual variation. Neither ancestry nor nationality grants access to a particular person’s thoughts.

### The fish and its pond

In studies Storr recounts, American and East Asian participants described underwater scenes differently. Some more often began with a conspicuous fish in the foreground; others began with the water and surrounding objects. Both groups noticed the central object. What differed was the weight given to context. In recognition tasks, changing the background could interfere more with people who initially perceived an object together with its surroundings.

Differences in explanations of human behaviour followed a related pattern. Analysis of crime reporting found more attention to perpetrators’ character in an American publication, and more to circumstances in a Chinese one. Storr sees a useful contrast: the same outcome can be explained through the properties of a separate actor or through the situation in which they acted.

He also describes variation within large cultural areas: Turkish communities with different livelihoods, Chinese rice- and wheat-growing regions, and American honour culture. These resist dividing humanity into two unchanging camps. Ways of life can support different habits of attention and ideals of worth within one country. Together, the materials challenge a familiar accusation: if someone fails, must the explanation lie entirely inside that person?

### Harmony makes demands too

A conversation with Korean psychologist Uichol Kim takes the comparison into stories. Kim describes plots where different accounts remain partial and readers must reconcile perspectives themselves. Storr contrasts these with the familiar Western protagonist who acts, overcomes obstacles and reaches a resolution. He retains exceptions: Asian literature also has heroes, and a cultural distinction is not an absolute law of genre.

The book discovers no simple escape from perfectionism elsewhere in the world. If a worthy self is defined through loyalty to family or an organisation, failing one’s duty can also produce unbearable shame. Storr discusses these pressures with Kim and anthropologist Chikako Ozawa-de Silva, including traditions that connect personal honour to the welfare of a group.

These accounts and interpretations do not explain every tragedy. Their role is narrower: different cultures provide different scenarios of defeat. The individualist hero may suffer from failing to control a destiny, while someone oriented towards harmony may suffer from letting their people down. Dependence on social value remains. As the Greek political world declines, the Western pursuit of perfection changes direction too: the next search turns inwards.

## Book Three. The bad self

### Pluscarden

Storr arrives at Scotland’s Pluscarden Abbey tired and disoriented. Familiar connections to the outside world are absent, and the monks live under the Benedictine rule. The old stone building, services and silent meals allow him to encounter a Christian model of personhood in daily arrangements, rather than only in history books.

At first he feels a familiar guilt. Catholic upbringing no longer determines his convictions, yet the language of sin still works on his emotions. Reading the rule reveals a sharp contrast with Greek glory: the ideal monk is instructed in humility, renunciation of personal desires and a low opinion of himself. Storr connects this ideal to a dependent world in which survival required obedience and service to the powerful.

This is his historical line, not an exhaustive description of Christianity or the Middle Ages. He also notices Greek continuities in the idealised body of the crucified Christ, faith in reason and the pursuit of improvement. The direction of struggle changes. Obstacles are sought not only outside the person: a corrupt inclination within must be watched. Perfection becomes bound to endless work on one’s own soul.

### The person other people watch

A monastic meal reminds Storr that culture also spreads without stories. Monks follow the abbot’s signals, rising and sitting together. He connects this with Joseph Henrich’s account of prestige-based learning: people notice those who seem to have succeeded and adopt their behaviour.

One’s own assessment of skill is only one source of information. Another comes from observing whom others watch, approach and listen to. In a study described in the book, preschoolers more often copied a way of handling a toy that observers had attended to especially closely. Prestige travels through visible deference. An ancient mechanism helps people learn from the skilled, but need not distinguish accurately between areas of expertise.

This also helps Storr explain the oddities of celebrity. A popular person can influence purchases or beliefs unrelated to their original achievements. Attention attracts further attention, and someone becomes exemplary through being famous. An abbot and a television star are not identical; the comparison concerns a route of influence. The ideal self does not exist only in imagination. It acquires the face of someone whom the group already considers worth copying.

### What Father Martin does each day

An interviewee called Robert intends to become a monk and speaks of the old self dying. Storr sees a life with almost no free time and assumes that its routine must suppress the need to act. Brian Little’s research on personal projects emphasises meaningful goals and a sense of progress. Where is the project in repeating the same activities for decades?

Father Martin disrupts this convenient explanation. He tried to enter Pluscarden several times, initially left, served as a parish priest and only much later remained. Family bankruptcy during his youth, anxiety and a search for meaning enter his story, but he describes vocation primarily as a pull that preceded verbal explanation.

Now Martin grows vegetables and sings psalms year after year without considering this empty repetition. His danger is becoming absorbed in his own skill, praise for a meal or superiority in fasting, and forgetting whom it serves. The internal struggle with vanity gives his life direction. Storr realises he mistook someone else’s project for the absence of a project. The monastery retains improvement, comparison and the danger of pride, but defines the reward and protagonist differently. Nor does the search for stability eliminate change: after vowing to remain in one place, Martin was sent to Ghana.

### A new investigation of inner evil

In the Christian strand of Storr’s account, the soul becomes immensely valuable and simultaneously an object of suspicion. People must repair what is wrong in them from the beginning. He hears continuities in secular language about battling inner demons. But he does not claim Greeks lacked morality or Christians ignored earthly success. The emphasis changes, rather than the whole content of life at once.

Sigmund Freud enters the book as another influential interpreter of a hidden inner world. Storr recounts biographical material about Freud’s family and his response to Oedipus Rex. The myth became a means of explaining his own feelings, then a claim about a universal pattern of childhood development. Storr is interested in the danger of moving from oneself to humanity. Biographical reconstruction does not give him direct access to the causes of another person’s theory.

Despite differences between religious and psychoanalytic explanations, the author sees continuity: people must still discover dangerous internal forces and struggle against them. At the threshold of another turn stands Fritz Perls, who visited the admired Freud and received a cold welcome. He would later reject his former teacher. In America, a new psychology would offer the opposite starting belief: human nature is good at its core; the problem is that something prevents its emergence.

## Book Four. The good self

### From labour to the power of belief

Storr places this turn within a changing economic world. Industry, education and social mobility created more opportunities to improve one’s position. Samuel Smiles’s Self-Help promised improvement through work, persistence and independence. People need no longer simply remain humbly where they were born. Yet the new hope preserved an older demand for discipline.

A different tradition developed alongside it. Healers and advocates of mind-cure linked illness to beliefs, and recovery to trust or the strength of faith. Phineas Quimby, Mary Baker Eddy and later preachers of prosperity appear in Storr’s account of this expanding promise. Eventually the same reasoning offered to overcome poverty by changing thought. Failure could mean that someone had not believed enough.

The book neither recommends these methods nor confirms their medical promises. It asks where the cause of events is being located. Attention shifts from the external world to the person’s inner state. If every change originates within, failure can readily become evidence of internal inadequacy. The explanation offers hope of control, while relieving others of the obligation to notice obstacles that conviction alone cannot remove.

### The age of an agreeable personality

The Great Depression and war interrupt a straight line towards greater individualism. In Storr’s account, government measures, unions and a post-war compromise support a period of reduced inequality, the Great Compression. Stable work and education create possibilities for people previously excluded from them.

The new environment also makes demands. People move into cities and suburbs, work in large organisations and live among strangers. Acceptance requires making a good impression. Sociability, optimism, attractiveness and likeability become especially valuable. In the formulation Storr recounts, a culture of character gives way to a culture of personality.

Books about friendship, influence and positive thinking therefore gain particular force. Outward smiles and inner attitudes become instruments of advancement. At the same time, people fear disappearing into corporations or being subordinated to machines and government. Children of this world seek freedom from conformity, turn towards community and explore Eastern practices. This complexity matters to Storr: the coming culture of radical selfhood emerges not simply from greed or competition, but also from a desire for more humane relationships and release from an oppressive order.

### Goodness beneath condemnation

Carl Rogers offers another way of relating to patients. Instead of suspicion about hidden corruption, he proposes trust and unconditional positive regard. In Storr’s account, the relationship with the therapist is an important discovery: people need a space where they are not reduced to a disorder or met with continual condemnation.

A broader belief develops from this. Human nature at its deepest is understood as friendly, rational and forward-moving; social demands obstruct its emergence. Encounter groups should allow honesty, loosen defences and recover authenticity. Methods intended for patients begin promising growth to people without mental disorders. The human potential movement emerges.

Storr does not confuse the value of compassion with proof that individuals possess unlimited power. Helpful relationships and a theory of an originally perfect self are different propositions. He also includes participant William Coulson’s later disillusionment with Rogers’s work among Californian nuns, which Coulson considered destructive to their community. This is a participant’s retrospective account, not a controlled experiment proving the harm of acceptance in general. It shows how liberating individual desires could conflict with the organisation of collective life.

### A camp for human possibilities

The Esalen Institute, founded by Michael Murphy and Richard Price on the Californian coast, becomes the movement’s principal stage. An initial ambition to explore human possibilities gradually combines lectures, bodily practices and intensive encounters. Psychotherapy is expected not only to relieve suffering but also to uncover unknown powers in ordinary people.

Storr shows that this hope could rest on mistaken arguments. Aldous Huxley spoke of the supposed use of only a tenth of the brain; the author explicitly identifies this as false. But the promise of an unlimited reserve proved immensely attractive. Divine perfection need no longer be sought outside human beings: it supposedly waits within, ready to be released.

Storr himself enters this environment while feeling depressed and alienated from other people. He enrols in Paula Shaw’s six-day course, The Max, presented to him as close to the early encounters. His investigation becomes a trial of his own. Watching methods from outside and being exposed to a group’s attention are different experiences. He hopes to understand something about himself while distrusting the promise of radical transformation.

### When the narrator steps into the light

At the start, Shaw establishes strict rules: participants must not discuss the process, become distracted or comfort someone who cries. Each person should go on stage, find another person’s eyes and describe bodily sensations rather than familiar labels for emotions. Storr recognises something close to his own doubt about the interpreter: a verbal explanation is indeed different from an experience itself.

But sensations then acquire a guiding story. Participants are asked who made them feel this way, and the father repeatedly emerges. They must imagine him before them and express what they previously held back. Storr hears real grievances while also thinking of fathers who may themselves have failed an impossible combination of strength and care. One person’s liberation story risks turning another person into a simple villain.

When his turn arrives, Storr’s plan to remain unremarkable fails. Under Shaw’s questioning, he discusses emptiness, age, past drinking and doubts about his decency, then cries. Shaw supplies an explanation: he has rejected the noisy adolescent he once was and must bring that person back. He leaves the stage shaken. Feeling that something important has been touched does not yet tell him whether the suggested cause is correct.

### Authenticity in the hot seat

The course alternates with the history of Fritz Perls. He rejected retreat into the past and demanded an encounter with oneself in the present. In his hot seat, people attended to bodily expressions, enacted sides of an internal conflict and addressed imagined interlocutors. Perls could humiliate participants, treating evasion as evidence of falseness. His authority to decide who was being authentic became almost unlimited.

Will Schutz shifted some of that authority into the group itself. Candour supposedly mattered more than ordinary politeness, and encountering other people’s feelings opened a route to freedom. But if someone suffered, the ideology of complete personal responsibility allowed that suffering to be declared their own choice. Storr recounts Art Rogers’s experience: pressure and threats from the group culminated in a breakdown described as a methodological success.

The contradiction within liberation matters to the author. People are encouraged to escape others’ dictates, but must accept a leader’s explanation and endure collective pressure to do so. Perls and Schutz themselves compete for attention and fame. Their rivalry retains the tribal stakes from which a new consciousness was supposedly going to free participants.

### Who is authentic here?

Psychologist Bruce Hood offers Storr an account of the self as a representation constructed by the brain. Our sense of ourselves depends partly on how we believe others perceive us. This does not make suffering or actions imaginary. The illusion concerns a single internal observer who knows themselves completely and governs behaviour identically in every situation.

Storr connects Hood’s account with Sartre’s waiter performing an ideal waiter, and with Mark Snyder’s research. In the study, a man received an unrelated photograph of his supposed conversation partner. Expectations of attractiveness altered his manner, which then influenced the woman’s behaviour. A quality initially present only in a false expectation appeared to be confirmed. Social surroundings can thus participate in producing the self that emerges.

Bodily states also alter decisions. Research by Dan Ariely and George Loewenstein described in the book compared men’s answers in calm and sexually aroused conditions. It concerned predictions and stated willingness, not observation of every act mentioned. For Storr, such material undermines belief in a pure, invariably good core that can be uncovered by discarding prohibitions. Different manifestations belong to one person. Discovering an unpleasant desire neither reveals a final truth about oneself nor authorises acting on it.

### A goat, a trolley and an unpleasant role

Storr tries to fulfil Shaw’s assignment. Wearing an old T-shirt, he pushes another participant in a laundry trolley, briefly feels adolescent freedom again, then leads a goat towards an orchard. An annoyed staff member demands its return, and delight quickly becomes familiar shame. The episode is funny, but its purpose is serious: recovering an earlier self offers no direct route to happiness, because actions occur among real people with purposes of their own.

The next assignment is to become someone who does not get along with others. Storr allows himself to say unpleasant things and unexpectedly enjoys it. He is frightened: is this angry loner his true identity? Gradually, though, the feared role loses power. Other participants respond more warmly than he expected, finding him amusing and likeable beneath the ostentatious rudeness.

The course ends with the group working on a sketch. They write, rehearse and joke about their experiences, and Storr laughs with an ease he has not felt for years. This is no newly discovered divine centre. He has been accepted among people and is doing something with them. An irritable remark nevertheless escapes before he leaves. The visit has altered his experience of life without erasing his character.

### The pain of being excluded

To understand the cost of group rejection, Storr turns to Kip Williams’s research. At a picnic, Williams became an accidental participant in a game of Frisbee; then the other two players stopped throwing to him. A minor incident hurt. He brought the situation into studies of exclusion from a game, observing changes in self-esteem, perceived control and a sense of meaningful existence.

Storr discusses social pain with researchers: rejection does not become harmless because no physical injury has been inflicted. The precise overlap between neural mechanisms of social and physical pain is, however, identified in the book as disputed. Observed brain activity does not justify declaring every kind of pain the same phenomenon.

This changes the assessment of group cruelty. If people construct a sense of themselves through others’ responses, mockery in a vulnerable state may destroy rather than liberate. Storr recounts deaths among people around early Esalen, while explicitly acknowledging the unfairness of automatically blaming the institute for every tragedy. He criticises an environment and doctrine capable of refusing responsibility for harm. Storr himself felt relief when other participants accepted him and worked with him on a sketch. This personal experience does not disprove the danger; the danger does not cancel that relief.

### Doubt arrives too late

Even the movement’s creators noticed a gap between promise and result. Maslow worried about conceit, while Rogers complained of insufficient discipline among followers. In 1973, Esalen organised a conference on spiritual and therapeutic tyranny, attempting to examine guru power and the commercialisation of personal transformation.

Through Peter Marin’s reporting, the meeting becomes a scene of renewed disappointment: audiences still await liberation, speakers argue and discussion returns to personal needs. Marin titled his article The New Narcissism. Tom Wolfe soon described a related culture as a decade focused on “me.” These are contemporary accounts and critical interpretations, not psychological diagnoses of every participant.

The idea has nevertheless moved beyond the institute. The belief that people are wonderful inside and wholly responsible for what happens to them could suit a new social order. If confidence in one’s own value is treated as the principal resource, society can be offered a solution to difficult problems by increasing that confidence. The next part traces how this hope acquires political power and an appearance of scientific endorsement.

## Book Five. The special self

### Ayn Rand and her Collective

Another strand of the American story begins with Alisa Rosenbaum, whose family lost its property after the Russian Revolution. Moving to the United States and becoming Ayn Rand, she creates protagonists who set independent creativity against collective demands for self-sacrifice. People have a right to their own happiness, and the productive, thinking individual deserves freedom from coercion.

A circle gathers around Rand, ironically named the Collective. Storr reveals a paradox: preaching independent reason coexists with almost unconditional submission to one person’s authority. Its members include psychologist Nathaniel Branden and economist Alan Greenspan. Each will carry a different element of her ideas further, one through self-esteem and the other through economic policy.

Precision matters here. Branden’s self-esteem is initially tied to rationality, activity and achievement. It is not yet the classroom assurance that everybody is wonderful regardless of what they do. Storr does not treat every version as an unchanged doctrine. He follows an idea’s transformation as it moves between fiction, psychological practice and public policy. Someone else’s plot can become a political resource even as its meaning changes.

### Reason also needs recognition

Rand’s own life complicates the ideal of the independent creator. After Atlas Shrugged appears, she receives fierce criticism despite its considerable popular success. Negative reviews hurt, and supporters organise a defence. Greenspan publicly champions her. A group preaching liberation from collective judgments acts as a cohesive community around a wounded reputation.

Later, Rand’s break with Branden brings anger and public denunciation. Storr presents this as the history of particular relationships, without deriving a clinical diagnosis. He is interested in the persistent contradiction between a doctrine and the human need for approval. Reason’s strength does not make someone impervious to rejection.

The author also retains a detail inconvenient to a straightforward prosecution: Rand considered racism a crude form of collectivism. He therefore rejects one of the harshest comparisons between her novel and totalitarian ideology as unfair. Criticism must not become another instance of tribal simplification. Nevertheless, her self-reliant, talented and purposeful protagonist becomes an important ingredient of a world increasingly ready to explain failure through the inadequacy of the loser.

### When the rules change

The crises of the 1970s undermine the post-war compromise between government, employers and workers. In Storr’s account, Friedrich Hayek’s ideas, promoted over many years, gain a new political opportunity. Fear of centralised coercion supports freer markets, limited government and competition. Reagan and Thatcher become leading figures in this turn, while Greenspan acquires enormous power within finance.

The book’s concern extends beyond a list of reforms. If government and employers guarantee less, success favours people who can continually offer themselves, improve and absorb risk. The image of a worthy individual changes with the requirements of survival. Enterprise becomes a demand on personality rather than simply an occupation.

Storr calls this order neoliberal and emphasises its competitive logic. This is his way of connecting economic history to the history of an ideal, not an exhaustive explanation of every reform or personal decision. It nevertheless helps explain a new promise’s appeal: when everyone must rely on themselves, belief in the power of the self appears especially necessary. In this environment, politician John Vasconcellos proposes increasing the population’s self-esteem as a remedy for social problems.

### The man who would not vote for himself

Vasconcellos, known as Vasco, grew up an exemplary Catholic boy. He excelled at school and tried to behave correctly, but thinking well of himself seemed almost forbidden. He once lost a school election by one vote, his own: he could not bring himself to vote for himself. Storr reconstructs this history through archives, letters, recordings and accounts by people who knew him.

Professional success does not resolve Vasco’s internal conflict. After becoming a legislator, he seeks help from priest and psychologist Leo Rock, who is connected with Rogers. Compassionate attention becomes an important experience. Reading humanistic psychology, meeting Rogers and attending Esalen seminars follow. A man who considered himself corrupt adopts faith in people’s original worth and possibilities.

The transformation is visible: rigid suits give way to informal clothing and ease. Yet colleagues continue encountering anger, contempt and dark moods. An employee writes that refusing responsibility for other people’s feelings is convenient for someone unwilling to notice them. Storr does not deny the help Vasco received. He shows that personal liberation does not guarantee considerate behaviour, and that a narrative of complete transformation may conceal substantial continuity of character.

### A social vaccine

Vasco wants to turn his personal discovery into public policy. He considers low self-esteem a cause of many problems, from educational failure to violence and welfare dependence. Teaching self-respect could supposedly prevent these problems and save public money. Self-esteem acquires the role of a social vaccine.

The project passes through setbacks, the politician’s own illness and resistance from Governor George Deukmejian. Eventually Vasco adds personal and social responsibility to its name and reduces the budget. The savings argument persuades the conservative governor. A state-funded task force is established in 1986. Its purpose combines investigating an idea with promoting something its creator already believes is true.

Journalists initially laugh. Doonesbury turns the Californian initiative into a national joke, bringing unprecedented attention. Thousands respond, and volunteers and supporters address hearings. Personal accounts persuade emotionally: praise ribbons, supportive teachers and former criminals’ testimony. But evidence that approval mattered to someone does not prove that artificially increasing self-esteem will remove the listed social problems. That conclusion requires different evidence.

### Science expected to agree

California legislator John Vasconcellos, known as Vasco, promotes self-esteem as a social vaccine. His task force recruits University of California professors: their review should provide the project’s scientific foundation. After sociologist Neil Smelser, who coordinates the research, addresses the group, it reports convincing positive associations. The press changes its tone: yesterday’s absurdity becomes an initiative endorsed by a serious university. The governor recommends the material to colleagues, and the final report reaches an unusually large circulation.

Storr develops the consequences of this legitimisation. School programmes, political speeches and popular broadcasting spread the idea of self-esteem’s central importance. Oprah Winfrey enters the story; encouragement practices become recognisable far beyond California. The author does not claim that nobody cared about children’s confidence before Vasco. He investigates how one version of the idea acquired governmental and scientific authority.

Then the investigation turns. Dissident member David Shannahoff-Khalsa, who refused to endorse the final document, shows Storr the published academic review. Relationships between self-esteem and its expected consequences are often unclear, weak or absent. The negative findings are not hidden in an inaccessible secret: they are printed in a book. But a different story has reached a mass audience. Storr must establish both the discrepancy and what happened between the research and the headline.

### What the tape contained

After unsuccessful searches, Storr finds an archive cassette recording the meeting of 8 September 1988. Smelser does not declare a general victory for the theory. He describes mixed findings. Positive, reasonably convincing correlations concern school achievement, among other things. This is the fragment used in public communications, with its meaning expanded.

The following part changes the message entirely. Smelser explains that even an association does not establish the direction of causation. Does self-doubt lead to heavy drinking, for example, or does prolonged drinking undermine self-regard? Without an answer, increasing self-esteem cannot be said to prevent alcoholism. He ends by warning against offering legislators ready-made instructions as though a particular intervention were known to deliver a desired result.

Another recording captures objections within the group. Shannahoff-Khalsa and Wilbur Brantley question how conclusions are being presented to the press. Vasco answers in terms of an individual quotation’s accuracy: if those words were spoken, nothing needs correction. But the problem is the loss of the claim’s boundaries. A verbatim fragment can mislead when its subject and evidential limits disappear. This is the book’s central investigative scene: a finding becomes cultural truth through selection and a shift in meaning.

### Why authority did not correct the message

Storr’s conversation with Smelser does not divide neatly into honest academics and dishonest politicians. The sociologist explains his loyalty to the university and the need for diplomacy. Vasco controlled the budget, so even a polite request for help could not be treated as an ordinary citizen’s approach. Smelser describes no direct threat to cut funding; the context of power itself mattered.

He considers the research conscientious but acknowledges an effort to preserve good relations. Some of his own statements sounded more favourable than the cautious conclusions. His memory of the disputed quotation is incomplete. For Storr, this is not proof that he knows all Smelser’s motives. It shows how professional and institutional loyalty can coexist with accurate data while obstructing their public defence.

Task force chairman Andrew Mecca speaks differently. In his interview with Storr, he openly places conviction above inconvenient science and describes work with editors and producers. Positive presentation was intended to overwhelm dissent. The campaign won because its version was louder and better organised. Two previous themes converge: people believe in their activity’s heroic plot, and university prestige helps others mistake that plot for knowledge. An individual critic need not prevail even when the document is available.

### A researcher changes his own belief

Roy Baumeister does not enter as someone who always knew the answer. As a young man, he too regarded high self-esteem as an almost universal good. Storr describes his severe father, intellectual searches and life and research partnership with Dianne Tice. Biography establishes personal stakes without replacing scrutiny of scientific conclusions.

Working on the psychology of evil, Baumeister begins to question the familiar explanation of aggression through low self-esteem. Sources cite one another, but a reliable foundation for the cliché proves elusive. He proposes an alternative: perhaps aggression follows threats to an excessively high opinion of oneself. He later has an opportunity to conduct a wide critical review of self-esteem research.

The team excludes work where well-being is established only through participants’ claims. Confident people saying their relationships are excellent is not independent confirmation of those relationships’ quality. The selected literature does not sustain promises of universal improvement. At school, success could precede confidence rather than follow it. Published in 2003, the review retains some benefits, including feeling better and taking initiative, but does not justify turning self-esteem into a remedy for every social ill. This is the conclusion of the work discussed, not a declaration about the final state of science today.

### Self-esteem and superiority

An experiment by Baumeister and Brad Bushman is particularly important. Participants could choose the volume of an unpleasant noise for an opponent. It was meant to test the suspicion that high self-esteem made people more aggressive. Self-esteem itself proved a weak explanation of the results. Narcissistic traits combined with provocation mattered much more.

The initial hypothesis therefore required revision. Confidence in one’s worth can be calm and justified; wanting superiority and special treatment is different. Someone who need not continually defend exceptional status is not obliged to react furiously to an insult. Storr preserves this scientific correction rather than stopping at the striking formula, “High self-esteem causes violence.”

Sociometer theory, associated with Baumeister and Mark Leary, treats self-esteem as monitoring one’s value in relationships. Painful signals then indicate perceived threats to acceptance. Inflating self-regard may bypass that connection, generating a sense of importance without corresponding relationships. This does not mean people should demean themselves. The criticism concerns indiscriminate mechanical inflation, rather than dignity, care or help for someone with low self-esteem.

### A generation in dispute

Jean Twenge and Keith Campbell ask whether narcissistic traits become more widespread as culture changes. Their meta-analysis combines students’ NPI results across years. They find an increase and call it an epidemic. Storr lets them explain their method: they compare similarly aged people in different periods, not simply older people with younger ones.

The dispute does not end with that clarification. Kali Trzesniewski challenges the analysis and the scale of its conclusion, presenting other datasets. Twenge responds with objections about mixing university samples and with calculations of her own; Trzesniewski maintains her position. Some self-esteem data do not confirm the expected increase, which Twenge acknowledges. Self-esteem and narcissism do not thereby become interchangeable measures.

Eddie Brummelman and colleagues investigate a more specific mechanism by following children and parents for two years. In Storr’s account, parental overvaluation of children’s exceptional qualities preceded stronger narcissistic traits; lack of warmth did not receive equivalent support. This distinguishes care from glorification. But the study does not prove that one politician caused a generational change. Storr acknowledges that history cannot be rerun without Vasco. He argues for the commission’s influence through historical evidence, while retaining limits around a causal chain extending to an entire generation.

### The cost of a free game

The economic strand returns to Greenspan and the Clinton administration, the priority given to financial markets and deregulation. Storr connects these decisions to vulnerabilities exposed by the 2008 crisis. Unemployment, lost homes and austerity show that defeat can result from changes outside a person’s control. Estimates of additional suicides mentioned in the book are research estimates of crisis effects, not knowledge of a single cause for each death.

The author explicitly refuses to cast neoliberalism as an evil character. He acknowledges improved living standards for many and reduced poverty through international trade. A system has benefits, losses and compromises. His objection concerns the distribution of risk and the moral explanation of failure: winners acquire more opportunities to shape the rules, while losers are required to become still more self-reliant.

Corporate explanations about playing by the rules and everyday transfers of work onto customers become parts of the same picture. Not every inconvenience equals an economic catastrophe; the connection is a movement of responsibility towards individuals. The next setting is where independence, usefulness and continual self-improvement have taken an especially attractive technological form: Silicon Valley.

## Book Six. The digital self

### A machine for human thought

In December 1968, Douglas Engelbart demonstrates an unusual way of working to an audience of computing specialists. Words appear on a screen and can be moved, copied and saved. A cursor responds to a small device beneath his hand. A network connects different people’s work. In an age of enormous machines associated with military and bureaucratic power, the computer appears as an extension of individual capabilities.

Storr returns to the moment Engelbart sought a life’s purpose. Career and love were already going well; the absence of another major goal felt empty. He resolved to create tools enabling people to cope with complexity. The ambition was not solely individual: augmenting intelligence meant cooperation and access to shared material. An independent user should not remain isolated.

The story matters because technologies also embody ideas about people. A machine can be conceived as an instrument for governing populations or freeing them. Yet Storr does not make Engelbart the sole inventor of every subsequent system. He follows influence through collaborative work, research institutions and later adaptations. The future digital world has a cultural as well as a technical ancestry.

### A laboratory promised liberation

Engelbart’s work encounters several difficulties: military funding requirements, disagreements within the team and departing specialists. Encounter groups associated with the human potential movement and est training add another complication. He hopes openness will increase employees’ creativity and strongly supports their participation.

Some experience it as interference in their lives. Employees complain about unpredictable leadership, division and the feeling of being experimental subjects. Storr draws on historical accounts here, not a controlled comparison of management methods. The laboratory’s crisis had several causes; a psychological programme is not made the only one.

Through Xerox and Apple, some ideas gain further life. In Engelbart’s account, a disagreement with Jobs emerges: a powerful computer on a desk is insufficient without access to other people’s documents and work. Storr uses this conflict to restore a neglected part of the original dream. Digital empowerment could mean expanding collaboration, not simply greater personal power and an attractive possession. Which version prevails cannot depend on one technical discovery alone.

### A catalogue of a new world

Stewart Brand, who helped with Engelbart’s demonstration, connects technology to counterculture. His Whole Earth Catalog offers tools, knowledge and ways of arranging one’s own life. The online community WELL and consultancy Global Business Network later emerge around this milieu. People from Esalen, engineers, entrepreneurs and influential clients enter the story.

Following historian Fred Turner, Storr shows anti-authoritarian hope moving closer to the market. Old hierarchies are supposed to give way to flexible networks, with each person an independent node of knowledge, creativity and income. Hostility to centralised power links some countercultural ideas with libertarianism. A particular network of organisations, magazines and acquaintances meanwhile helps this future acquire prestige.

The Long Boom, an optimistic text by Peter Schwartz and a co-author, describes prospects for growth and openness. It is presented as a scenario, not a guaranteed prediction. But the ensuing dot-com crash exposes the danger of overly confident expectation. A social internet follows the early wave: expression, personal disclosure and recognition acquire interfaces, counters and audiences. The promise of a new world begins operating through millions of everyday habits.

### One profile among others

In the social internet, everyone can display themselves and receive responses. For Storr, this technically joins ancient and new forces: tribal sensitivity to reputation encounters personal branding, and the search for recognition meets measurable likes and followers. Participants can choose their image while also being pushed to compare it with others.

Technology does not create every desire from nothing. Storr has already traced public worth, prestige and perfection. Social platforms make this history continuous, portable and available for numerical comparison. A successful body, relationship and lifestyle can be displayed daily, while other people’s approval becomes a rapid signal of personal value.

This is the author’s explanation of the period he describes, particularly Web 2.0 and the aftermath of the 2008 crisis. It is not an assessment of present platforms. He asks how promised personal freedom can coincide with stronger self-monitoring and competition. To see people inside that promise, he visits communities of technology workers.

### Dinner with the inventor of a selfie drone

At Rainbow Mansion, prospective residents are selected for their interests, work and connections. Vanessa, who works for NASA, explains that the community wants people who can contribute. Storr sees a network of mutual usefulness, friendly and demanding at once. Acceptance depends on more than wanting to live together.

Over dinner, engineer Jeremy shows him a device designed to unfold from a wrist into a small drone, photograph its owner and return. The technical achievement astonishes Storr. But the account of creating it includes almost continuous work, sleeping in a warehouse and the possibility of losing compensation if the company fails. Jeremy is proud of making something remarkable and does not conceal the cost.

Asked about wealth, he says money alone is insufficient for recognition here. People must also create something influential and change the world. Social mission does not replace success; it becomes an additional requirement. This is one interviewee, not a representative of every engineer. His account nevertheless makes the founder ideal intelligible: an independent, tireless visionary who benefits millions while winning a competitive game.

### Enterprise instead of autobiography

At another residence, 20Mission, Storr meets Shannin, who has left software engineering for a food business. Working independently lets him combine an interest with the hope of success. Another resident, Berkeley, sees more value in technology services than municipal government. Storr argues for collective support without persuading him: continuing poverty appears to Berkeley to demonstrate the failure of government efforts.

The conversation reveals business as creativity and self-expression. One person’s central project is a work of art, another’s a company. Storr recognises Rand and an older plot of the active protagonist. His own reactions remain part of the reporting: among younger residents, he feels age and awkwardness rather than occupying an all-knowing judge’s position.

Steph, who works online, describes openness in relationships and hopes for technological singularity. These expectations belong to the interviewee, rather than being presented as an achieved future. The contrast between the experimental community and poverty outside sharpens the question: who gets included in promised freedom, and who bears the consequences of its arrangements?

### Can everything be improved?

Daniel Faber describes extracting materials from asteroids for future activity in space. He sees an immense task and proposes a route to solving it. Storr admires the scale while questioning the certainty. The cities, resources and timescales remain his interviewee’s plans, not achievements certified by the book.

Faber is also interested in political experiments in isolated space settlements. Storr hears a familiar continuation: remove restrictions and let intelligent people try, and a better arrangement of life can emerge. Yet the absence of an already known solution also means unknown consequences.

Kate Levy, who is close to Rainbow’s community, supplies an internal objection. She challenges enthusiasm for a robot capable of preparing all meals: convenience could remove livelihoods from people with nowhere else to go. Not everyone can become a programmer. She also feels alienated when her religious background invites contempt. People want a good future, but their image of those who can benefit may resemble themselves too closely. Storr returns to an old mistake: treating the workings of one’s own mind as universal.

### The politics of a wounded hero

The reporting expands to the events of 2016. Storr turns to Katherine Cramer’s work among Wisconsin residents who felt they followed the old rules diligently but received less money, influence and respect. When the promised connection between effort and a decent life breaks down, a familiar story of one’s place in the country ceases to work.

In this frame, Trump and Brexit campaigners offer restored control and opposition to contemptuous elites. Storr neither excuses racism nor claims that all voters had identical motives. He acknowledges his biases and the limits of the explanation. His question is why an emotional plot about recovering dignity can be more powerful than economic calculations.

Social media strengthens the political role of such plots. A politician’s message can appear online and then reach people who do not use the platform through traditional reporting. Indignation unites supporters, repeated exposure to opponents’ extremes deepens hostility, and blocking or unfollowing tightens circles of agreement. Storr sees the tribe technically reorganised rather than dissolved. His analysis is not a complete theory of elections or subsequent political developments.

### CJ faces the camera

Back in Britain, Storr meets twenty-two-year-old CJ, a drama student. She takes many selfies and may edit photographs for hours. What matters is not merely appearance, but how it will look in a photograph. During their conversation, she assesses a picture’s quality and pursues something good enough for an audience.

Her admissions seem almost performative. She loves attention, wants fame and readily discusses herself. She scores highly on the NPI questionnaire, though Storr allows that she might deliberately intensify her image as a model case. This is an assessment of a trait and a journalist’s observation, not a clinical diagnosis. He later explicitly states that treating her as typical of most millennials would be absurd.

A familiar arrangement of recognition nevertheless emerges. CJ especially values another person wanting a photograph with her: the picture proves she is wanted. One compliment can sustain her day, but the absence of further responses renews the need. Her sense of worth is maintained by continuously arriving reactions, rather than confidence discovered once and for all.

### A girl in a protected house

CJ describes real dangers and bullying in childhood. Her parents tried to protect her by moving, taking her out of school and arranging education at home. Later, financial comfort allowed wishes to be fulfilled almost immediately. She heard that she was exceptionally beautiful and talented, received gifts and enjoyed abundant attention.

Protection gradually became isolation. She resented visitors, disliked being touched and became angry with relatives. Her account includes self-harm that briefly offered control over feelings. The meaning matters here, not the method: an outwardly privileged life did not eliminate pain or the need for control. Storr cannot establish from a single biography which element of upbringing caused each consequence.

At performing arts school, CJ interpreted criticism as misunderstanding her talent and prided herself on a directness that hurt others. Rare praise from her became especially desirable to classmates. Approval turned into power. The book’s theme takes an unpleasant turn: someone can regard cruel candour as proof of authenticity, and a wish for recognition as authority to determine others’ value.

### Admiration instead of intimacy

In early romantic stories, CJ seeks adoration above all. She learns a young man’s interests, becomes his perfect girlfriend and loses interest once she is certain of his attachment. A gift she describes, a portrait of Eminem for a fan, appears caring but, by her own explanation, serves conquest. Storr preserves that attribution: he hears her account of her actions, not the feelings of every participant.

Her later relationship with Perry is different. She falls in love and allows him to see her without a carefully prepared image. Discovering messages to his former partner destroys her expected exclusivity. After the breakup, suffering becomes entangled with her body and other people’s attention. Accusations of selfishness upset her less than the suggestion that she is unexceptional.

The story does not support a new demand to hate oneself. It distinguishes recognition of human worth from dependence on superiority. Storr does not turn CJ into a monster: he likes her when they part, while her decision to work over the holidays shows an obligation missing from a simple portrait of a spoiled child. A living person remains more complicated than the case that helps an author develop an argument.

### The pool nobody enters

Researcher Jesse Fox describes students sitting fully dressed beside a conference swimming pool. They had swimwear, but seeing themselves through a potential spectator’s eyes kept them out of the water. Online life adds to ordinary embarrassment the possibility that someone will take and publish a picture. Experience becomes governed by an imagined future audience as well as the audience physically present.

Fox explains the dangers of constant positive feedback and sharing a feed with celebrities. An apparently accessible route to fame intensifies comparison. But she qualifies that research available at the time did not establish an unambiguous causal effect of social media on depression. An association with distress and a possible mechanism are not equivalent to that proof.

Storr also retains positive generational differences: fewer of some risky habits, and greater attention to health and structural inequality. A cultural cause has not been established. At the section’s end, he worries about online punishment and intolerance, including on campuses. These are his judgments and particular cases, not grounds for declaring a generation ill or incapable of empathy. The central line remains: treating one’s feelings as sacred can coexist with cruelty towards someone outside one’s group.

## Book Seven. Surviving the age of perfectionism

### The man who wanted to rewrite life

The last major story concerns Austen Heinz, founder of Cambrian Genomics. Storr reconstructs it through presentations, publications and conversations with his sister Adrianna, friend Mike Alfred and partner-company founder Audrey Hutchinson. This is journalistic reconstruction; the author’s presence at private scenes is not implied.

Heinz works on reducing the cost of producing DNA. He envisages treating biological code almost like information and dreams of organisms that could help solve major human problems. Storr distinguishes the technical work and the company’s collaborations from sweeping claims about the future. Promises of creating perfect beings do not become established capabilities.

The desire to improve humanity brings the cultural history to an extreme: perfection is imagined as an engineering project as well as a psychological one. Yet Austen remains vulnerable, struggles to understand social responses and has experienced difficult periods. Scientific intelligence and courage cannot guarantee that he can endure any public situation. The conflict between an all-powerful protagonist and a particular person will become central.

### How a medical project became an outrageous headline

At a conference in 2014, Heinz describes his technology and briefly discusses partner projects. One, Sweet Peach, was founded by Audrey Hutchinson to develop personalised vaginal probiotics. During the presentation and subsequent explanations, scent appears as a possible indicator of activity. The founder is not named on stage, and she had not approved this presentation of her company.

Coverage turns the account into a story about men deciding to give women’s bodies a fruity smell. Headlines make something secondary and confusedly explained the main subject. Storr considers this unfair: a medical purpose and the underlying technology disappear behind an image of smug sexists. Yet he does not erase Hutchinson’s legitimate dissatisfaction. She had reasons to object to being denied a voice and to inaccurate descriptions of her product.

Later clarifications do not travel with the same force. Hutchinson publicly says Heinz supported her and helped make the project possible, but the favourable correction scarcely alters the image already established. Storr presents a distinction between a mistake, criticism and turning a person into a ready-made villain, rather than a perfectly innocent hero. Professional awkwardness and lack of agreement require accountability; they do not make every subsequent accusation accurate.

### When the way out disappears

Public controversy is followed by difficulties raising money, shrinking the team and solving technical problems. Austen’s sister describes exhaustion, sleeplessness and the repeated conviction that everything is over. Relatives try to help, and the family seeks professional care. In March 2015, Heinz dies by suicide at thirty-one.

Storr does not reduce his death to one headline or add a diagnosis of his own. Adrianna disputes circulated claims about bipolar disorder. Referring to medical records, she says the diagnosis was major depression. She considers the attacks a trigger for the final deterioration, while also saying nobody can be singled out as responsible for his death. This is his sister’s assessment, not an experimental finding about causation.

The account returns to O’Connor: defeat becomes especially dangerous when it appears final and no escape is visible. Storr sees an interaction of character, previous vulnerability, business difficulties and a hostile environment. He need not accept every ambition of Heinz’s to consider public harassment unjust. Nor must he consider social skills adequate to recognise the cost of their absence. A perfect hero who can invent, charm, attract money and withstand humiliation all at once exists only in a story.

### The lesson Little did not teach

Brian Little recalls a graduating student thanking him for the confidence that she could achieve anything. This was exactly what he had not taught. His research on personality and personal projects concerns goals that fit particular capacities, rather than unlimited desire. Even after attentive study, cultural expectations can replace what was heard with a more agreeable promise.

For Storr, the next step is uncomfortable: people possess enduring differences they cannot simply cancel. He encounters descriptions of five traits: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism. His own results help him see familiar solitude, irritability and anxiety as more than failures of moral self-improvement.

Psychologist Daniel Nettle explains that traits vary by degree rather than being switched on or off. They involve many small differences and do not exhaust a person’s description. Questionnaires have limits, and biological explanations remain incomplete. Storr preserves this discussion rather than turning five names into another classification that predicts everyone’s life. He is interested in enduring tendencies and how they alter the meaning of demands placed on oneself.

### Stability does not mean immobility

A small difference in responses to conflict can gradually alter a social world. One person reacts irritably more often; another lets an unpleasant signal pass. Others respond, producing different reputations, friendships and familiar situations. People also select suitable communities and activities. Personality does not merely exist within an environment: it helps select and create an environment that can then strengthen its tendencies.

Nettle does not say change is impossible. Age, learning, crises, therapy and circumstances can alter manifestations and some average scores. Relative stability in differences between people is not a promise that every test result remains identical forever. Storr explicitly preserves this boundary after his own dark metaphors of restriction.

Even parental influence supplies no mechanical instruction. Research on twins and differences between siblings challenges a simple calculation: raise children identically and obtain identical characters. The same experience interacts with different characteristics. This does not remove parental care, safety or responsibility; it limits the claim to design a future personality completely. The book has been moving towards distinctions between influence, possibility and total control.

### Correcting his own idea of the self

Earlier, Storr was attracted to the idea that a stable self is an illusion. He could then regard an unpleasant side of himself as a temporary role that would vanish in different surroundings. He now recognises that changes across contexts and relatively enduring traits need not exclude each other. One person can behave differently while bringing persistent tendencies into different situations.

Little calls actions against habitual temperament for an important project “free traits.” An introvert can address an audience. Yet the action may require effort and recovery; performing it is not the same as becoming another person. Storr returns to Pridmore, who left crime for Catholicism, to Vasco, who gained confidence but retained his temper, and to his own visit to Esalen: a new life story genuinely changed something, but did not erase everything that preceded it.

His later acknowledgement of a kind of authenticity therefore does not restore Esalen’s perfect soul. It concerns actual limits and stability, not an ideal being hidden within who should be given unrestricted freedom. This turn prevents the ending becoming another myth about choosing any self. People can act and change their circumstances without possessing unlimited power over their nature.

### Accepting limits while remaining responsible

For Nettle, the connection between success and happiness is not obvious either. Anxiety may make people check their work again and seek better solutions while also making life painful. He does not propose declaring suffering a necessary virtue or choosing pleasure for everyone. What people value, and what price they accept, has no single answer.

Storr gradually experiences relief. If some qualities are enduring characteristics rather than shameful malfunctions, endless self-accusation loses its basis. But he expressly distances himself from a Perls-style position where authenticity excuses any behaviour. Characteristics can hurt others. Their effects need to be moderated; apologies and efforts to fulfil obligations may be necessary. Recognising limits does not remove the need to be punctual or care for the people one lives with.

The criticism of an ideal therefore opposes a total verdict against the person. Failure in a conversation does not prove someone wholly bad; another person’s success does not establish equal opportunities for everyone. Storr does not advocate diminished dignity or self-hatred. He wants to weaken the demand to become a culturally approved hero continually. A calmer understanding of oneself should leave more room for actual actions and relationships.

### Changing a life without promising a different person

Little’s next step is choosing meaningful, achievable personal projects. Revising a goal that does not fit one’s capacities need not mean surrender. Another route to a fulfilling life may be possible. A project need not concern a career, fame or grand altruism. Its meaning to the person and the possibility of acting within it matter.

Storr shifts attention from remaking an essence to conditions: companions, daily arrangements and aims. The monk Father Martin would not exemplify happiness for everyone, but found an appropriate combination of order, meaning and relationships. Storr himself felt relief at Esalen among people engaged in a shared activity. Different stories do not produce a universal technique, but show why one ideal character cannot suit everyone.

A person is not a god fully governing themselves and the consequences of their actions. Recognising this does not release them from the world. Others participate in our existence, and we participate in theirs. The final argument therefore joins limitations, context and responsibility. Reducing self-punishment, choosing a more realistic direction and noticing others’ vulnerability belong to one rejection of absolute autonomy, rather than a promise of new perfection.

### A rocket in the fog

The book ends with Storr travelling with Rainbow residents to a SpaceX rocket launch. They pass places through which his investigation has taken him. Vanessa mentions a question from NASA selection: does she consider people good or bad? She answered good. After all the arguments, the author enjoys being with these people.

This neither cancels his criticism of Silicon Valley nor establishes that technical progress has solved human contradictions. Storr retains affection, surprise and the pleasure of a shared trip. People whom his argument placed inside cultural patterns remain companions and interlocutors, rather than a collection of examples of mistaken beliefs.

Thick fog surrounds the countdown. The rocket is invisible, but a powerful roar sounds and the ground shakes. Spectators smile and raise their phones. The final scene holds together the wish to move forward and the inability to see everything, technological wonder and human company. A history of perfection ends by recognising imperfect people. The desire to look, hope and stand beside others has not disappeared.
