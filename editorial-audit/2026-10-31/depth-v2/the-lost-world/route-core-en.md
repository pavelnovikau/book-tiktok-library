# Core

Derived reading route for root; screen copy is exact.

## cover · The Lost World

Reporter Edward Malone seeks a heroic deed. Challenger offers an investigation of an incredible world beyond red cliffs. Essential stops at the foot of the plateau; Core stops at the empty camp. Both are unfinished openings. The complete retained story and ending are in Deep after consent.

## money · A conversation about money

That evening I spent more than an hour listening to Mr Hungerton talk about money. He seemed perfectly certain that I called three times a week for the pleasure of his company. All I wanted was a chance to be alone with his daughter. When he finally went off to dress, I moved nearer to Gladys. She immediately understood that a proposal was coming and asked me not to spoil things. She liked our friendship. That satisfied me about as much as an intimate conversation with a stationmaster would have done.

## gladys-ideal · Gladys’s ideal

Gladys confessed that she loved someone else. I jumped up; she laughed and explained that he was still only an ideal. Her chosen man must perform great deeds, face death, discover unknown countries. She thought of explorers and their wives, who shared the reflected glory of their husbands. I was ready to turn myself into almost anything to please her. But that readiness was precisely what she disliked: a real hero would act from his own nature, rather than at a girl’s request.

## mine-report · The colliery report

I protested that I had never had an opportunity to distinguish myself. She found opportunities for heroism everywhere. What about the recent colliery explosion? Why had I not gone down to help, instead of merely describing it? I had to explain that I had gone down. Gladys became interested, but my motive disappointed her. To write well about an event, I explained, you had to be where it was happening. That sounded too ordinary to her. She wanted a famous husband whom other women could envy her for.

## evening-duty · Evening duty

I left on that foggy November evening determined to find a worthy deed. I was twenty-three, in love for the first time, and had no intention of sorting out the love and vanity in her dream. I was already late for evening duty at the Daily Gazette. There, in the office, I decided to ask immediately for a dangerous assignment. At that moment I could imagine the glory ahead much more distinctly than any possible danger.

## mcardle · The assignment

Our news editor, McArdle, a stooping, red-haired Scot, received me kindly: he had liked my accounts of the mine and the fire. When I mentioned a hazardous commission, he first supposed I wanted to get myself killed. I tried to explain that I wanted to justify my life. Such lofty ambitions fitted newspaper expenses badly. Major expeditions were costly, the blank spaces on maps were disappearing, and an inexperienced reporter could scarcely expect a special mission. Then McArdle remembered Professor Challenger.

## challenger-record · The man at Enmore Park

This zoologist had returned from South America with incredible stories and damaged photographs. He would not say exactly where he had been. Questions produced insults, and some journalists had been thrown out. McArdle proposed exposing a modern Munchausen. I remembered reports of an injured colleague and agreed nevertheless. At least the promised danger was conveniently close to the office. The editor supplied a short biography and dismissed me with the comforting thought that my employer provided insurance.

## tarp-henry · A scientific acquaintance

Before visiting the professor, I consulted Tarp Henry of Nature. He described a brilliant mind and an impossible temper. Challenger managed to insult even people who invited him to address scientific societies. But perhaps, I thought, there was one way to approach such a man: through his own science. He might exclude a journalist but admit a young investigator wishing to discuss his ideas. All I needed was a scientific question.

## borrowed-science · The letter

At Henry’s office we opened an account of Challenger’s dispute with other scientists in Vienna. I understood the references to uproar and protests perfectly; I understood scarcely anything of the article itself. At last I found an impressive sentence about heredity and put it in a letter. I wrote as though I had reread his admirable address and wished to clarify some objections. Henry called me a liar as I read it out. My conscience survived. I asked for eleven o’clock on Wednesday and obtained permission to use the journal’s address.

## appointment · Wednesday at eleven

The reply was everything one might have expected. The professor did not need my approval, had no intention of altering his mature convictions, and wondered at my inability to understand plain English. Nevertheless, I could come. I must show the envelope to his servant, who would thereby distinguish me from intrusive journalists. After reading it, Henry recommended a treatment for bruises. On Wednesday morning I went to Enmore Park, still hoping that the opening minutes might offer a chance to confess without a fight.

## impostor · At the professor’s desk

The professor sat behind a desk covered with maps and books. Above it rose a tremendous head, massive shoulders, and a thick black beard. His voice matched the rest. My expressions of approval received mock gratitude: the support of so young an expert would naturally strengthen his position considerably. I tried talking about the trend of modern thought, but he demanded definite evidence. Then he asked several questions, to which I confidently agreed. He promptly announced that he had been talking nonsense and that I was an ordinary journalistic impostor.

## chair · In the passage

I could only back towards the door. Challenger advanced, listing the sums it had already cost him to eject my colleagues. I warned him that I played rugby and would not allow him to lay hands on me. He rushed me. We locked together, collected a chair on the way, and rolled along the passage towards the door Austin, the servant, had opened. The steps completed our introduction: the chair broke, we separated, and the professor sprang up, still eager to fight. A policeman was already beside us with his notebook.

## policeman · The notebook

The policeman recognized a recurring offender and asked whether I wished to press charges. A black eye was developing, but I refused. I had entered the house by deception despite the warning. The policeman put away his book and dispersed the spectators. Challenger looked at me differently: behind the fury there was something like amusement. He ordered me back inside. It was scarcely reassuring, yet I followed him once more into the house from which I had just emerged so violently.

## confidence · A cigar and a promise

Now he offered a cigar and explained the change. My answer to the policeman had shown some capacity for detached judgment, rare, in his opinion, among my profession. He was therefore prepared to tell me something. But not a word must appear in print without his consent. I tried objecting; he immediately prepared to end the interview. I had to promise. Only then did the battered sketchbook of an American named Maple White appear on the desk.

## white-belongings · A traveller’s sketchbook

Two years earlier Challenger had been investigating the Amazonian forests. On his return, people in a village summoned him to a dying stranger, but he found the man already dead: emaciated, in rags, having come out of the forest alone. His belongings included a name and address in Detroit, paints, poems, a weapon, an unusual bone, and the sketchbook. Challenger thought little of the poetry but treated the book almost as a sacred relic. He made me turn its pages in order. At first there were ordinary travellers, villagers, and animals; I could not see the discovery.

## drawing-scale · The man in the drawing

Then came sheer red cliffs, an isolated pinnacle with a tree, and a strange gigantic animal. The little man beside it looked like a dwarf. I suggested that the artist had been drinking. Challenger explained that the man provided scale, as did the tall palm behind the beast. Then he opened a scientific book at a picture of a stegosaurus. The resemblance was striking. I still allowed for imagination or a remembered illustration, but the next object, a large fresh bone, already exceeded my meagre zoological knowledge.

## first-journey · The first red cliffs

Following White’s traces and local accounts, Challenger reached the cliffs in the picture. The guides were unwilling to go towards the supposed dwelling of the terrifying forest spirit Curupuri. Gifts and threats eventually persuaded them. The professor found remains of the American’s camp, saw the inaccessible plateau, heard strange cries from above, and shot a winged creature in the tree on the pinnacle. He could not, however, reach the plateau itself. The rains and dwindling supplies forced him to turn back.

## wing · A fragment of wing

On the way back his boat was wrecked in the rapids. Water spoiled almost all his photographs and swept away his specimen. Challenger tried to hold it and was left clutching a piece of wing. That fragment now lay before me. I called it the wing of an enormous bat; he made me compare its structure with a drawing of a pterodactyl. In his account, the pictures, bone, photographs, and wing combined into evidence. I believed him, already imagining the extraordinary material he could provide for the paper.

## isolation · Challenger’s explanation

The professor supplied his explanation: an ancient upheaval had isolated a tract of land, preserving creatures that had vanished elsewhere. He spoke with complete assurance. Scientists in London had received him differently, suspecting a fraud. After the first disputes he had stopped showing his evidence and driven away the curious. Now, he declared, he would try self-restraint: at a public lecture that evening he would tactfully hint at the discovery. He gave me an invitation. I should attend as at least one ally, however ignorant and ineffective.

## waldron · The public lecture

That evening I went to the public lecture. The hall was full of scientists and students ready for entertainment. The chairman spoke so softly that he seemed to be addressing the water-carafe. Waldron, the lecturer, knew how to engage an audience and began a vivid history of the Earth. When he reached the ancient reptiles, extinct long before humanity, Challenger’s bass sounded from the platform: he challenged the statement. Waldron tried to continue, but the objection returned at every suitable moment. Students began anticipating it and shouting with the professor. The promised self-restraint had produced a magnificent scandal.

## question · The objection

In moving the vote of thanks, Challenger explained how superficial popular lectures were and how ignorant their audience. Then he declared that prehistoric monsters still lived and that he had personally seen them. People called him a liar. When he demanded to see the offender, students hoisted a completely innocent little man in spectacles. The professor’s indignation only increased their delight. Finally he offered a test: let the audience select representatives to travel to South America and establish the truth themselves.

## summerlee · The investigation

Professor Summerlee, a tall, lean, acid-tongued comparative anatomist, rose among the audience. He asked why Challenger had seen what earlier investigators had missed and demanded exact coordinates. Challenger explained the size of the Amazon as though Summerlee were confusing it with the Thames. He would disclose the place to a committee under specified conditions. Summerlee agreed to serve. Younger companions would be needed to check not only the original statement but also the investigator’s eventual report.

## volunteers · The volunteers

I sprang up before I had thought out my words. Here was Gladys’s opportunity. Henry tugged at my clothes, begging me not to make a fool of myself; I kept repeating that I would go. Lord John Roxton, the famous traveller and sportsman, rose at the same time. He had already been up the Amazon. They decided to send us both. As the meeting finally poured into the street, I glimpsed Challenger’s umbrella rising among the students, then his departing carriage. My life had acquired a direction it had not possessed that morning.

## rifle · The gun cupboard

At Roxton’s rooms it emerged that my shooting was indifferent. With some dismay Roxton opened his gun cupboard and selected a good magazine rifle for me. He handled each weapon almost affectionately. One recalled a rhinoceros, another his campaign against rubber slave-drivers. In a South American border district he had tried to protect people tortured into collecting rubber. Receiving threats for his trouble, he had armed fugitives and begun a war of his own. He had personally killed their leader, Pedro Lopez. Notches remained on the stock.

## quay · On the quay

The paper agreed that I should send letters to McArdle, with publication awaiting Challenger’s conditions. Telephone negotiations went badly. One conversation ended with his wife’s anxious request not to anger him; another with the crash of a broken instrument. On the quay before our departure he handed over a sealed envelope, to be opened at Manaos on the specified day and hour. I could write, but not reveal the country’s location; nothing was to be published until our return. He said farewell to each of us in his own distinctive fashion, especially wishing that Summerlee might grow wiser.

## river-party · The expedition’s people

Along the Amazon Roxton was known for his campaign against rubber slave-drivers. Some welcomed a protector; others remembered the profitable trade he had destroyed. He spoke the local common language, knew the country, and never tired of describing it. We engaged the powerful Zambo, who had worked on steamships and spoke a little English; Gomez and Manuel, experienced river men; and three boatmen from Bolivia, Mojo, Jose, and Fernando. Gomez also spoke English. Challenger’s own guides would soon join them.

## envelope · At the appointed hour

At the appointed noon we sat around the table. Roxton put his watch before him while Summerlee objected even to having to wait for the precise time. They opened the envelope and found a blank sheet. Turned over, it was blank again. I suggested invisible ink. Summerlee was already triumphant: the deceiver had admitted the deception. Then a familiar voice sounded from the veranda and Challenger appeared in the sunlit opening, wearing a straw hat, his hands in his pockets.

## arrival · A voice from the veranda

He had intended to arrive before the envelope was opened but had been delayed. From the first he had meant to supervise the investigation himself; had he told us earlier, we would have wanted to travel together. Summerlee assured him that this could easily have been avoided while another ship remained on the Atlantic. The exchange changed nothing. Challenger occupied a chair that creaked alarmingly beneath him and ordered the preparations completed. Instead of a map, we acquired the discoverer himself and the full weight of his command.

## guides · After the steam launch

In a hired steam launch we went upriver, turned into a tributary, and landed at a village. Rapids lay ahead, and Challenger sent the launch back. We pledged to conceal the exact route; my future maps could therefore preserve the relation of places without serving as directions. We prepared two light canoes, easy to carry, and engaged Ataca and Ipetu, who had accompanied the professor before. They feared repeating the journey, but their chief had accepted the bargain and their wishes counted for little.

## gomez-knife · By the hut

Trouble arose even before we left. Zambo discovered Gomez near the hut where we were discussing plans and brought him before us. Gomez drew a knife; Zambo disarmed him with one hand. After a reprimand both were made to reconcile. We hoped that ended it. Reconciliation between the scientists went less well. They quarrelled so constantly that we had to put them in separate canoes. Challenger dreamed of posthumous honour in Westminster Abbey; Summerlee pretended he meant the prison by the embankment.

## drums · The drums

The forest closed above us in an immense dark roof. Almost nothing moved below; life stirred far up in the sunlit crowns. Even Challenger lowered his voice. Then we heard drums. Gomez said we were being watched and would be killed if opportunity offered. The messages answered from different directions, although nobody appeared among the trees. Our boatmen froze with fear. The professors continued discussing plants and birds, turning briefly to an argument about the origins of our unseen neighbours.

## reed-gate · Beyond the reeds

We spent the night in midstream with heavy stones for anchors. There was no attack. Next we carried the canoes past the very rapids where Challenger had lost his evidence. The following day he recognized a leaning palm and pointed to pale reeds on the opposite bank. No passage showed in the solid green wall. We pushed the boats through the growth and emerged on a narrow, transparent river beneath interlacing branches. Behind the noise of our previous journey lay another world, green and quiet.

## canoes · The canoes stay behind

For three days we followed this corridor. Animals were unused to hunters, birds perched on fallen trunks, and fish showed through the water. Eventually it became too shallow. We hid the canoes in bushes, marked a tree, and divided the loads for walking. Here Summerlee refused a fresh order: Challenger, he reminded him, was not their commander but the man whose claims were on trial. Challenger sat down and invited his judges to find their own way. Roxton and I had to placate them both. Eventually we found a saving agreement: both detested Dr Illingworth of Edinburgh.

## bamboo · Through the bamboo

The ground rose, the forest changed, and swamps gave way to stony country. Sometimes Challenger disputed the direction with the guides. The party trusted them, and he soon recognized landmarks himself. The bamboo was worst: a whole day cutting through, a narrow strip of sky overhead, and Roxton’s back before me. We heard heavy animals beyond the stems but never saw them. When we reached open ground, exhaustion made an ordinary night’s camp almost blissful.

## distant-wings · Above the tree-ferns

A large grey creature rose above the tree-ferns. Challenger called it a pterodactyl; Summerlee cheerfully turned it into a stork. Roxton had caught it in his glasses and would not identify it, but it was no familiar bird. At last, beyond another ridge, the long wall of red cliffs appeared, just as in White’s picture. Challenger strutted in triumph. Summerlee fell silent without surrendering. Jose, whose arm had been injured by bamboo, was going back, and I sent a letter with him. One task still lay ahead: reaching the summit.

## cliffs · Beneath the wall

Close up, the cliffs seemed higher still. Their tops overhung, ruling out a direct climb. Beside them stood an isolated pinnacle crowned with a tree; Challenger had previously climbed only halfway. Beyond it the plateau was at the same height, but a chasm separated them. We decided to follow the wall in search of an ascent. Once a large snake emerged from the greenery above. Challenger seized Summerlee’s chin and made him look: here was proof of life on top. Summerlee was interested in the snake but did not consider it an excuse for such familiarity.

## chalk-arrows · Chalk arrows

Chalk arrows on the rocks led onwards to a narrow gorge. A cave darkened the wall high overhead, with another mark beside it. We climbed up after Roxton and his electric torch. The passage began almost level, then rose steeply. This seemed to be the Americans’ route. But a rockfall completely blocked it. When we pulled at the stones, others threatened to roll down upon us. We had to withdraw. Back below, an enormous rock passed very close to us. Gomez and Manuel insisted that it had come from the summit. We suspected someone up there disliked our arrival.

## supper · Supper by the fire

That evening Roxton had shot a small animal, and we were cooking our share by the fire. Suddenly enormous leathery wings swept out of the darkness. For a moment I saw a long neck, a red eye, and a toothed beak. The creature took our supper and vanished over the cliffs. We sat silent. Then Summerlee solemnly apologized to Challenger: he had been wrong. For the first time they shook hands. Supper was lost, but stubborn disbelief had finally ended.

## circuit · Around the cliffs

We continued our circuit. Over several days we encountered bare stone, swamp slime, and snakes from which we had to run. The walls changed colour and height but offered no approach. I asked where the rainwater went, since there were no waterfalls outside. The professors proposed an internal lake and began discussing drainage and evaporation. After six days we were back at the first camp. The only discovered passage was blocked. Supplies remained, but the rains would not wait for us to invent the impossible.

## pinnacle · The isolated pinnacle

Next morning Challenger was radiant. He pointed at the pinnacle and proposed that we first climb it. I could see only the next chasm, but he promised further intellectual resources. With rope and climbing equipment we began. The upper stretches made me cling with my fingers to tiny ledges. The professor reached the tree first and secured the rope, without which I could scarcely have managed. From the little platform our journey spread out far below. The plateau was close, roughly level with us, across an impassable gap.

## beech · The beech

The tree was a beech. Summerlee welcomed a familiar acquaintance, Challenger declared it an ally, and Roxton finally understood: a bridge. Properly felled, the trunk would span the chasm. Under the professor’s direction Roxton and I worked with the axe until the tree crashed into the opposite vegetation. The severed base shifted dangerously near the edge but held. Challenger wanted immediately to enter the unknown land first. Roxton stopped him: weapons and provisions before ceremony.

## crossing · Along the trunk

We climbed down and brought the guns; Gomez and Manuel helped raise the supplies. Then Challenger straddled the trunk and worked his way over. Summerlee followed with two rifles, then I crossed. Roxton walked along as though there were no abyss beneath him. All four of us were on the plateau and had moved into the bushes when a crash sounded behind us. We rushed back. The bridge already lay shattered far below.

## gomez-revenge · On the other side

At first we thought the edge had crumbled. Then Gomez’s face appeared above the opposite platform, distorted with triumph and hatred. He called to Roxton and explained how long he had waited. The stone at the cave had been their attempt too. This would be better: a slow death up there, without burial or rescue. Lopez, whom Roxton had killed, was his brother. A name that had meant a past victory over an enslaver in a London room had returned with our destroyed road home.

## broken-bridge · Far below

Roxton ran along the edge to a place overlooking the descending Gomez and fired. We heard a cry and a distant impact. He returned blaming himself: he should have remembered the possibility of revenge. He had let Manuel go, not knowing his precise involvement. But below, Zambo was already pursuing the fugitive. We watched them fall together, then Zambo rose; the other man remained motionless. Neither death repaired anything. No suitable tree grew near our edge, and our rope could not reach the ground.

## zambo-rope · A rope across the chasm

Zambo climbed the pinnacle and asked what he should do. He would not leave us, but he could not keep the boatmen much longer. Under our directions he threw the rope across and helped haul over food, ammunition, and useful equipment. Then he returned to the camp below. We stayed by the edge, drank bottled mineral water, and dared not enter the forest at night. I wrote by lantern-light: we now belonged to the plateau. The road to the canoes was visible, but one missing trunk separated us from our former lives.

## fort · The clearing and spring

We found a small clearing with a spring beneath an enormous ginkgo. We gathered the stores and surrounded the place with thorn bushes. With four rifles, a shotgun, ammunition, and provisions, we could last several weeks. We named our refuge Fort Challenger and the country Maple White Land. Roxton insisted upon caution: we should know our neighbours before they knew us. Firing must be reserved for emergencies. On our first journeys the stream from the spring would provide our route back.

## iguanodons · The family in the glade

An enormous three-toed foot had marked the soft ground. Roxton took it for the print of an unheard-of bird, but a five-fingered forefoot appeared among the tracks. The professors grew excited and we cautiously continued. Beyond the bushes two adult iguanodons and three young ones were feeding. Even the youngsters were immense. They gambolled clumsily around their parents; one adult, unable to reach some leaves, tore down a tree and brought it upon its own head. It yelped indignantly. Then the entire family moved off into the forest.

## rookery · The hollow

Further on we heard whistling and gabbling. Beyond some rocks a hollow opened, with water, reeds, eggs, and hundreds of pterodactyls. Young ones and females crowded below; males kept watch on stones. A heavy smell rose from it. The professors would gladly have spent all day observing the colony and disputing its arrangements. Arguing a point, Challenger raised his head too far. The nearest sentinel cried and flew up; the others followed. Very soon the magnificent spectacle had surrounded us in a narrowing ring.

## wings · Between the wings

We retreated towards the wood, striking with our gunstocks, but could scarcely hit the rapid wings. A beak cut Summerlee’s face; something struck my neck. Challenger fell, and when I bent to help, I fell upon him. Roxton’s heavy rifle crashed. One creature struggled on the ground and the others rose, giving us time to reach the trees. There the wings had no room. We escaped limping and holding our wounds; the pursuers circled above the canopy for a long time.

## first-raid · Belongings on the ground

Fresh anxiety waited at home. The thorn hedge and entrance were intact, but something had scattered our belongings, crushed a meat-tin, and torn apart a cartridge. There were no tracks on the ground; a ginkgo branch overhung the camp. Zambo’s voice from the opposite pinnacle reminded us that ordinary people still existed. Later Roxton questioned me about the pterodactyl hollow. He was particularly interested in the bluish clay beside the water but gave no explanation. Even as I fell asleep, I heard him repeating something about clay in a volcanic vent.

## fever · After the wounds

Next day Summerlee and I suffered pain and fever, while Challenger could scarcely use his injured leg. Roxton strengthened the thorn wall. I could not shake off the sense that we were watched; the professor attributed it to fever. That night a monstrous cry woke us. For several minutes the forest sounded with agony and a low growl like laughter. Then everything stopped. We sat by the fire, hardly daring to speak, when heavy, soft footsteps sounded outside.

## firebrand · The burning branch

The creature stopped at our entrance. We could make out a dense shadow and hear its breathing. We seized our rifles, but Roxton told us to save the shot for the last extremity: the sound would betray us for miles, and a wounded beast might charge through the hedge. He took a burning branch from the fire and went out alone. Its light briefly revealed a warty muzzle with blood around the mouth. Roxton thrust the flame into its face, and the beast crashed away into the undergrowth. From that night we kept watches.

## departure-debate · The evening argument

That evening Summerlee demanded that we finally seek an exit. The committee’s work was done: Challenger was right. Now we must bring the result home before it perished with the investigators. At first I regretted all the unused newspaper material, but his argument was reasonable. Challenger too disliked losing his chance to refute his enemies for ever. He agreed to address the return after at least a general survey. He refused to leave without a map. Yet two days in the thick forest had shown us little beyond the nearest trunks.

## face · Among the branches

Then I looked at our enormous ginkgo. On the rocks I had been inferior to my companions; I had climbed trees since childhood. Above the forest, a view might supply what weeks of wandering could not. They hoisted me to the lowest branch; Challenger’s particularly energetic shove nearly sent me beyond it. I climbed. Beside a dense growth I suddenly encountered a face: pale, almost human, with a heavy jaw and bared teeth. It expressed hatred, then fear. A reddish hairy body crashed away through the leaves.

## panorama · Above the forest

I nearly went back down. But returning without a result after so much effort seemed humiliating. I climbed further, survived a moment on a rotten branch, and finally settled above every other crown. The country lay like a bowl beneath me, wooded slopes descending to a great central lake. Across it stood inner red cliffs with dark cave openings. Strange long living bodies lay on the sandbanks. Until the light failed I sketched the map, noting the iguanodon glade, the hollow, and the relative positions of the shores.

## lake-name · A name on the map

Below, they greeted me as a hero. For once both the idea and its execution had belonged entirely to me. I described the face in the branches; Challenger questioned me about fingers and a tail, while Summerlee again insisted that escape was our chief duty. That evening we drew the map properly. I was allowed to name the lake and chose Gladys. Summerlee would have preferred a descriptive Central Lake. I wanted the girl for whom I had come there to leave her name for ever upon the distant plateau.

## night-pride · A sleepless night

Pride and excitement kept me awake. Summerlee nodded by the fire, Roxton lay under his poncho, and Challenger filled the forest with snores. In the cold moonlit air an idea formed: what if I visited the lake alone and returned by breakfast with fresh discoveries? My companions would value me still more. Gladys would hear of a genuine deed, McArdle would receive a splendid article, and perhaps I might be trusted with future war reporting. I took a gun, parted the thorns at the entrance, and slipped out.

## shotgun · The gun in my hand

Before I had gone a hundred yards, I regretted it. The black spaces between the trees seemed hiding-places for monsters. I remembered the night’s cry and the bloodstained muzzle. Shame before myself prevented retreat. When I opened the weapon to load it, I discovered another problem: in my haste I had taken the shotgun instead of a rifle. Here was a sensible reason to abandon the walk. But returning would require explanations, and I wanted the venture to remain my own. Again I went on, listening to the stream, my thread back to camp.

## cave-lights · Red circles

After one in the morning I reached the great lake, drank, and climbed an isolated lava block. Reddish circles glowed on the opposite cliffs. At first I took them for volcanic light, but they were high above the shore and sometimes obscured, as if someone passed before them. They were fires inside the caves. People lived on the plateau. My reckless venture had suddenly acquired a substantial justification: I could bring my companions more than another description of water.

## stegosaurus · The drinking-place

The lake lived beneath me. Rings spread across it, fish flashed, backs curved above the surface. A long-necked creature slid from a sandbank and dived. Large armadillo-like animals came to drink, followed by a magnificent deer with its family. They took alarm and disappeared. Something new was coming down the path: a heavy body, arched back, rows of plates, and a small head held low. I recognized the stegosaurus from Maple White’s sketchbook. It drank so near that I might have reached towards its back. Then it departed, leaving me on the rock with the incredible sight in my memory.

## pursuer · A sound behind

On the return I was already proud of my discoveries. Then a low croak sounded behind me. I hurried; after a while it returned, closer. At last an enormous beast came out of the bushes onto the moonlit path. It moved on powerful hind legs, occasionally dropping to sniff my track. At first I wanted to believe it was a familiar, harmless iguanodon. The broad toad-like face and unmistakable pursuit removed that hope. There was no sufficiently large tree or rock nearby.

## running · On the animal path

I threw away the useless shotgun and ran along a well-trodden animal path. When I stopped for breath, I briefly thought the pursuit was over. Then branches cracked and footsteps thundered: now the beast could see me and was overtaking me in bounds. I heard its breathing almost at my shoulder. Another effort, a few more steps, and the ground collapsed beneath my feet. I fell through space and lost consciousness.

## pit · A circle of sky

I woke to an appalling smell. There were flesh and bones beneath my hands, a circle of sky overhead. Feeling myself, I discovered that I could move. Rotten remains covered the bottom of a pit, with a tall sharpened stake in its centre. By the light of a match I understood: this was a human-made trap for large animals. Miraculously, I had missed the point. Earlier prey had been cut up here to clear it for the next victim. A pit from which an animal could not escape still offered a climb for a man.

## pit-exit · Before dawn

I was afraid to show myself above. I took comfort from the professors’ discussion of the great reptiles’ limited intelligence: surely my pursuer could not understand my disappearance and wait patiently. Eventually I climbed to the edge. Nothing moved in the silence before dawn. I sat ready to jump back, then went to find the stream. On the way I retrieved the gun I had thrown aside. I wanted neither fame nor fresh encounters now. I wanted to reach familiar people and hear their voices.

## empty-camp · The open entrance

One sharp rifle-shot sounded far away. At first I feared for my companions, then supposed that they had missed me and were signalling. I hurried as fast as my tired legs allowed. Seeing the familiar clearing, I called a greeting. Nothing answered. The enclosure stood as I had left it; only the entrance was open. Our belongings lay in confusion. A large pool of blood darkened the grass beside the dying fire. All three companions were gone.

## spent-case · The abandoned rifles

I rushed through the woods calling until despair gave way to an effort to understand. The rifles remained, and Roxton’s held a spent cartridge. The blankets suggested that the professors had been asleep. Stores and cameras were scattered, exposed food had disappeared. What could carry off the men while leaving their weapons? I found no explanation. Once I lost my own way and struggled back. Only then did I remember that I was not entirely alone: Zambo was below.

## messenger · A letter below

Another man was with him, a returning boatman whose companions had robbed him of his earnings. He was ready to carry a letter and bring ropes. Hearing my account from the pinnacle, Zambo urged me to descend, but no usable crossing existed. I wrote about the disaster, added an appeal to any trader or captain the messenger might meet to send help, and provided money. That evening I passed the packet down. My friends had vanished; for the moment I could only send word of them and of what we had seen into the world.
