## cover · Treasure Island

A stranger brings a chest to a coastal inn. Jim Hawkins does not yet know who is pursuing him or where the hidden papers will lead.

Essential and Core are unresolved openings through the stockade battle. The continuation and ending open in Deep after a warning.

## inn-arrival · The lodger

The sailor walked up to my father's inn, the Admiral Benbow, with his chest following in a handcart. A pale scar crossed his cheek; a pigtail hung over his dirty blue coat. He drank some rum, surveyed our cove, and asked whether we had many visitors. Hearing that we had few, he stayed. He gave no name, told us to call him captain, and threw down several gold coins for his lodging. By day he wandered along the coast with a telescope; in the evenings he drank silently beside the fire. When a seaman appeared on the road, he peered into the room from behind a curtain before entering. He promised me, Jim Hawkins, a silver coin each month to watch for a man with one leg. Soon the stranger entered my dreams, jumping hedges and chasing me, more terrible every time.

## inn-evening · An evening at the inn

When drunk, the captain made our guests sing with him and listen to stories of hangings, storms, and robbery. Nobody dared leave before he fell asleep. Father feared he would ruin the inn, although some of the younger neighbours rather enjoyed being frightened. Ruin threatened for another reason: the first payment was long exhausted, and Father could not bring himself to demand more. He was already seriously ill. Dr Livesey came to see him one evening and continued talking calmly through the captain's orders for silence. The sailor sprang up with a knife. Livesey did not even rise: he reminded him that he was a magistrate as well as a doctor, and that threatening someone with a knife could lead to hanging. The captain looked away first, put the weapon away, and remained quiet for several evenings.

## winter-visitor · A winter visitor

One frosty January morning, while the captain was out, a pale stranger arrived with two fingers missing from his left hand. He asked for his friend Bill, made me wait near the door, and suddenly called out to our returning lodger. Our terrifying captain went blue. He called the stranger Black Dog. I brought them rum and withdrew. At first I heard murmuring, then curses, overturned furniture, and clashing blades. Black Dog burst outside with a bleeding shoulder. The captain's last blow struck our signboard, and the fugitive vanished over the hill. Back inside, the captain demanded rum and talked of leaving, then collapsed. Mother and I searched for a wound, but Livesey arrived and said it was a stroke.

## upstairs · Upstairs

The doctor had me hold a basin while he bled the patient. On the exposed arm I saw lettering and a drawing of a gallows; one inscription named Billy Bones. When he opened his eyes, his first question was about Black Dog. Livesey told him that his own drinking was the immediate danger. He helped get Bones upstairs and ordered him to stay in bed for at least a week; another stroke, he warned, would be his last. The doctor went to my father, while I was left with a name for our captain and the memory of the fear on a face that had frightened everyone in our house.

## rum-request · At the bedside

Bones begged a glass of rum from me: his fingers shook, he imagined Flint in a corner, and without a drink he threatened to disturb the whole house. Afraid for Father's peace, I gave in but refused his offered guinea. Hearing that he must spend a week in bed, he tried to rise and fell back exhausted. By then, he said, they would find him and deliver the black spot. He had been Flint's mate; his former shipmates wanted the chest containing what their captain had given him before dying in Savannah. If Black Dog, the one-legged man, or the spot appeared, I must ride to Livesey and summon the authorities. But Father died that evening. The funeral, visitors, and work pushed everything else aside. Bones drank downstairs again and moved about with his hands against the walls for support.

## blind-visitor · The stranger's hand

The day after Father's funeral, a blind man in a ragged cloak approached the inn. In the voice of a helpless beggar he asked for my hand, then gripped it so fiercely I nearly cried out. He ordered me to take him to the captain; when I warned him about the cutlass, he threatened to break my arm. At his command I brought him to Bones and put their hands together. He passed something across, then made his way nimbly back to the road. Bones looked at what he had received: he had until ten that night. Six hours still; he could escape them! He jumped up, clutched his throat, and fell face down. This time nothing could be done. I cried, although I had never liked him: the first death in our house was still too recent.

## hamlet · In the hamlet

Mother and I ran to the neighbouring hamlet for help. People readily offered to fetch the doctor and provide horses for our escape, but nobody would return with us: they knew Flint's name. They gave me a loaded pistol. Mother refused to lose money owed to her fatherless son. She borrowed a bag for her lawful payment, and we went back alone. Inside the bolted inn I picked up a black paper circle beside the body. On its reverse was indeed the deadline of ten o'clock. Our clock struck six. There was no key in his pockets; I forced myself to open his collar and found it hanging from a cord around his neck. Upstairs we opened the chest, which smelled of tar and tobacco.

## chest · Over the chest

Beneath a new suit lay weapons, instruments, silver, foreign objects, and shells. At the very bottom, under an old cloak, we found an oilskin packet and a bag of coins. Mother would take exactly what was owed. But amongst the doubloons, louis d'or, and unfamiliar money there were few English guineas, so counting was painfully slow. Outside, the blind man's stick began tapping. He tried our locked door, waited, and went away. I begged her to take everything and run; she still wanted to finish counting. Only a whistle from the hill brought her to her feet. She took the counted money, I took the packet, and we left the candle beside the chest and slipped into the fog. On the road Mother began to faint. At the little bridge I dragged her down the bank, but could not get her entirely under cover.

## road · On the road

I peered from behind a bush. Seven or eight men were running towards the inn, leading the blind man between them by the hands. He was called Pew, and he commanded them all. Inside they found Bill dead and the chest disturbed upstairs. Money remained, but Pew wanted Flint's papers. He immediately suspected me and ordered a search of the house and its surroundings. Another whistle came from the hill, twice this time. The sailors wanted to leave; Pew shouted about the thousands they were throwing away and struck them with his stick. Their quarrel bought us time. Hooves thundered, a shot came from the hedge, and the gang scattered. Left alone in the road, the blind man blundered about calling his companions and ran under a horse. Its rider tried to stop, but Pew was killed.

## revenue · After the pursuit

They were revenue officers led by Mr Dance. The boy sent for Livesey had met them on the road. Mother was carried to the hamlet and revived; she still regretted the unpaid balance. Dance's men reached the little cove where the pirates' vessel lay, but it was already leaving. Someone aboard threatened to shoot and then did shoot. Back at the inn I saw broken possessions and our clock thrown down. The packet was still inside my coat. Dance offered to take it, but I named Dr Livesey. He decided to bring both me and the papers: Pew's death had to be reported to a magistrate anyway. I said goodbye to Mother and climbed up behind one of the riders.

## library · In the library

The doctor was not at home; he was dining with Squire Trelawney. We found them in the library beside the fire. Dance's account excited the squire so much that he broke his pipe, and Mother's courage particularly delighted him. I was given pie, the revenue officer was refreshed and sent away, and Livesey cut the packet's stitching with his medical scissors. It contained Bones's notebook, full of sums, dates, and crosses, and a sealed sheet. Trelawney took the notebook for an account of pirate loot. Under the seals was an island: bays, soundings, hills, and three red crosses. Beside one was a note marking the main treasure. The back held directions for finding it, with further notes about silver and weapons.

## expedition · New plans

Trelawney could no longer sit still. He would buy a ship in Bristol, Livesey would be its doctor, and I would be cabin boy. We would take Redruth, Hunter, and Joyce, find the gold, and become rich! Livesey agreed, but named one man he feared. The squire demanded the villain's name. The doctor named Trelawney himself: he could not keep his mouth shut. Determined and dangerous men were hunting these papers. Until we sailed, we must not go out alone, and nobody must hear about the discovery. The squire promised complete silence. I had already remembered the shape of the island and could now visit it even beside a fire at home. Every path across the map was still open.

## letter · A letter from Bristol

The preparations took longer than expected. Livesey went to find a replacement for his practice, Trelawney worked in Bristol, and I stayed under grumbling Redruth's supervision, exploring the island in my imagination. At last a letter arrived. The schooner Hispaniola had been bought, and a crew recruited with the help of a remarkable one-legged sailor, John Silver, landlord of the Spy-glass. The squire had hired him as cook and was delighted: the best seamen seemed to appear as soon as he did. The letter also revealed that all Bristol knew about the treasure. I tried pointing out that the doctor would dislike this, but Redruth could not imagine who had the right to forbid the squire from talking. A postscript told me to visit Mother and hurry to the ship.

## farewell · Before leaving

Trelawney had paid for the inn to be repaired, and Mother now had an armchair and a boy to help her. Only when I saw him in my place did I truly understand that I was leaving. My tears did not prevent me from finding fault with the clumsy newcomer: I missed no opportunity to correct him. Next morning Redruth and I left, and the mail coach later carried us to Bristol. Walking along the quays, I stared at masts, carved figureheads, sailors' earrings, ropes, and men high above the decks. I had lived by the sea all my life, yet felt I was approaching it for the first time. The squire met us in a blue seagoing outfit, already copying a sailor's rolling walk. We were to sail tomorrow.

## spyglass · At the Spy-glass

I brought the squire's note to the Spy-glass. The place was clean, with red curtains, and its tall, one-legged landlord moved cleverly amongst the tables, joking and clapping acquaintances on the shoulder. After Bones and Pew, I thought I knew what pirates looked like. This pleasant man was quite different. Silver shook my hand, and at that very moment a customer bolted for the door. I recognised Black Dog and shouted. Silver sent men after him, expressed horror that a robber had been drinking in his house, and questioned an old sailor named Morgan. Supposedly Morgan knew neither the man's name nor much of their conversation, beyond keel-hauling. The landlord cursed, hurried about on his crutch, and urged on the pursuit.

## quays · Along the quays

They did not catch the fugitive. Silver worried what the squire would think and asked me to confirm how it had happened. Then he remembered the unpaid bill for three drinks. He laughed so hard at his own forgetfulness that I joined in. We went to report together. Along the way he explained the ships we passed, described the work in the port, and taught me nautical expressions. By the time we met Trelawney and Livesey, I was ready to guarantee his honesty. His account was lively and accurate, with constant appeals to me for confirmation; I could confirm every word. The doctor approved of the squire's discovery, and I was already delighted to have such a shipmate.

## captain · A cabin conversation

On the Hispaniola we found a man who liked almost nothing: Captain Smollett. Hired for a secret mission, he had learnt its purpose from the crew. He had not chosen the sailors, the mate Arrow was too familiar with them, and the secret of the map was already abroad. Trelawney grew angry; Livesey made both men hear each other out. The captain wanted powder and weapons moved aft, the squire's people berthed nearby, and the original map kept secret even from himself. He did not claim that the crew would certainly mutiny, but he was responsible for the ship and would not sail without these precautions. The squire reluctantly agreed. The doctor said we now had at least two honest men aboard: Smollett and Silver.

## berths · Before departure

We shifted weapons and sleeping arrangements. The cook arrived with the last sailors, noticed the work with the powder, and worried that we would miss the tide. Smollett curtly told him to prepare supper; Silver obeyed at once. I was examining our gun when the captain drove me to work too, announcing loudly that he would have no favourites aboard. I disliked these new arrangements. Trelawney found the captain intolerable, and I entirely agreed with him. In the galley, by contrast, there was someone willing to talk, who knew everything about ships and never tired of being kind to me.

## departure · The passage begins

Before dawn the boatswain's whistle sounded, the men heaved at the capstan, and Silver began the very song with which Bones had tormented our inn. For a moment I seemed to hear the captain's voice again. Then the anchor rose, the sails filled, and the coast slipped away behind us. The passage generally went well: the schooner proved excellent, the seamen skilful, and Smollett knew his work. Only Arrow increasingly appeared drunk, lost control of the crew, fell about, and lay in his bunk. Nobody discovered where he got the liquor. One dark night he disappeared and was never seen again. Some of his duties fell to the boatswain Anderson; the squire and the experienced Israel Hands helped too.

## galley · Galley and barrel

At sea Silver cooked with his crutch braced against a partition and crossed the deck using ropes specially rigged for him. He found ways to do everyone a favour, and the sailors listened to him. He often invited me into his spotless galley. His parrot, Captain Flint, screeched about pieces of eight and swore. Silver claimed the bird had witnessed immense treasures and had been travelling the seas for nearly two hundred years. I loved listening. The squire spoiled the crew with treats, and an open barrel of apples always stood amidships. Towards the end of the passage I climbed inside for an apple. Few remained at the bottom, and I grew drowsy in the darkness with the movement of the ship. Someone sat down heavily beside it, leaned against the barrel, and spoke in Silver's voice. Within a few words I no longer dared move.

## dick · Voices on the other side

Silver was telling a young sailor, Dick, that he had served with pirates: first England, then Flint, whose quartermaster he had been. Unlike his companions, he had saved his money. His wife now had it, the tavern was sold, and after this voyage he intended to live as a gentleman. He flattered Dick with the same words he had used to win me. I listened and hated him. The young man agreed; their handshake even shook my barrel. Hands joined them. He had had enough of Smollett and wanted the cabin and its fine wine. Silver checked him. They still needed the captain to plot a course and the owners of the map to find the gold and help load it. The ship must be seized at the last possible moment, not now.

## apple · Keep still

Asked what they should do with our people afterwards, the cook chose death. He did not want witnesses returning while he was riding about in his carriage. He claimed Trelawney for himself. Then Silver asked Dick to fetch an apple. I lay at the bottom, unable to get up; Hands saved me by proposing rum instead. The cook provided the key to the keg. While Dick was away, I caught a few more words: none of the others would join. So loyal men remained aboard. The plotters drank to luck, Flint, and themselves, and a cry of land came from above. Moonlight fell across the edge of the barrel. Everyone hurried to look at the island, and I finally had a chance to climb out unseen.

## chart-copy · In the moonlight

On deck Silver was already explaining the entrance to the harbour. Smollett showed him a clean copy of the chart, without the red crosses or the treasure directions; the cook concealed his disappointment. Then he took my arm in friendship, promised food for my island excursions, and talked of bathing and climbing hills. I could scarcely keep from shuddering. Under an innocent pretext the doctor arranged a meeting in the cabin, and I told him, the squire, and the captain everything I had heard. They seated me at the table, gave me raisins and wine, and drank my health. Trelawney admitted his mistake and said he would await Smollett's orders. The captain did not boast of foresight either: few crews could conceal a conspiracy so well.

## council · Behind a closed door

We could not turn back; that would bring an immediate mutiny. We had time until the gold was found, and must use it to strike when least expected. For the moment we could be sure only of the captain, the doctor, the squire, his three servants, and me. Seven out of twenty-six, and I was a boy. Livesey hoped the sailors would speak more freely in front of me; Trelawney placed tremendous confidence in me. Their words only frightened me more, because I felt entirely helpless. Ordinary shipboard life continued above us, the island lay dark outside, and we still did not know which men on deck could be kept from murder.

## anchorage · Off the island

In the morning I saw grey woods, yellow sand, and bare rocky peaks. The island looked nothing like the place I had longed to reach. Without wind the ship rolled in the swell, making me sick, while surf thundered endlessly beyond the shore. We had to tow the schooner in boats to the southern anchorage behind Skeleton Island. The rowers cursed, and Anderson cursed with them. Silver confidently directed the passage. The enclosed bay smelled of rotting leaves; the doctor feared fever. Sailors sprawled on deck and answered orders so badly that another confrontation threatened open rebellion. The cook, meanwhile, sang, smiled, and obeyed every command with enthusiasm. It was clear how desperately he was trying to restrain his men from acting too soon.

## boats · Towards shore

Smollett allowed anyone who wished to spend the rest of the day ashore. This would give Silver time to reason with his people, and us a breathing space. Six sailors stayed aboard; thirteen, including the cook, took to the boats. Without permission I climbed into one, deciding that I could do little to help my companions seize the ship just then. Silver noticed me from the other boat and called out, and I regretted my idea at once. Our boat was ahead. As soon as it touched the trees along the shore, I caught a branch, swung myself out, and plunged into the undergrowth. Someone called behind me, but I ran, ducked, and broke through bushes, stopping only when I was completely out of breath.

## marsh · Voices by the marsh

After escaping Silver, I briefly enjoyed my freedom. Unfamiliar plants grew around me and snakes slipped amongst the stones; one raised its head and rattled. I did not then understand the danger I had avoided. Birds rose in alarm beyond the marsh, and human voices followed their cries. I hid under low branches and crawled closer. Beside the swamp Silver was persuading a sailor called Tom, supposedly warning a special friend that resistance was useless. Tom still addressed him as an honest man and refused to abandon his duty. In the distance came an angry cry, then another, then a long scream. The birds rose again, and echoes rang amongst the rocks. Silver calmly said that it must be Alan.

## tom · Amongst the trees

Tom declared that they were shipmates no longer and turned towards the beach. Silver caught a branch, pulled out his crutch, and hurled it into Tom's back. The sailor fell. The cook reached him in an instant and stabbed him twice. Trees, sunlight, and mountain spun before my eyes. When I could understand what was happening again, Silver was standing on his crutch, wiping the blade with grass. Then he took out a whistle. I crawled backwards, and when his accomplices answered I ran. Returning to the boats now seemed impossible: after Tom and Alan, they would kill me. I was cut off from the ship, my friends were far away, and before me lay only strange country in which to hide for as long as I had the strength.

## islander · Someone ahead

Gravel rattled down the rocky hillside. A shaggy creature flashed amongst the trees, perhaps a man, perhaps an animal. I turned back, but it circled to cut me off, moving faster than I could run. Remembering my pistol, I gathered courage and walked towards it. The stranger withdrew, approached again, and unexpectedly fell to his knees. His name was Ben Gunn. He had not spoken to a Christian for three years: his companions had marooned him. Sticks and scraps of rope held his clothing together; his skin was burnt dark. He felt my jacket, delighted in my shoes, and talked about the goats, oysters, and berries that kept him alive. Above all he wanted cheese, particularly toasted cheese; he dreamed of it at night. I promised some if I got back to the ship.

## ben-account · Ben's account

Ben recalled his pious mother, promised to reform, and mysteriously insisted that he was rich. Solitude must have driven him mad, I thought. But the mention of Flint's men and one-legged Silver made him instantly serious. I described our predicament, and Ben offered help: would the squire give him a thousand pounds and take him home? I was sure he would. Ben had sailed with Flint. He had seen the captain go ashore with six men and return alone; the others aboard never learnt where the plunder lay. Later Ben had brought another crew here to search for it. After twelve fruitless days, they left him a musket, a pick, a spade, and the opportunity to continue alone.

## white-rock · A cannon sounds

He wanted me to repeat mysterious words to the squire about his activities on the island, pinching me to make them more convincing. I understood little, but heard something useful: beneath a white rock was a boat he had built himself. If things became desperate, we could try it at night. Then a cannon fired. I ran towards the anchorage, and Ben trotted easily beside me, showing the way. Musket fire followed, and soon a British flag rose over the woods. I wanted to reach my friends, but Gunn held my arm. Who stood beneath the flag, and what had happened aboard, I did not yet know.

## spring · The doctor: reconnaissance

While Jim wandered about the island, we aboard the Hispaniola were considering what to do with the six sailors left behind. There was no wind: even if we took the ship, we could not sail away. Learning that the boy had gone with the others alarmed us still more. I, Dr Livesey, went ashore with Hunter to investigate. The boat guards stayed beside their vessels while we disappeared behind a bend and landed near the fort marked on the chart. There was a strong log house with loopholes, surrounded by a tall stockade. Most important, a spring rose inside it. Aboard we had arms, food, and wine, but no water for a siege. At that moment a death cry came from the woods. Thinking Jim had been killed, I hurried back.

## cargo · The doctor: supplies

We immediately planned our move. With loaded muskets, Redruth blocked the passage between the cabin and the forward part of the ship; the captain warned Hands that any signal ashore would be answered with gunfire. Joyce and I loaded powder, pork, biscuit, weapons, and my medicine chest. We safely delivered the first cargo, passed it over the fence, and left Hunter and Joyce guarding the house. I returned for the others. The boat guards were now alarmed, and one ran to his companions. We hurriedly filled our boat with another load; spare muskets and powder had to be thrown overboard. Through the water we could see the steel shining on the sandy bottom.

## gray · The doctor: before leaving

Before leaving, Smollett called Abraham Gray. He believed him a decent man and gave him half a minute to follow his captain. At first there was no answer, then a scuffle, and Gray ran from the forward quarters with a cut cheek. He jumped in, and Smollett followed. Five grown men, provisions, and ammunition occupied the little boat; two of our people were already waiting in the fort. We were clear of the ship, but the ebb pulled us sideways, and returning pirates might appear on the shore between us and the house.

## current · The doctor: the current

Water almost came over the stern. The captain rearranged our weight, but we could not row harder without swamping the boat. The ebb swept us past our landing place. We had to point almost across our intended course, gradually escaping the strongest stream. Gray told us where it was weakening, and we accepted his advice as though he had always been one of us. Then the captain noticed the gun. Five men were working around it aboard the Hispaniola; we had forgotten to destroy its charges and shot. Hands had been Flint's gunner. Trelawney, our best marksman, aimed at him. We stopped rowing and leaned over to balance the boat. At the instant of the shot Hands stooped, and the bullet struck another man.

## last-trip · The doctor: towards land

Shouts came from the beach, and men ran for the boats. We were nearing a strip of sand where we could land, but now offered the cannon our whole broadside. The squire watched for the match. At his warning the captain and Redruth abruptly checked the boat; the gun fired at the same moment. None of us knew exactly where its ball went. Our boat gently sank by the stern. Fortunately the water was waist-deep or less: we stood, the others surfaced, and everyone could reach land. Only two muskets remained dry. Half our supplies lay underwater; voices already sounded amongst the trees, and the fort was still ahead.

## redruth · The doctor: by the stockade

We hurried through the woods. The squire took the captain's dry musket, and I gave Gray my cutlass. Near the stockade seven men led by Anderson almost caught us. We fired, with Hunter and Joyce joining from the house; one attacker fell and the others disappeared. When we approached the dead man, another shot came from the bushes. Redruth collapsed. We carried him over the fence, but I saw at once that the old gamekeeper could not be saved. The squire knelt, kissed his hand, and begged forgiveness. Redruth wondered whether it was proper for a servant to forgive his master, but agreed. Then he asked for a prayer to be read: it was customary. Soon afterwards he died. Trelawney cried like a child.

## flag · The doctor: beneath the flag

Meanwhile the captain emptied his swollen pockets of a flag, a Bible, tobacco, and writing materials, set up a tree trunk with Hunter, and raised the British colours. He covered Redruth with another flag. On short rations we had only ten days' food, and no search vessel would be sent before the end of August. The ship began firing at our flag. Smollett refused to lower it. Most cannonballs passed over the house or buried themselves in sand; one pierced the roof and floor. Gradually we grew used to them. We could not recover the submerged supplies: Silver was already directing their removal into his own boat. Then familiar calls came from inland. Jim Hawkins approached the fence, alive and unharmed.

## jim-return · Jim: amongst friends again

Beside the flag, Ben Gunn had insisted that my friends were there: Silver would certainly have flown black colours. He would not come himself without an honourable promise from a real gentleman. The doctor or squire must meet Ben alone, carrying something white, and hear him out. A cannonball interrupted us, and we scattered. I made a wide circuit. From the shore I saw the pirate flag on the Hispaniola, drunken activity around a fire, and a white rock on the spit. When the bombardment ended, I reached the stockade. My friends had abandoned the schooner and moved some supplies to the log house with its spring; Redruth was dead. They welcomed me warmly, and I reported my meeting with Gunn and his hidden boat.

## sand · Sand and smoke

Wind sifted fine sand through the house. It gritted in our teeth, entered our food, and danced in the spring water. Smoke escaped poorly through the hole in the roof, bringing tears to our eyes. Gray sat with his cheek bandaged, Redruth's body against the wall. The captain kept us busy: he divided watches, sent men for firewood and to dig a grave, and appointed the doctor cook. At the doorway Livesey questioned me about Ben. I doubted his sanity; the doctor thought that unsurprising after three years alone. Hearing about cheese delighted him: he carried a piece of Parmesan in his snuffbox. Now he had something for Gunn.

## morning · The next morning

We buried Redruth before supper. Our leaders conferred: with few provisions, we must protect our own people and reduce the enemy's numbers, hoping drink and illness would weaken them too. Beyond the trees the pirates shouted songs late into the night. The captain had lost a ship for the first time. After such a day I nevertheless slept soundly. Voices woke me, speaking of a white flag. Two men stood outside the fence. One held the cloth; the other was Silver in his best blue coat. He had come to talk, now on behalf of the men who had elected him captain.

## truce · A white flag

Smollett recognised no Captain Silver, but allowed the cook to enter. Silver threw his crutch across the fence, climbed over, and struggled up through the sand and stumps. No invitation indoors followed: he had to sit outside the threshold. He spoke of a night attack in which one of his men had been struck on the head. Smollett did not know what he meant but gave no sign; I remembered Ben's hints. The cook proposed a bargain. We would surrender the map and stop attacking; they would find the gold. Then they would either put us safely ashore somewhere or leave us here with part of the stores and send help from the first ship they encountered. He spoke loudly enough for everyone inside to hear.

## terms · At the threshold

The captain smoked patiently, then gave his own terms: the mutineers would come in unarmed, one at a time, be put in irons, and be taken to England for trial. Otherwise he would fight. Without the map they could not find the treasure, and without a navigator they could not manage the ship; he had finished comforting Silver. The cook flushed with rage. He asked for a hand to get up; the captain refused, and nobody else moved. He had to crawl to the porch and lift himself. Silver spat into the spring, promised to smash the house within an hour, and went downhill. After several failed attempts, his companion with the white flag helped him over the stockade. We watched him leave. It turned out that only Gray had stayed at his post throughout the negotiations.

## posts · Waiting

The captain sent us back to our loopholes, shaming even the squire and doctor. Now we waited for an attack. Spare loaded muskets lay on stacks of firewood, cutlasses in the middle; the fire was put outside to clear the smoke. Trelawney and Gray took the long north wall with its five loopholes, the doctor the door, Hunter and Joyce the sides. The captain and I would load. Heat intensified, and resin melted in the logs. Joyce politely asked whether to fire if he saw anyone. Then he fired, and shots answered from the surrounding woods. Their flashes and sound told us that the main force lay to the north, but Smollett would not leave the other sides unguarded.

## attack · Beside the house

Pirates rushed over the fence. Two were brought down, one fled, and four got inside. Anderson appeared at a loophole. At another, Hunter lost his musket: an attacker wrenched it out by the barrel and struck its owner. A third man attacked the doctor at the door. Smoke and shouting filled the house; our shelter no longer protected us. The captain ordered cutlasses and a rush outside. I ran into sunlight, turned around the house, and faced Anderson. His blade rose; I jumped aside, lost my footing in sand, and rolled downhill. It lasted only moments. Gray, following me, cut down the boatswain, the doctor overcame his attacker, and another pirate was killed at a loophole. The last threw away his cutlass and climbed back out over the fence.

## casualties · After the fight

When we returned indoors, the price of victory was plain. Joyce lay shot through the head, Hunter was unconscious, and the squire supported the wounded Smollett. The captain first asked whether the enemy had fled, then counted those remaining: there were fewer now, and the odds were better than aboard. The doctor attended the wounded. We had held the stockade, but armed men remained beyond the trees, and the captured Hispaniola in the harbour. We still had the map. How to find the treasure, recover the ship, and escape the island remained unanswered.
