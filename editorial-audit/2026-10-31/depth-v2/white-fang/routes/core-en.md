# White Fang — core

## White Fang

On a frozen river two men haul a long box. Hungry wolves follow. One she-wolf comes too close to the fire. Essential and Core reach the arrival of an unfamiliar opponent and stop before the fight is resolved. Deep continues to the last scene—after your consent.

## The box on the sled

Down a frozen river went a sled carrying a long box. Henry walked ahead on snowshoes, Bill behind; between them lay a man who no longer had to break trail or save his breath. They were bringing Lord Alfred's body back to other people. Six dogs laboured in the traces, their breath freezing over their coats. In the brief northern daylight the forest on either bank seemed an unmoving black wall. Then a howl rose behind them, and another answered farther to the left. Henry turned to Bill. Animals were following their trail. They had seen no game for a long time, and both understood what had attracted their pursuers.

## An extra portion

At camp the coffin served as table and seat. The dogs crowded near the fire and scarcely quarrelled. Bill reported something peculiar: he had taken six fish from the bag and handed them out, but One Ear had received none. He had needed a seventh. Henry counted the team: six. Bill knew that too, but he had seen another animal take its fish and vanish into the darkness. Its tracks remained in the snow. At first Henry thought weariness and their dismal load were making his partner imagine things; then another howl interrupted them. Pairs of eyes were lighting up around camp. The dogs shifted nearer the men's legs, and one singed its coat against the fire.

## Five by morning

They had three cartridges left. Bill wished for three hundred, for an end to the cold, for warm Fort McGurry and cards by the fire. Looking at the box, he remarked that the dead rich man was luckier than they were: they themselves might get no more than a few stones over their bodies to keep the dogs away. Henry answered his anxiety with grumbling. In the night Bill got up to add wood and once again counted seven animals beside the fire. By morning there were five. Fatty had disappeared. The men cursed his foolishness, as though that could still help, and harnessed the survivors. There was still no sound of life in the forest except the hungry voices behind them.

## At the edge of firelight

At the next camp Bill caught the uninvited diner beside the fish being handed out. He struck with a stick, but the animal escaped with half a salmon. In the darkness it had looked like an ordinary dog. By morning Frog, the strongest of the team, was gone. Henry no longer explained the disappearances simply as canine stupidity. Bill tethered the four survivors with long sticks: a dog could not reach the thong at its neck, while the wood kept it away from the far end. Even so, One Ear strained forward and chewed his restraint. A large grey she-wolf, her coat carrying an elusive reddish tinge, entered the light. She watched the dogs, and One Ear answered with eager whining.

## Coffee for Bill

Now the men understood how their animals were disappearing: the she-wolf lured them out to the waiting pack. In the morning, despite the sticks, Spanker was gone too. Henry refused Bill coffee, reminding him of his promise to go without if another dog disappeared. Then he offered the cup himself, but Bill stubbornly ate a dry breakfast. One Ear had apparently chewed through another dog's tether when he could not free himself. Soon they found Spanker's stick on the trail, stripped clean of its leather. The coffee joke no longer concealed how rapidly the team was dwindling. Henry tried to keep his spirits up, while Bill increasingly spoke of the wolves getting the men as well.

## Before the rifle rises

In daylight the she-wolf appeared openly on their back trail. She was big but thin, like the pack Bill had examined on an excursion: ribs standing out, bellies drawn against their backbones. Bill called her as though she were a sled-dog; she did not flee. Fire, people, the distribution of fish—all this must have been familiar to her. He decided to spend a cartridge on a certain shot. But as soon as he slipped the rifle from its lashings, she slid into the spruce. She knew that movement too. Bill swore to ambush her. Henry told him not to go far: three shots could not stop an entire pack. That evening the three dogs had to finish their day's journey earlier than usual.

## Three shots

On a bad stretch the sled overturned and jammed between a rock and a tree. The men unharnessed the dogs to free the load, and One Ear ran towards the waiting she-wolf. She retreated before him, briefly allowed their noses to touch, then drew him farther away. The rifle was trapped beneath the sled. By the time they recovered it, wolves were racing out of the woods to cut the dog off. One Ear turned towards the men; the she-wolf instantly followed. Bill could bear no more and plunged through the bushes with the rifle to intercept the chase. Henry heard three quick shots, a dog's yell, a wolf's cry. Then silence. Bill did not return. Henry sat on the sled for a long time, the two remaining dogs trembling at his feet.

## The coffin above the snow

He put his own shoulder into the harness beside them. The next day he raised the coffin onto a platform between trees: at least the wolves would not reach the dead man. The lighter sled moved faster, but they could not go far. The wolves no longer hid. At night they lay around the fire, slept, stretched and inched closer. Henry could not sleep. He could only throw burning wood and replenish the fire; the two dogs pressed against his sides. The she-wolf sat only a few paces away, looking on with hungry expectation. He watched her lick her lips and understood: fire separated his living body from becoming the sort of food he himself had eaten so often.

## A dream of the fort

Sleep approached unnoticed. Henry tied a burning pine-knot to his hand: pain woke him whenever his head drooped. For several hours it worked; then the knot fell off. In his dream he was already at the fort playing cards, with wolves howling helplessly outside the door. Suddenly the door crashed open. He woke to a real attack, teeth on his arm and a slash in his leg. Jumping straight into the fire, he scattered coals by handfuls with his heavy mittens. The pack recoiled, but he could not endure the heat for long either. When he reached the edge, the two dogs were gone. Only he, his axe and the fire remained in camp.

## Gaps in the fire

Henry built the fire into a ring and crouched inside it with his blankets. The wolves settled outside, warming themselves like domestic dogs. The she-wolf lifted her muzzle and howled, and the whole pack followed. By morning the wood was exhausted. Gaps widened between the burning sections, while the wolves prevented him going out for more. Henry sat with his head on his knees. Struggle was yielding to sleep; sooner or later they would come in, and he almost no longer cared. Waking briefly, he saw the she-wolf facing a gap in the ring. She was in no hurry. Then he sank back into a sleep he did not expect to leave.

## Four sleds

Later, men arriving with four sleds shook him awake. The wolves had gone on hearing voices and dogs. Henry gave a confused account of the she-wolf: first she ate the dog-food, then the dogs, then Bill. Asked about Lord Alfred, he said the man was roosting in a tree at the previous camp, dead and in a box. He could explain no more. They laid him on blankets, and his snores rose immediately into the frosty air. Far away the hungry howling could still be heard receding. For Henry the pursuit was over. The she-wolf was running on with the pack to find other food.

## After hunger

While hunger drove the pack, the wolves stayed together. A moose at last gave them abundant meat, and the urgency slackened. The pack began breaking into pairs. Three males remained around the reddish she-wolf: a young leader, old One Eye and an ambitious three-year-old. All three endured her bites but forgave one another nothing. When the youngest tore the old one's ear, the two rivals joined forces and killed him. Then the young leader turned his head to lick his shoulder. One Eye instantly slashed his throat. For the first time the she-wolf answered the victor kindly. They touched noses and began jumping after one another, unexpectedly like puppies.

## Fires beyond the trees

They hunted together and shared their kills. Gradually the she-wolf began investigating roots and crevices in banks, lingering over them. One Eye waited, not understanding her search. One night they approached a human camp. Its smells alarmed him, but she watched the fires for a long time, listening to children, quarrelling dogs and women's voices. She wanted to move closer and enter that familiar bustle. Restlessness nevertheless drew her back into the forest. Nearby, a rabbit One Eye was chasing suddenly soared into the air and danced above the snow. The wolf sprang back: he had never seen prey behave like this. The she-wolf began jumping for it without particular surprise.

## The rabbit in the air

The rabbit hung in a snare attached to a bent sapling. One Eye finally caught it and pulled it down, but the spruce bent towards him. Frightened of a blow from above, he released the rabbit and it shot up again. The she-wolf punished her unlucky helper with a bite; he struck back in alarm and received further punishment. Then he jumped again, caught the rabbit and froze beneath the threatening tree, afraid to move. The she-wolf took the prey and bit off its head. The released sapling straightened, and at last they could eat in peace. Afterwards they visited the other snares. The old experienced hunter followed his mate, learning this strange human method of obtaining food.

## Five blind bundles

A shot near a human camp drove the pair away. The she-wolf moved ever more slowly until she found a dry cave beneath a clay bluff. A narrow entrance opened into a round chamber; after examining walls and roof she lay down facing the light. One Eye still wanted to hunt. Outside, spring was beginning: softening snow, sunlight, faint sounds of water. After an unsuccessful day he returned hungrier than he had left and heard unfamiliar squeaks within. Five blind bundles stirred against the she-wolf's belly. She would not let him near. He lay down at the entrance, then went out for meat again—this time for more than himself.

## The hunters wait

At a fork in the stream he scented a lynx and chose the other branch. There he found a porcupine rolled into an impenetrable ball of quills. One Eye remembered the pain of a quill in his own muzzle and did not attack. He waited, left and caught a ptarmigan. Returning, he spotted the same lynx watching the porcupine. He put his bird down and hid downwind. The hunters lay almost motionless. At last the porcupine began to unroll. The lynx struck beneath its soft belly but could not withdraw her paw before the quilled tail hit it. In fury she attacked again and received quills in her nose. She rolled, rubbed her muzzle in the snow, then fled screaming upstream.

## One Eye’s burden

One Eye left cover only when everything was quiet. The porcupine was mortally wounded but could still defend itself. The wolf waited and picked up blood-soaked snow. Once the quills drooped and the animal finally stopped moving, he carefully turned the body over, took hold and dragged it home with his head aside to keep his feet clear. On the way he remembered the ptarmigan and went back. He ate that himself. Then he resumed his heavy load. In the cave the she-wolf examined the offering and licked his neck before warning him away from the cubs as usual. This time her growl was softer. A provider had appeared at the entrance who still had to establish that he was safe for the family.

## The fourth wall

One of the five cubs was grey, without his mother's reddish tinge. Knowing nothing beyond the cave, he knew warmth, milk, his mother's soft tongue and the bodies of his brothers and sisters. He could tussle with them, seize an ear, turn another over with a paw. With sight, his world acquired walls. Three were hard and hurt his nose; the fourth shone. His father passed through it and returned with meat. His mother would not let the cub approach: she nudged him, pinned him with a paw, sent him back. The light drew him ever more strongly. He did not yet understand that it was an entrance, or that anything existed beyond it except light.

## The empty place at the entrance

Then game disappeared, and his mother's milk failed. Sleep replaced the little battles and cries in the cave. The parents ranged far for food; the former human camp had gone, and snares no longer helped. When the grey cub regained his strength, only one sister remained beside him. She no longer played and scarcely raised her head. Food had arrived too late for her. So he became the only cub. Later still, One Eye ceased to appear. The cub saw only an empty place beside the bright wall. His mother knew more: following a trail, she had found the wolf's remains and the traces of his battle with the lynx. There were kittens in the lynx's lair, and now she avoided that branch of the stream.

## A step into air

One day the cub finally reached the entrance. The wall suddenly expanded to the trees, the mountain behind them and the sky above. The light hurt his eyes, the distance frightened him. The cub crouched at the edge and tried growling at this enormous new world. It did not answer. Fear gradually yielded to curiosity, and he stepped forward—straight into the air. All his life his feet had rested on a level floor. He did not know what falling was. Now he struck his nose, rolled down the slope and yelled until the grass below stopped him. After lying still, he licked clay from his coat and sat up to look around. Nothing so terrible had happened to him before, but his feet soon carried him onward again.

## Seven in the nest

A squirrel was as frightened of him as he was of it and shot up a tree. An impudent little bird, by contrast, pecked his nose when he tried touching it with a paw. Roots, twigs and stones also proved not to be where he expected: they arrived too soon, struck his sides, shifted beneath his feet. Climbing a rotten trunk, he fell through bark and bushes straight into a nest containing seven ptarmigan chicks. They squeaked and moved; first he recoiled, then pinned one beneath his paw, took it into his mouth and discovered the familiar taste of meat. He ate the entire brood. Emerging from the bush, he encountered the beating wings of the returning mother.

## The wing between his teeth

Fright gave way to excitement. The cub seized the bird's wing; she dragged him along the ground and struck with the other. He growled and pulled his prey into the open until she began pecking his sore nose. Then the chicks' conqueror ignominiously released the larger bird and fled. Resting beside some bushes, he suddenly darted beneath them—a hawk swept past very close. The ptarmigan, lingering beside her ruined nest, did not notice the second swoop. The cub saw the talons catch her and carry her upward. Only a moment before he had wanted to eat her himself; now he lay in shelter he might not have reached in time.

## An unreliable path

The stream looked like a convenient level path. The cub stepped onto it, went under and choked. Surfacing, he worked all four legs. The nearer bank was behind him, and he swam towards the farther one until the current took him. In the rapids the water spun him and dashed him against stones, drawing a fresh yell with every blow. Below, a quiet eddy carried him onto gravel. He crawled out, tired and wet. He wanted his mother badly. On his way home he found a tiny young weasel. He turned it over with his paw—and immediately received a bite in the neck. Its mother carried her little one into the bushes, then came back for him.

## His mother’s jaws

The little yellow body proved more terrible than many larger objects that day. The weasel fastened onto his throat, and he could not shake her loose. Growling gave way to crying; all he could do was struggle beneath her teeth. The she-wolf arrived in time. The weasel now sprang at her throat, caught her jaw instead, but the mother tossed her up with a sharp movement and caught her in her mouth. Afterwards she licked her son's wounds for a long time. Together they ate the dead weasel and returned to the cave. For a while the whole vast world narrowed again to his mother's side. He had had enough adventure for two days; then he went out once more and found the way back himself.

## Meat from another lair

The cub accompanied his mother farther and farther and tried hunting alone. His prey escaped: the squirrel reached its tree, and the birds flew away. When hunger returned, play became a serious search for food. He sniffed out mice, studied the squirrel's movements and once did not even hide from the hawk: he sat openly, as if challenging it to come down. There was meat in the sky too. But the hawk declined the challenge. His mother came home thinner and thinner, and there was nothing to eat in the cave. At last she brought unusual prey—a partly grown lynx kitten, smaller than the cub. He ate it all. The she-wolf had already satisfied her own hunger on the remaining kittens from the same lair.

## Under the low roof

His full-bellied sleep ended with his mother's growling. The lynx whose litter had been destroyed stood in the bright entrance. The she-wolf shielded her son and thrust him back. The low roof prevented the intruder leaping; she had to crawl in, and the she-wolf sprang upon her. Two bodies thrashed in the confined space, teeth and claws flashing. The cub seized a hind-leg, dragging on it with his weight. The fighters crushed him beneath them and broke his hold. When the animals separated briefly, one blow from the lynx laid his shoulder open to the bone and flung him against the wall. He had time to cry himself out while the battle continued. At the end he was clinging to the leg again.

## A week in the cave

The lynx lay dead. The she-wolf first licked her son, then lay beside the body and scarcely moved for a day and a night. For a week she went out only for water. In the cave they ate the lynx while their wounds slowly healed. The cub still limped, but when he emerged he carried himself with greater confidence. He had been attacker and prey, had seen large animals kill and die. Yet a full belly, sunshine and rest remained wonderful. So long as his mother found meat and returned, life was not made of terror alone. He ran beside her along the familiar stream again—and one day, going down to drink, encountered five motionless creatures unlike any he had ever seen.

## Kiche

On the familiar path to the water, five men sat on their haunches, looking silently at the cub. He did not run: the new sight seemed to pin him to the ground. One approached and extended a hand. The cub bared his teeth; the man laughed at the white fangs. When the hand descended farther, he bit it and received a blow on the head. His cries brought his mother. She burst in with such fury that the men retreated. Now everything should be right: she would protect him as before. But one man called out the name Kiche. The she-wolf suddenly crouched, whined and wagged her tail. The cub watched strange hands touch his fearless mother while she pressed submissively towards them.

## White Fang

The man was Grey Beaver. Kiche had belonged to his dead brother and had fled during a famine. Her mother had been a dog, her father a wolf; there was still more wolf in the grey cub. Grey Beaver named him White Fang and claimed him as his own. He tethered the mother to a pine with a stick. When the rest of the camp arrived, the dogs rushed at the unfamiliar pair. White Fang went under their bodies, biting legs and bellies. The people drove the attackers off with clubs and stones. The very hands that struck now saved him. Creatures on two legs could make even dead wood fly and inflict pain. Beside them he felt tiny.

## Walls that grow

Following the tethered Kiche, he reached the Mackenzie. Here they set out canoes, hung fish, raised poles and covered them in skins and cloth. Huge dwellings grew before his eyes. At first White Fang feared even their moving walls; then he crept up, tugged one with his teeth and discovered an entertaining game. An indignant cry inside sent him running. Another puppy, older and larger, approached importantly. This was Lip-lip. White Fang was still ready to be friendly but received a sudden bite on the shoulder the lynx had wounded. He attacked in return and was bitten repeatedly before fleeing to his mother. So their acquaintance began.

## A burnt tongue

Beside Grey Beaver he saw another marvel: a bright moving creature grew from moss and sticks. Curiosity drew him close. White Fang touched the fire with nose and tongue, recoiled and yelled. Licking his nose hurt still more—his tongue was burnt too. The people laughed. Only Kiche strained furiously against her tether to help. He fled to her from the pain and general laughter. That evening the noise of camp kept him awake: too many voices, dogs, movements. He wanted the cave's quiet. But the people could grow biting, sun-coloured life out of wood and command dogs and his mother. To him these were actual gods, close, frightening and solid.

## The route between dwellings

White Fang learned his way around camp, but Lip-lip prevented him playing with the other puppies. Whenever he left his mother, his tormentor appeared. Most encounters ended in flight. Once White Fang retreated particularly slowly, always staying one leap ahead. Between the dwellings he led his pursuer straight to the tethered Kiche. Her teeth brought Lip-lip down and mauled him thoroughly. When he escaped and began wailing, White Fang seized his hind-leg himself and chased him to his own dwelling, from which stones flew. This was his first major success. Another day the persecution could begin again, but now he knew he could answer it with cunning.

## After the canoe

Kiche was finally released. White Fang led her towards the forest: running ahead, returning to lick her muzzle, inviting her with play. The stream, cave and familiar quiet remained out there. She stopped at the edge and looked back. Then she turned towards camp. Her son sat for a while beneath a birch, whimpering, and followed. An invisible bond held his mother more securely than a stick. But this closeness ended too. Grey Beaver owed Three Eagles a debt and gave him Kiche along with cloth, a skin and cartridges. White Fang saw her being put into a canoe. Struck when he tried to climb after her, he jumped into the water and swam after his departing mother, ignoring orders to return.

## In the canoe’s bottom

Grey Beaver pursued in another canoe, lifted him by the scruff and beat him while holding him in the air. Flung into the bottom and kicked, White Fang bit his master's foot. Then the paddle was used. After the further beating he lay without resistance. On shore Lip-lip rushed at him; Grey Beaver kicked that dog away too. The master could hurt him but permitted no other creature to do so. White Fang limped after him. That night he mourned Kiche, was beaten again and learned to whimper more softly. He stayed to wait: people went away and returned—one day his mother would return as well. Sometimes the master tossed him meat and defended him from the dogs. He gave no caresses.

## Behind the master

Lip-lip drew the other puppies after him. White Fang could already defeat many alone, but the start of a fight immediately brought them all. He learned to attack without warning, slash and spring away before the others arrived. If knocked aside, he had to land on his feet at any cost. So he obtained food, defended himself and ambushed his enemies in turn. Once he killed a young dog near the forest. The people demanded vengeance, but Grey Beaver put him inside his dwelling and refused to surrender him. White Fang heard the angry voices outside. Behind the master there was protection; beyond the entrance were canine teeth and the stones of people tired of his thefts and fights.

## The camp is gone

In autumn the camp prepared to leave for hunting. While covers came down and canoes were loaded, White Fang hid in the forest, breaking his trail in the stream. He heard Grey Beaver, his wife Kloo-kooch and their son Mit-sah calling, but did not emerge. When the voices ceased, freedom at last belonged to him. With darkness it became cold and empty. There was no warm dwelling, no piece of fish, not even the familiar noise of enemies. A tree-shadow suddenly thrown by the moon frightened him. A frozen trunk cracked—he fled towards the people. But only rubbish and smells remained where the camp had stood. Sitting on the site of Grey Beaver's dwelling, White Fang howled for the first time in his life.

## The broken piece

In the morning he ran down the Mackenzie to find them. Through day and night he climbed above bluffs, swam streams and broke through thin ice. He searched only his own bank: the other simply never occurred to him. By the following evening his feet were torn, snow blinded him, hunger and exhaustion broke his run. He did not know Grey Beaver had intended to camp across the river. But Kloo-kooch spotted a moose here, Mit-sah turned the canoe, and a lucky shot kept the family on this bank. White Fang found a fresh trail, heard voices and emerged beside the fire. He crawled towards the master, expecting blows. The raised hand broke a lump of tallow. He received meat, and the dogs were kept from taking it. He slept at Grey Beaver's feet.

## Lip-lip’s place

In winter White Fang was harnessed to Mit-sah's small sled. The puppies ran in a fan on ropes of different lengths. Lip-lip went first, on the longest: he had to flee the others continually. If he tried to turn, Mit-sah whipped him, yet fed him conspicuously and generously, sometimes only pretending to do so. The former bully became the object of general hatred. White Fang worked hard and obeyed. There was no peace with the dogs: he would seize an unfinished portion from one that lingered and answer any objection with a swift bite. He never learned to play with them. His strength went into work, fighting and guarding his master's belongings.

## Other puppies

In spring he returned to his home camp much larger, though still rangy. Old Baseek approached his portion of moose meat. First White Fang shrank by long habit, but when the old dog began eating he struck without warning and drove him off. Later he suddenly encountered Kiche. Recognising her, he approached joyfully and received a wound on his cheek. She had new puppies beside her. One crawled towards him; the mother struck again. White Fang stood bewildered, then left. She was defending her present litter from a strange adult animal; their former closeness did not return.

## No fish

In his third year came a famine unlike any he had known. Fish failed in summer; in winter the caribou did not come. In camp they ate leather, harness and dogs; the dogs ate one another. White Fang went into the forest. He waited hours for a squirrel, dug out mice, stole rare catches from snares—once from Grey Beaver, who could scarcely remain on his feet. Encountering a starving young wolf, he killed and ate him. Once he himself had to flee a pack; having fed on a lynx for two days saved him. He reached the old cave too, but Kiche greeted him with hostility again. He settled farther on in the abandoned lynx lair. The childhood home was no longer something he could recover.

## The meeting beyond the rock

Towards the end of the famine Lip-lip emerged round a rock ahead of him. He too had wandered into the woods but was thin and miserable; White Fang had lately fed well. The sight of his old tormentor raised his hair as before. Only now there was neither a chase nor human protection. White Fang struck shoulder to shoulder, rolled his enemy over and fastened onto his throat. Waiting until it was over, he went on. Soon beside the Mackenzie he discovered his own camp in a new place. It smelled of fish and sounded well-fed. He walked straight to the familiar dwelling. Grey Beaver was absent, but Kloo-kooch welcomed him and gave him a whole fresh fish. White Fang lay down to await his master.

## First in the team

Later White Fang himself went first in the team, at the end of the long rope. All day the dogs he had once dominated raced behind him. Stopping without orders meant exposing himself to their teeth; the whip prevented him turning. In camp he repaid the day's humiliation, but now attacked a united pack. He spared no strange dogs on the long journeys. When he was nearly five, Grey Beaver took him over the mountains to the Yukon. In the summer of 1898 Fort Yukon was crowded with gold-seekers travelling towards the Klondike. His master traded profitably in furs, mittens and footwear; White Fang first saw such numbers of unfamiliar people, large log buildings and steamboats delivering still more people and dogs.

## Another steamer

The steamboat dogs looked strange: legs too long or too short, unfamiliar coats, clumsy fury. Often they rushed at the wolfish creature themselves. White Fang slipped aside, overthrew an attacker and slashed its throat; the local pack completed the killing. Then the owners recovered from their surprise and scattered the pack. White Fang was already standing apart. One newcomer shot six dogs in revenge for his setter. This too could be watched from a distance. With the next steamer it began again. White Fang had no regular work, and fighting was something he understood. Local old-timers came to see the newcomers pay dearly for carelessness. One spectator particularly admired the work of his teeth.

## The black bottle

He was called Beauty Smith—a name the others used mockingly. He cooked at the fort and cowered before men, but the sight of a dying dog could make him jump with delight. He wanted White Fang. The animal backed away from his approaches and bared his teeth. Grey Beaver refused at first: he had money enough and had never owned a better sled-dog. Then Smith began arriving with whisky. The trading profits dwindled while the craving for drink increased. When there was nothing left to buy it with, they discussed the dog again. This time the price was in bottles. One evening Grey Beaver himself tied a thong around White Fang's neck and sat with a bottle beside him until the buyer appeared.

## Back to the master

White Fang heard the familiar approaching footsteps and tried to draw the thong quietly out of the fingers. His master's grip tightened. Smith extended a hand, received a warning snap and returned with a club. Grey Beaver handed him the tether. White Fang resisted, then rushed at the stranger and was struck down. He had to follow. In the night, however, he cut through the thong with a few movements of his teeth and returned to Grey Beaver. For him the former master still existed, and he belonged to him. In the morning he was handed back to Smith. Tied up, he could neither escape the beating nor strike back. Now a stick restrained him, too close to his neck to grasp easily.

## A chain instead of wood

For hours he arched his neck and gnawed the wood until he was free. With the remnant dangling, he returned to Grey Beaver, submitted to another tether and was surrendered a third time. His old master watched him being beaten and did not intervene. Afterwards White Fang could not get up for a long time, then stumbled after Smith, reeling and scarcely seeing the way. At the fort they chained him. His teeth could not cut the links; he could not tear their fastening from the timber. A few days later Grey Beaver, sober and bankrupt, left for the Mackenzie. White Fang remained. There was nobody left to return to; before him stood a man whose mere appearance now brought rage.

## An opponent beyond the door

Smith kept him chained in a pen, teased him, prodded him, laughed and pointed. Mockery drove White Fang frantic. He hated his owner, the people beyond the slats, their dogs, the chain itself. Then one day it was removed and a huge mastiff was pushed inside. At last there was a living body within reach. He struck and sprang away, never allowing the heavy opponent to close its jaws. The mastiff's owner removed it when Smith beat the victor back with his club. Outside they settled bets. Smith received money, while White Fang received the only occupation for which the door now opened. Beyond it, again and again, another opponent appeared.

## The autumn steamer

More contests followed. One day three dogs were sent into the pen in succession; another day it was a newly captured adult wolf. Then they set two dogs against White Fang together. He killed both, but almost died himself. In autumn, with the first snow and drifting ice on the river, Smith took him by steamer to Dawson. His cage stood on deck; passengers watched, poked sticks and laughed. In town they paid gold dust to see the Fighting Wolf. Whenever he lay down, a sharp stick brought him to his feet again: the spectators must get a performance for their money.

## The short dog

At last, fighting stopped altogether: no animal considered a worthy opponent could be found. Until spring White Fang remained an exhibit behind bars. Then the gambler Tim Keenan arrived with Cherokee, the first bulldog in the Klondike. For a week the coming match was the subject of conversation. White Fang knew nothing of it. When the chain was removed once more, a short unfamiliar dog stood before him.
