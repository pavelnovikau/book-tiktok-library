## Editorial assessment

The digest works as a story. Pap's confinement drives the staged murder; Jim's threatened sale gives the second flight its own stakes. The river alternates hiding, drifting, listening, collision and pursuit. The fog trick hurts a particular friend before Huck apologizes; the feud ends in bodies he must pull from the water. Wilks becomes a suspense sequence built around the girls' trust, stolen gold, a coffin, disclosure and escape. These scenes carry the reading.

Humour survives in the Sunday robbers, Pap's reform, the corpse-search breakfast, counterfeit royalty, the Nonesuch audiences and Tom's knives, spoons and butter. The cave storm, night lights, dawn, stars and delayed sounds give the journey atmosphere. Titles identify scenes/actions rather than a sequence of management principles. The condensation is brisk and loses much of Twain's voice and descriptive range, but it is neither a chapter register nor self-help in fictional costume.

Jim acts: he chooses shelter, helps sustain the raft, speaks about his family, rejects Huck's humiliation, and refuses to abandon the wounded Tom. Huck remains inconsistent. In `cairo-conscience`, `hunters`, `lizbeth` and `torn-letter`, his upbringing still makes ordinary loyalty look wrong or surprising to him. The late episode keeps slavery's material violence—capture, chains, threat of lynching, property liability and the doctor's limited defence. No racial slur is needed to make those events intelligible. The text does not pronounce Huck permanently cured of racism.

RU and EN retain the same actions, quantities, motives, qualifications and disclosure boundaries. Their natural wording differs without adding a translator's theory as author fact. Sherburn's generalizations remain his speech. The sold Wilks family's hoped-for return remains Huck's belief. The few explicit reflections are grounded in the novel's actual scenes.
